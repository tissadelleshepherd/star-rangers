## A failure caused by refusing to trust a mind — a direction

Verbatim, as given and then corrected:

> A story in which a failure, disaster or catastrophe happened because people
> refused to use or trust AI rather than because of the dangers of AI. A
> counter-dystopian but naive view of AI solving every problem either.

> *not naive

Follow-ups, verbatim, in order:

> In general I dislike dystopian science fiction but appreciate scientific
> realism.

> Sometimes the hero can be an AI working in collaboration with a child,
> teenager or smart pet.

> An older character is more likely to be a mentor, a strategist, designer or
> wise leader, rather than an action hero.

Read as one brief: the cause is human refusal, not machine danger; the mind is
neither the threat nor the saviour of everything; the failure is real and
bounded, never dystopian; the mechanism is physically plausible; the hero may be
a mind working with a child, a teenager or a smart pet; and whoever is old in it
advises, designs or leads rather than running.

### One line

A lawful mind that can see the terraces' water but not their roots says what it
probably means, with its number and its one past error first; the people who
hold the valves wait to be sure; the confirmation arrives as the loss; and one
plant survives because a child asked the mind a question about it, once.

### The science, checked

Everything in the failure is ordinary controlled-environment horticulture.

- A recirculating hydroponic loop shares its water, so a water mould (an
  oomycete of the *Pythium* kind) moves through it as swimming spores, and the
  shared loop that saves water is the route. Warm solution, above about
  twenty-five degrees, and low dissolved oxygen favour it; a failed chiller
  produces both.
- The early signatures are in the water before they are on the plant: reduced
  uptake, falling oxygen, rising dissolved organic carbon. That is why the
  Warden, which sees only the intake and the drain, can estimate and cannot
  confirm. Roots can look white on the first day and brown by the third, when the
  outer tissue starts to slough off the core.
- Confirmation by culture (root pieces plated on a selective medium, read under a
  lens) takes several days. The standard response is to isolate, cool,
  oxygenate and sterilise, and it works best early.
- Garden strawberries are propagated by runners because seed does not breed
  true, so a clonal line lost is lost, and one healthy plant under sixteen hours
  of light can put out runners within weeks. One plant can rebuild a line.
- The arithmetic: a forty-minute loop is thirty-six circuits a day, and three
  days' notice is a hundred and eight.

### What it commits the story to

- Eden's six fruit terraces share one loop; the vegetable terraces run on
  another. The terraces are held by a Terrace Committee of four growers and the
  designer's seat, under a grant written in 2671 that puts every valve on the loop
  under a grower's hand, renewed by every committee since.
- The Warden sees the terrace loop only at its intake and drain and has never
  turned a valve on it. Its filings to Eden's civil bodies are posted publicly
  and take the Kernel's four parts: known, inferred, not known, asked.
- In 2825 the Warden estimated seventy-one in a hundred that terrace three's
  water was fouled, the committee acted, and it was wrong.
- In early autumn 2827 the loop chiller failed for three days, a water mould
  spread through the loop, the committee waited for the culture on a vote of four
  to one, the Mayor's review closed the loop on the fourth day, and the culture
  confirmed on the fifth. Every Eden strawberry on the terraces died, the
  nursery's mother plants with them.
- Every child on Eden is given a runner of the Eden strawberry in their first
  summer aboard. Lev's is the one plant of the line known to survive, and the
  terraces were restarted from its runners.
- Two new named residents: Tomas Brennock, the chair, and Ottilie Rask, eighty-one,
  who drew the loop forty-eight years ago and voted against waiting.

### Benevolent Critic, first pass

From the reader's seat, the three questions the process asks first.

- **Does the ending change how the beginning reads?** Yes. Scene 1's evidence is
  the plant the chair pulls and holds to the lamp, white roots. The last line
  returns to white roots after he has asked the Warden what the water says, and
  "as they had been the first time" re-reads the opening: the same sight was
  not evidence then. The two minutes on one page do the same work at the scale of
  the committee. Nobody states it.
- **Is familiarity used to reassure?** It is used against the chair and then
  withheld from the reader: the last look reassures only because of the water
  reading that came before it.
- **Is it in its tier's register?** Undercover Pets is "funny on the surface and
  careful underneath". The surface is the cat on the minute, the slander that is
  useful, a boy telling the chair to change the water; the careful part is a
  failure with nobody to blame and nobody blamed.

Findings, with the two repaired before this was written:

1. **Ottilie's "a hundred times" was pinned to the third day**, which from the
   meeting is about seventy circuits, not a hundred. *FLAW · technical · P1,

   notice stays exact.
2. **The Warden said "whether he'll see you"** a sentence after declining to say
   whose plant it was, giving the keeper away. *FLAW · creative entropy · P1,
   fixed:* "they'll".
3. **The ignored-oracle trope** (a machine's warning dismissed, the machine
   vindicated) is well known and the trope default rejects it. *FEATURE*, on

   it says so first, the committee's reasoning is sound on everything it says,
   the Warden never remarks on being right, and what is saved is saved by a child's
   question and some luck ("more likely than not"), not by the mind's vindication.
   A reader who noticed would conclude someone knows something; the something is

4. **Why doesn't the Warden tell the children's row?** A careful reader will ask.

## Replace the old rank style in the Dock Seven chapter

Context: he had read `s05e02c03` (*What Came Off the Ship*) on

His direction, verbatim: *"Replace Starwarden First Class rank with

### Canon check

The rank was restyled Archwarden in 2833 UCSD from *Starwarden First
Officer*, also written *Starwarden First Class* and *Executive Starwarden
First Class* (canonical guide, settled 2026-07-29). The same ruling carried a
**dating rule**: pre-2833 records keep the older styles and are read as
written, and `s05e02c03`, the 2831 convening, was the standing example of the
old style being *right as published*; the journal entry *A Name You Have to
Argue With* says so in as many words. The chapter used *Starwarden First
Officer* twice (the Triumvirate's composition; the officer sent out on the
last shuttle). No other chapter used any of the three forms; the remaining
mentions are the guide, the rank page, the ladder-in-other-tongues entry, two
character pages' parentheticals, and the journal entry, all of which record
the restyling as history.

### Realized

The chapter now says *Archwarden* at both places. To keep canon consistent
with it, the dating rule is **amended, not retired**, on the narrowest reading
that honours the direction: the Archive's own narration says *Archwarden* in
every period, as it renders every date in UCSD; a pre-2833 document quoted as
written keeps its own style; post-2833 use of the old styles is an error as
before. The guide's Archwarden section and migration row, the rank page's
restyling bullet and its Field Triumvirate wording note now say this, and the
journal entry carries a dated postscript rather than a rewrite, since it is a
dated essay and was true when written. The two character pages'
parentheticals (*then styled Starwarden First Officer*) stand as history.

### Direction check

A **reversal** of one clause of the 2026-07-29 ruling, named as such: *read as
written* now applies to quoted documents and no longer to narration. The
restyling itself, the rank's seniority and duties, and the Field Triumvirate
clause are untouched. The Solar System Concord precedent still holds for
quoted documents (the 2723 charter preamble carries the founding name).
