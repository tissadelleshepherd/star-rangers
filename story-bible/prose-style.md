# The house style of the narrative prose

Derived 13 August 2026 by reading the corpus rather than by prescribing to it.
**Nothing here is a new rule.** Every item is a pattern already running through
the 70,000 words in `src/seasons/`, described so that a new chapter can be
written in the same hand — and so that a drafter who has not read all of it can
be told what to match.

Where a claim is measurable it is measured. Where it is a judgement it says so.

**Scope: the chapters.** Everything below describes `src/seasons/` — the
scene wrappers, the POV blocks, the withholding between viewpoints. The
Codex is not bound by it (Dermot's ruling, 12 September 2026): a codex
author writes in whatever mode a document of that kind takes — first person,
an apparently omniscient narrator, a ballad, a transcript, a school reader —
and is held only to being valid for its author.

---

## The one-sentence version

**A calm institutional voice, very close inside one head at a time, that
withholds its own best line and lets the reader arrive at it.**

---

## 1. The register is *filed*, not *narrated*

This is the load-bearing choice and everything else follows from it. The prose
reads like a record kept by someone competent and unhurried: costs are named,
dates are exact, nobody exclaims. It is the same register the in-world Survey
Archive uses, applied to the story itself.

This is also **the horror limiter**. The universe carries cyber-revenants,
predatory entities, dead children. What keeps it from tipping is that the record
files those calmly and leaves dread to implication. A scene that starts
*performing* its darkness has left the style.

> She filed her formal testimony two days later, citing response-time figures
> the hearing had already heard nine times from nine other delegates. Nobody
> outside the chamber ever asked her about it.

Note the movement: the fact, the detail that costs, then the silence after it.
The prose does not tell you that this is sad.

## 2. Negation carries characterisation

**175 instances of "does not" / "did not"** in 70,000 words. This is not a tic;
it is the primary tool for saying who someone is.

> She does not volunteer assessments she cannot support. She does not perform
> confidence she does not have.

People are defined by what they decline. It suits a cast of professionals, it
avoids adjective-stacking, and it leaves the positive claim for the reader to
make. **Use it, but notice when three land in a row** — the corpus does it
deliberately and rhythmically, not by default.

## 3. The explanatory hinge: *which is*, *not because … but because*

**84 uses of "which is/was"** — the clause that turns an observation into a
judgement without an authorial aside.

> …which is why her crews' loyalty has always run a little ahead of her
> reputation.

And the corpus's favourite corrective shape, used sparingly (5 times) and always
at a load-bearing moment:

> …**not because** the habitats couldn't police themselves, **but because**
> nothing built to police one habitat could ever be expected to notice a pattern
> that only existed across all five.

## 4. Rhythm: short, short, then one long one that does the work

Median paragraph in a sampled chapter: **12 words. Longest: 120.** That spread is
the style. Short declaratives establish; then a single long sentence, usually
hinged on a dash or a *which*, carries the actual thought.

**611 em-dashes** across the corpus — roughly one every 115 words. The dash is
the house punctuation for the qualification that matters more than the clause it
interrupts.

## 5. Very close third, one head at a time

`:::pov` blocks are hard boundaries. Inside one, the prose knows only what that
person knows, and it thinks in their idiom — free indirect style, thought
reported without quotation marks or attribution.

> He had known Krast. Not closely — she had been three ranks above him for most
> of his service…

The corpus never head-hops inside a block, and it never tells you a thing the POV
character has not noticed. When two people understand a scene differently, that
is done with two POV blocks, not with an omniscient summary.

## 6. Dialogue argues on the merits

**207 dialogue-opening lines, and only 26 questions in the whole corpus.** People
here state positions rather than fishing. Antagonists are sincere and often
right about something — the standing principle is that opponents are misguided
rather than villainous, and the dialogue reflects it.

> "Strike it and you get a corps that has to stand outside a burning habitat and
> ask permission to enter."

Nobody monologues, nobody quips, and nobody says the theme aloud. **Speech is
where positions collide; the meaning stays in the narration.**

## 7. Institutional detail is the texture

Ranks, filings, forms, weeks-into-a-hearing, item numbers, initialled flags. The
setting is made of paperwork and the prose loves it — because a bureaucracy that
is rendered precisely is how you make an institution feel real enough to fail.
Specific numbers, always: *eleven weeks*, *forty seconds*, *item sixteen*.

*(One caution the continuity audit turned up: **eleven** is this record's
favourite number, across years, weeks, days, crates, seconds and generations. If
a new span wants to be eleven, check that it is not habit.)*

## 8. The best line is withheld

The most consistent habit in the corpus, and the hardest to imitate: **the story
does not point at its own resonance.**

The intake note about Wender's origin states the rule outright — a child saved by
machines that had to judge who could be reached in time grows up to specialise in
telling executable orders from ones that only look executable, and the direction
is: *"Let a reader find that. Do not let a character point at it."*

Similarly: *"None of this should be said aloud by anyone. It is structure, not
dialogue."*

**If a character explains the theme, the scene is wrong.**

## 9. Endings: a plain-words aphorism, or a door left open

Chapters end on one of two shapes — never on a summary.

A flat sentence carrying the weight:

> She wrote: *That should have been harder. Find out why it wasn't.*

Or a small forward-leaning observation that declines to resolve:

> A High Captain who let a good officer keep her reasons, Wender had found,
> generally got the officer's judgment intact when it finally mattered…

## 10. What the prose does not do

- **No metaphor-reaching.** Characters who use imprecise language get corrected
  in-world; the narration holds itself to the same standard.
- **No gore, no body horror, no peril-thriller beats.** Unsettling, never horror.
- **No stereotyped shorthand.** Strength and vulnerability are orthogonal in this
  cast; competence is never signalled by coldness, nor warmth by softness.
- **No diagnosing.** The record describes how a person *is* and declines to label
  it — true of plural minds, of Falkorian's refusal, of Elvira's classification.
- **No exclamation marks**, effectively. The register does not raise its voice.

## 11. The Union's Irish in Union mouths

*This is a direction, not a finding from the corpus.* Dermot, 2026-09-24, verbatim:
*local dialogue or POV in Celtic Union characters may use hints of 279th century
Celtic Union gaelic*. *279th* is read as *29th*, the century of the Union's Irish
ruled the same day (`intake-2026-09-24.md`).

- **Who.** Characters of the Celtic Union, in their own dialogue and in their own
  POV blocks. Documents a Union hand wrote count too. The Archive's narration does
  not use it, and neither do characters from elsewhere. The one exception is an
  off-worlder repeating a word they have learned, and the prose shows that it was
  learned.
- **How much.** Hints: a word or a short phrase at a time, such as a greeting, an
  endearment, a name for a thing, or a Union institution. It never becomes a
  sentence the reader must translate to follow the scene. The context teaches the
  word, or the English beside it carries the meaning. There are no footnotes, no
  bracketed translations, and nothing is italicised as foreign on the Union's own
  ground.
- **Which Irish.** The Union's own 29th-century Irish, held to neither today's
  standard nor the old forms — a language partly lost on the crossing and rebuilt
  from the texts and from linguistic principles (Dermot, 2026-10-05), so it is
  regular where the old was irregular, and a Union speaker may know that. Where the glossary gives a term's Union form, a
  Union speaker uses that form: a Union Ranger may call the corps *na Maoir
  Réalta*, and a Union child says *na Daoine Maithe*. *Ilchruinne* and *fian*
  never appear.
- **Where it shows.** It shows most at home and under strain, and least when the
  character is working with off-worlders. It is the sound of a person at home, not
  a costume. It is used sparingly, because a word that comes back on every page
  stops carrying anything.
- **What it is never.** It is never stage-Irish. There is no comic accent, no
  phonetic spelling of English, and no *begorrah* register. That would be the
  stereotyped shorthand §10 already refuses. The hint marks a living language
  spoken by people for whom it is ordinary.
- **Tier.** It is allowed at every rung. On the children's rung the context
  glosses the word in the same breath.

## For a drafter, in order of importance

1. Write it as a **record**, calmly, and let the awful parts be awful quietly.
2. Stay **inside one head**, and only that head.
3. Say who someone is by **what they decline**.
4. Give the theme to the **reader**, never to a character.
5. **Short, short, long.** End on a plain sentence.

## The tonal target this serves

> Enigmatic and haunting, with beauty, mystery, hope and serenity woven through.

**Hope and serenity are load-bearing.** Haunting on its own drifts toward the
horror line this style exists to hold — and the calm of the register is what
makes the difference between a record that is haunting and one that is merely
grim. *(Exception: **Undercover Pets** is cute, cool and clever, and the contrast
is deliberate.)*
