The fleets that crossed to other stars before there was a quick way to do it named themselves, deliberately, for the smallest boats their ancestors had ever trusted to open water — the Irish *currach*, the Norse *knarr*. The choice was not only sentiment. The odds were about the same.

## The Only Road Out

Before the first [fold routes and harmonic corridors](/star-rangers/lore/ftl-mechanics/) turned the crossing into a matter of weeks — the Eden Passage among the earliest, roughly 2712 UCSD — reaching another star meant a **generation ark**: a sealed sublight hull on a transit measured in decades, carrying a company that expected to be born, to live, and to die aboard, handing the voyage on to children who would see neither the world it left nor, for most of them, the world it sought. No rescue could be sent after one. No signal it sent home could be answered inside a human lifetime. An ark was a one-way wager placed by people who would not live to learn whether they had won it.

## The Odds Were a Seafarer's Odds

The survival of such a crossing was, in the plainest comparison the record reaches for, the survival of the early open-boat ocean voyages the arks were named after — the Norse passages to Iceland, Greenland, and the coasts past them, and the Irish monastic *peregrini* who put out past sight of land in hide boats and trusted the rest to providence and seamanship. Which is to say: perilous, heavy with loss, and a genuine crossing all the same. Ships were lost, and lost often, and their loss was expected and mourned rather than taken as proof the thing could not be done — because other ships arrived, and the routes were made and remade by the ones that did.

What decided a given crossing was not the drive; every ark ran the same slow physics. It was provisioning and the durability of a company across generations. A cohesive, self-sufficient community with a reason to hold the voyage's discipline down the decades carried a real chance across; a desperate or under-found departure, fleeing more than it was sailing toward, most often did not. The arks that survived were, more often than not, the ones that had least needed to run.

## A Rate the Record Cannot Give

No clean figure survives, and the reason is structural rather than a hole in the archive. From the Solar System's side a surviving ark and a lost one were indistinguishable: both went silent for generations, both sent nothing back. The [Imperium](/star-rangers/lore/the-imperium/) accordingly closed the file on every ark that slipped its patrols as a *presumed transit loss* — and, those departures being unauthorised and uncounted, no one holds a reliable tally of how many even left. The confirmed survival rate of the era is therefore not a measurement of anything. It is an artefact of a record that entered every crossing, arrived or vanished, in the same column: lost.

## The One That Came Back Into the Record

The measure of how badly that record undercounts is the [Celtic Union of Planets](/star-rangers/lore/celtic-union-of-planets/). The Currach Fleet's six arks all crossed and all founded worlds — a complete six-for-six the founders earned with precisely the preparation and cohesion the era rewarded, and which Solar System record nonetheless carried as six presumed losses for close to three centuries, until Star Rangers survey re-contacted the Union within the last generation. It is the only pre-fold interstellar venture whose fate the record now knows, which makes it at once the era's standout success and the gauge of how much of the era is simply unrecoverable: a whole age of departures went out into silence, and one fleet, found by accident, is what came back into the books.
