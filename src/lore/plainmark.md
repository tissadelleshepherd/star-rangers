There is a kind of reading that has to work on the worst day of somebody's life, in a language they never learned. The Compact's answer to that is the plainmark, and the first thing to know about it is that it is not writing.

## What It Is

**Compact Common Marking** — nobody calls it that; everybody calls it the **plainmark** — is the [Orbital Habitats Compact's](/star-rangers/lore/orbital-habitats-compact/) standard for marking infrastructure without script: hatches, corridor junctions, shafts, berths, valves, crates. A plainmark contains no letters, no numerals, and no characters of any script, human or otherwise. There is nothing in it to spell, nothing to pronounce, and nothing to mistranslate. It is read the way a road marking or a component's colour bands are read: figure by figure, in place, in a glance.

A spacer coming off a foreign hull at [Eden](/star-rangers/lore/eden-space-habitat/) with no word of any Compact language can follow the ochre rings to sealed air before she has met a single person, and the Compact regards that sentence as the standard's entire justification.

## The Grammar

A plainmark is a stencilled panel of one to four horizontal colour bands, stacked, read top to bottom. Each band pairs one ground colour with one class of figure, and the pairing is fixed:

- **Black ground, ochre lozenges — hazard.** One lozenge is a controlled hazard; two is an immediate one; three means the space beyond is held against entry. Three is rare enough that crews remember every place they have seen it.
- **Slate-blue ground, bone-white chevrons — the way.** Chevrons point: along a corridor, up or down a shaft. Their count is the number of junctions to pass before the mark's subject, and the standard never counts past four — beyond that, the plainmark does not say *far*; it hands you on, panel to panel, and each panel tells the truth about its own stretch.
- **Bone-white ground, black strokes and blocks — count.** A short stroke counts one; a wide filled block counts five; blocks paint first, left to right. Fourteen is two blocks and four strokes. Deck, gate, berth — whichever the panel's placement makes it. There is no zero and no fraction: the plainmark cannot say *nothing is here*. It says nothing instead.
- **Ochre ground, black rings — refuge.** The figure people learn first and forget last: a sealed, breathable volume, maintained as marked. A ring on a hatch frame says *air behind this hatch*; a ring beneath chevrons says *air that way*.

One further figure strikes the rest: a **diagonal bar** across any band voids it — closed, lost, no longer to be relied on. The bar's colour is whatever contrasts; the figure, not the paint, is the negation. The schedule requires the strike rather than the removal, and the reasoning is pure Compact: a scraped-off ring looks like a wall, but a struck ring is a record that something was promised here and is no longer.

The stack order is fixed — hazard, way, count, refuge — and the crews' gloss for it is better than the schedule's: *bad news first; read down to the air.*

Two rules make the grammar survivable. **Colour never carries meaning alone**: each figure class belongs to exactly one band, so under red emergency lighting, in smoke, or to eyes that sort colour differently, a panel goes monochrome and loses nothing. And **placement is part of the sentence**: a panel sits on the fixed frame beside a hatch, latch side, shoulder height — never on the moving leaf, because a door standing open somewhere else would carry its promise to the wrong wall. Junction panels sit on the corner post; cargo marks go on two adjacent faces of the same corner, so no honest stack can hide them. Proportions, not sizes, are what the schedule fixes — the same mark reads at thumb height on a valve and at full stretch across a berth mouth.

## Whose Standard It Is

The habit is older than the schedule. Ark companies of [the generation-ark era](/star-rangers/lore/the-generation-ark-era/) stencilled their hulls in figures for a plain reason: on a crossing measured in lifetimes, the ship's speech would drift, and a mark on a pressure door had to stay readable to whoever stood in front of it three generations on, in whatever the ship's language had become by then. Paint outlasts grammar, and the people who marked the arks were marking for descendants they would never meet. The Belt's depot crews kept versions of the habit through the sublight centuries — the [Knarr Line](/star-rangers/lore/knarr-line/) still paints an older cousin of the standard, unanswerable to the Compact's schedule and proud of it — and the Compact codified what its habitats were already doing, fixed the figures, and published the result.

So the plainmark is a Compact standard, and it is scoped like one. It runs wall to wall through the five member habitats, where the case for it is sharpest: dense mixed populations, transient crews off every registry, and corridors where a wrong turn meets vacuum. It travels where the Compact's problem travels — with Compact-certified fittings and Compact-trained maintainers, into the dockside bands of ports that take foreign hulls in numbers — and it thins out exactly as fast as that problem does. The [Celtic Union](/star-rangers/lore/celtic-union-of-planets/) letters its corridors in its members' own languages, as any polity does where nearly everyone reads them; the Compact's schedule has no writ there and claims none.

And the standard is drawn for one body. Panels sit at a standing human's shoulder; counts fit a human glance; the four grounds were chosen for separability by human colour vision, common variations included — which is why the figure rule exists at all. The record does not pretend the plainmark serves other eyes. Species with other bodies and other senses mark their own infrastructure by their own conventions, and a Compact panel on a bulkhead makes a claim about who maintains the bulkhead, not about who ought to be able to read it.

That, in the end, is what a marked corridor tells you. The schedule requires that whoever holds a pressure boundary keep its marks current, so a fresh plainmark is a small checkable claim: someone stood here recently, with paint and a stencil, and renewed a promise. A flaking one tells its own story, and surveyors read that story exactly the way it looks like it should be read.

## What It Cannot Say

The record [refuses the universal translator](/star-rangers/lore/what-the-record-refuses/): language is learned, and translations carry error bars. The plainmark is not an exception to that discipline — it is the same discipline arrived at from the other side. A notation that needs no translation is only honest if it carries far less than language does, and the plainmark's entire design is the paying of that price in full.

It cannot name. No person, no vessel, no company, no world can be written in it; a berth count is not the ship lying in the berth. It has no verbs, so it cannot instruct, request, forbid or promise. It has no tenses, so it speaks only of now: no *was*, no *will be*, no *unless*. It can mark a hazard and cannot say why the hazard is there; it can strike a refuge and cannot say where the people went. And it cannot coin — the figure set is closed, a new sign is a schedule revision through the Compact's own deliberate process, and the years that takes are treated as a feature, because a notation you can improvise in is a notation strangers can no longer trust.

Everything a plainmark can say fits in a breath: *what kind of thing, which way, how many, and whether the promise still holds.* Read aloud, it comes out in the reader's own tongue, and no two readers' glosses match word for word — which costs nothing, because the mark's whole content survives any of them. That is the measure of how little content there is, and the Compact's schedule opens by saying so: the plainmark does not replace language. It stands where language fails — the stranger, the dark, the smoke, the worst day — and it holds there precisely because it attempts so little.
