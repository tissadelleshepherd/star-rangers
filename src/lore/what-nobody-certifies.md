A hull is certified. A route is certified, and expires. A boundary analyst carries a designation, and a lawful mind carries a commissioning. Ask which office certifies a settlement's right to be where it is, and the question has no answer — not a difficult one, not a contested one. There is no such office.

## What Is Certified

The record is unusually thorough about this, which is what makes the omission visible.

- **Hulls.** The [Safety Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-safety-corps/) certifies them, branch by hazard, to published standards, and files its own bad calls beside them.
- **Routes.** The [Navigation Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-navigation-corps/) certifies those, and **that certificate expires**, because a corridor drifts away from the survey that cleared it.
- **People.** The certified boundary analyst designation is issued on examinable grounds, reviewed, and refused to external bodies case by case.
- **Minds.** Commissioning authority over agentic systems belongs to the AI Governance Commission, and no Ranger field system operates outside [kernel compliance](/star-rangers/lore/ai-safety-kernel/).

Four different objects, four bodies, four sets of standards, and in every case a document somebody can point at and a body somebody can complain to.

**Nothing performs the equivalent office for a licence to be somewhere.** No standard, no issuer, no expiry, no review, and no complaint route. A settlement charter is issued once, by whoever was competent at the time, and is thereafter simply a fact about the world.

## Why Not

Three reasons, and only the third is interesting.

**Certification is a method for things that fail testably.** The Corps' whole practice is to *define failure, test for it, certify what does not fail that way, and preserve the standards that keep certification meaningful.* A hull's aperture tolerance can be exceeded. A route's geometry can drift past its survey. **A charter has no physical failure mode**; it cannot be put in a rig, and there is nothing in it for an instrument to disagree with. The method that built the Corps has no purchase here, and the Corps has never pretended otherwise.

**And certification is voluntary in any case.** The Safety Corps *has no power whatsoever to make anyone use it*; certification is a matter for the polity registering the vessel. A charter standard nobody was obliged to meet would document the problem rather than solve it.

**But the reason that decides it is jurisdictional, and it was argued out on purpose.** A body competent to review whether a settlement should be where it is must be able to **enter** and to **compel** — to go and look without being asked, and to act on what it found. Those are exactly the two powers the Star Rangers were forbidden at founding.

## The Argument That Was Actually Had

The charter's fiercest negotiation was never about what the Rangers could do. It was about what the outer-station delegations would make them swear, in writing, never to become: **no authority on planets, none inside self-governing orbital habitats, and departure when the invitation is withdrawn.**

**The SSDC objected, and objected on precisely this ground: that those limits created dangerous gaps.** They were not wrong, and the record does not pretend they were.

The delegations' reply is the sentence the whole arrangement rests on — that *a boundary-operations body with unrestricted right of entry would simply be the MSC wearing a different crest.* They had recent memory of what such a body does with such a power, and they preferred the gaps.

**Both parties were right, and the record holds both without adjudicating.** The gap is real and the objection to closing it is real, and what looks from a distance like an institutional oversight is a decision that was taken in full knowledge, by people who could name the cost and accepted it.

## Where the Mechanism Grips, It Holds

This is worth saying before the failures, because the failures are not the usual case.

The Rangers enter a world by invitation and leave when it is withdrawn, and the [Cerebraun](/star-rangers/lore/cerebraun/) withdrawal is cited as often as it is for a plain reason: the limit is real, it gets exercised, and it holds. A protection that is never tested is a claim. That one is a practice.

## And Where It Fails, It Fails in Two Directions

**The clause assumes a counterparty that persists** — somebody able to issue an invitation, and still there to withdraw it. The record now holds a published case on each side of that assumption, and they fail in opposite directions.

**[Fliade](/star-rangers/lore/planets/fliade/) — nobody to issue.** There is no body on that planet able to issue an invitation in any form the charter recognises, or withdraw one. The Pandoids did not come to anyone; they were arrived at, by a party that could leave whenever it chose and return with whatever it liked. The protection is thinnest exactly where the asymmetry is greatest.

**[Corryn](/star-rangers/lore/planets/corryn/) — nobody to withdraw.** A permission *was* issued, in proper form, by an authority that then stopped existing. No successor assumed the file. The [Combine](/star-rangers/lore/halvern-combine/) holding it has broken no term and has never been served notice by any office competent to issue one, and both halves of that are true.

**One has no counterparty yet. The other had one and outlived it.** Same absence, reached from opposite ends, and neither is a case anybody drafted for.

## What Would Close It

An office with standing to review a charter would need the two powers the founding argument refused, and there is no third arrangement anybody has proposed that does not reduce to them. **So the gap is not the kind that better drafting closes.** It is the shape of a decision, seen from underneath.

**Whether the trade was correct is not something this record settles.** The Archive holds the argument, both sides of it, and the two cases where the cost came due. It does not hold a verdict, and the officers who work nearest the gap are the least inclined to offer one — which is usually a sign that the people closest to a question have found it harder than it looks.
