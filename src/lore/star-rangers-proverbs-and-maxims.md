The Corps trains its officers to write flatly. A finding is stated in a form that can be checked, confidence is not manufactured to make a report sound more finished than the evidence supports, and a report that cannot survive being read back in a hearing six years later has not done its job — the whole house style is set out in [Decision-Making and Communication in the Star Rangers](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-decision-and-communication/).

And then the same service runs, in its colleges and its corridors and the first ten minutes of every handover, on a body of compressed sayings, not one of which would be admissible in anything it files.

Both things are true at once, and the rule that reconciles them is short enough to state in a line: **a maxim is a mnemonic for a discipline, never a substitute for a finding.**

## Where a Maxim Is Allowed, and Where It Is Not

The line is drawn at the document. A saying belongs in the college, in the corridor, over a handover, and to a cadet who has just made the mistake it names. It does not belong in a log, a report, a certification, or a finding. An officer who answers a question with a proverb has stopped reporting; a board that accepts one has stopped examining. The [Academy](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-academy/) teaches the sayings and the same faculty fails a submission that leans on one, and the cadets who notice the apparent inconsistency are told, correctly, that it is the whole lesson.

There is a second reason for keeping them off the wire, and it is the less obvious one. The Corps' reporting style is deliberately low-context because the Corps is so often the thing carrying a statement across a species boundary, and a message that needs shared cultural context to parse is a message that degrades in transit. A proverb is the opposite construction. It is maximum context, compressed — the one part of Ranger speech that reliably does not survive translation intact. What Rangers say to each other about the shape of the work is not what the Corps says as courier.

## On the Record

Most of the Corps' sayings are not the Corps'. They come from the [Survey Archive](/star-rangers/codex/the-verification-doctrine/), whose standing doctrine is posted at every desk and has been worn down by usage into things that fit in a sentence.

- **"A claim is not a holding."** Article One, near enough verbatim. Everything arrives as a claim and carries its source on its face; the door believes nothing, which is precisely what lets the door admit anything. New officers hear it as coldness and are meant to.
- **"Evidence moves standing. Nothing else moves standing."** Article Three, exactly. Not seniority, not urgency, not eloquence, not repetition, not the number of desks a claim has crossed.
- **"Two envelopes, one report."** The field shorthand for the rest of Article Three: the same report arriving twice is one piece of evidence wearing two envelopes. It is the saying most often deployed against a corroboration that turns out to be an echo.
- **"We never overwrite. We append."** From [Three Disciplines of the Record](/star-rangers/codex/three-disciplines-of-the-record/). The record of the Corps' errors is a shelf, not a gap.
- **"Mark the absence; do not explain it."** [Sen](/star-rangers/characters/sen/)'s [Marked Absences](/star-rangers/codex/marked-absences/), stated by its author as the one line the paper reduces to. An unexplained gap invites a better-informed colleague to fill it; an explained gap invites the record to defend a guess forever.
- **"A rumour with good handwriting."** Not advice but a verdict, and the most quoted phrase the Archive has ever produced: what a Ranger calls an assertion presented without its standing.

The borrowing is documented and was invited. Sen's working paper on the three disciplines ends by arguing that the next institution to need this discipline should not begin by inventing one — *borrow it whole* — and the Corps, which had been doing exactly that for a century by the time the paper was circulated, has never pretended otherwise.

## On What an Officer May Say

- **"Say what you can support."** The oldest of them, and the only one that is also a section heading in standing doctrine. Inherited whole, with the rest of the certification habit, from the [Safety Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-safety-corps/)'s seven centuries as a standards body.
- **"Quiet is not doubt; loud is not certain."** Two clauses because it is addressed to two people. The first half is for the officer, and grants nothing: it does not permit vagueness, it permits a supportable finding to be delivered without being sold. The second half is for the room, and is the harder instruction, because it asks a board to read the evidence in front of it rather than the volume of the person presenting it. [Tissadelle Shepherd](/star-rangers/characters/tissadelle-shepherd/) is the standing example and equally the standing embarrassment — the record of boards mistaking a Ranger who will not claim past her evidence for one who is not sure of it is long enough by now to count as a pattern, which is a fair measure of how much easier the maxim is to state than to apply. The distinction it is trying to preserve is that the confidence and the performance of confidence are different objects, and only one of them is being withheld.
- **"The pleasing reading is checked first."** Flattery, in Corps usage, is not a compliment; it is a property an explanation can have. The maxim treats an account that flatters the person holding it as a class of evidence with a known defect — the reading you will examine least, because you already want it — and moves it to the front of the queue for exactly that reason. The practice is old and well documented: the officer at Dock Seven who refused to make a finding she could not defend, the analyst on the boundary desk who refused to name a mechanism she had no evidence for, and the field analyst who declined to record the flattering explanation of the event she had logged. [The Institute](/star-rangers/lore/the-institute/) has filed a charge of mythologising against the Corps notwithstanding, which is a strange charge to bring against the one body in settled space that declines the pleasing account on principle.
- **"Write it for the hearing six years out."** The test the house style actually applies. Not *is this true*, which the writer cannot settle alone, but *will this hold up when it is read back by someone who was not there, to someone who is not sympathetic, after everyone who remembers has been reassigned*.

## On the Watch

- **"Attend on the days nothing is wrong."** Sen's phrase, from the same paper: the discipline of standing beside something that must not drift and attending to it on the days nothing is wrong, so that on the day something is wrong, someone competent is already there. Every college teaches it against the same case. Two chronometers in Dock Seven disagreed by forty seconds; the discrepancy was classified as a calibration issue and the report closed; recalibration was performed; the discrepancy returned inside forty-eight hours and was written into the maintenance record as a single line, and not filed as a new incident. That single line is the part the colleges dwell on. The record it opened runs eleven years.
- **"A loud stop, not a quiet error."** Sen again, and the load-bearing preference behind the whole verification apparatus: a count that fails honestly can be repeated, and a count that succeeds dishonestly poisons every count after it.
- **"The process is most necessary exactly when skipping it is most attractive."** Quoted verbatim, usually by whoever is being asked to skip it.

## At the Boundary

- **"Decide at the boundary, not at the desk."** The charter clause in five words, written directly against the [Military Space Command](/star-rangers/lore/military-space-command/)'s documented inability to answer a boundary event in under six weeks. Authority sits with whoever is closest to the event and holds the rank, not with whoever is most senior and furthest from it.
- **"Test it against the ground before you pass it down."** [Karla Wender](/star-rangers/characters/karla-wender/)'s contribution, though she has never claimed it and the Archive cannot show she coined it. An officer who passes an order down without checking it against ground truth has reintroduced the MSC's failure mode one rank at a time, and the difference between an order that can be executed and one that only looks executable on a map is the difference the saying exists to keep visible.
- **"Neither door is comfortable."** [Frontier Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-frontier-corps/) usage, and the college's standing instruction on its two documented caretaker failures — a world the Corps correctly stayed out of that went dark anyway, and a world it re-entered on a remnant magistrate's arguable authority where Rangers escorting food convoys were being called occupiers inside a year. A Frontier Ranger is expected to find neither of them comfortable. The saying is that instruction worn smooth, and its point is that the discomfort is the correct reading rather than a failure to have resolved the question.
- **"You pay it every time, not once."** The Corps' answer to the officer who has adopted a discipline and expects to have finished paying for it. Declining to claim past the evidence costs a Ranger slower belief in rooms that would have taken a louder officer at volume, and it costs that on every occasion, not on the first one. The maxim is not consolation. It is a price list.

## On the Provenance of the Sayings Themselves

The Archive can source about half of the above to a document, a date, and a desk. The rest arrived in service usage without an origin, and are old enough that the officers who first said them are not identifiable from anything the Archive holds.

It would be easy to supply attributions. Several of these sayings sit close enough to a known officer's practice that a plausible name could be attached to each, and the Corps has more than once been offered a tidy account of where its own idiom came from. The Archive has declined, on the doctrine it teaches: an origin invented to fill a gap becomes an origin the record must defend forever. The sayings whose provenance is unrecorded are marked here as unrecorded, and they will stay that way until evidence moves them — which is, as it happens, one of them.
