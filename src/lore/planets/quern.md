Quern's weather report has not been revised since it was first filed, and the Survey Corps does not expect to revise it. The world has weather the way a river has a shape: violent everywhere, and the same violence in the same places, on no calendar at all — a deficiency its settlers have made good in their own way, as this entry will come to.

*Ten seconds from a fixed camera at the Millrace, looking along the rain toward the storm wall.*

**Quern** is the innermost planet of the **Kiln System**, a red dwarf star small enough and close enough to hold its one rocky world face-on. The planet is tidally locked: its rotation and its orbit are the same twenty-nine hours, so one hemisphere keeps the star and the other keeps the dark, permanently. The first survey crew named the star for what it does and the planet for what the star does *to* it — a quern being the pair of millstones a hand-mill grinds between — and by the time they were done charting, the naming theme was carrying itself. The people who later settled the world declined the whole vocabulary and named the only three things that matter when you live there. The record keeps both sets of names, and is careful about whose is whose.

## The Standing Storm

The working term on the first survey was *the hurricane*, and the record keeps the word while noting everything wrong with it. Nothing in the storm is water. Nothing about it is tropical. And it will never move on or make landfall, having no coast, no season, and nowhere else to be.

Directly beneath the star, rock does not stay rock. The substellar region — the survey crews called it **the Glazing**; the settlers call the whole sunward hemisphere **the Burn** — is an open melt, a desert of molten silicate under a sky that never changes its angle of light. Heat rising off the melt drives a single permanent cyclonic storm, anchored where its fuel is and organised into rotation by the planet's own twenty-nine-hour spin. What the storm carries is not water vapour but rock vapour: sodium and silicon monoxide boiled off the melt, lofted, cooled at the storm's rim, and returned as a thin, perpetual rain of stone. The storm cannot exhaust its energy source and cannot leave it, so it does what no storm on a turning world is permitted to do — it simply continues.

## The Night Side

The far hemisphere has never seen its sun. What warmth crosses to it arrives worn out, and the dark side pays the debt the bright side runs up: everything on Quern that can freeze ends there, laid down in order of its freezing points. Water ice nearest the light, in ranges pressed outward from the storm belt. Carbon dioxide frost beyond that, where water has long since finished falling. And at the antistellar deep — the coldest ground on the world, under a sky of unmoving stars — the air itself gives up. Nitrogen, the bulk of Quern's atmosphere, condenses and falls as snow.

The survey crews' name for those last fields was **the Meal**, that being what a quern produces; the settlers' name for the whole hemisphere is **the Hush**, and theirs is the accurate one. It is the stillest place the Survey Corps has ever instrumented: no wind that a landed probe recorded above a whisper, no sound but the instrument's own, and snowfall too fine and too sparse to see except in a lamp beam — the atmosphere, arriving at its own funeral, a few grains at a time.

## The Millrace

Between the two hemispheres runs the belt the world is named for. Day-side air, hot and laden, pours toward the dark at altitude; night-side air, cold and dense, drains back toward the light along the ground. The two flows meet in a ring around the whole planet and do not mix so much as grind — a permanent shear zone, some few hundred kilometres wide, that the crews called **the Millrace**, the record calls the terminator belt when it is being formal, and the settlers call **the Howl**, for the reason anyone who has stood in it will supply unasked.

It is the only place on Quern where liquid water exists, and the water is never still. Steam driven off the day side condenses the moment it crosses into the cold, and the winds it condenses into run along the belt, not across it, so the rain arrives horizontally — carried parallel to the ground at speeds the first survey logged and the second survey declined to fly in. The shear keeps supercooled droplets and ice in constant collision, and the charge that builds discharges where it builds: inside the rain. Lightning at the Millrace does not come down. It comes *along* — a lit seam travelling inside the weather, visible from orbit as a flicker running the ring of the world.

## The Breathing

The belt is permanent; its position is not quite. Over a cycle of roughly three standard years — never exactly, and never twice the same — the whole ring of weather walks night-ward by some tens of kilometres and walks back, as the standing storm and the cold return flow trade momentum in a slow oscillation the Science Corps has modelled and the settlers have never needed to. The rain-line advances over ground that was frozen, and retreats from ground it had been drowning, and everything that lives on Quern lives by where it stands in that walk.

The settlers call the cycle **the Breathing**, and one full breath is their year. It cannot be computed in advance, only watched: the calendar is kept by eye, against lines of waist-high marker stones — the settlers call them **turning-stones**, and the record notes that the echo of the world's name is their joke, not the surveyors' — set across the walk of the rain-line generations ago and maintained since. When the rain reaches the far stones and turns, the year turns, and the watch that saw it turn says so. A calendar with no arithmetic and no anniversary twice alike; the Archive converts their dates to standard the way it converts [everyone's](/star-rangers/lore/solar-time-and-local-calendars/), and notes that Quern's is the only calendar on file that is also a meteorological record.

## The Settlement

People live in the Howl. This is the fact about Quern that most requires stating plainly, because nothing else in this entry suggests it should be possible, and the Survey Corps' own engineering reviews said it was not: three proposals for a permanent station in the belt are on file, each declined on the finding that no design on record would survive it. The settlers' answer, which was not submitted to any review, was that the Corps was designing buildings that resist the wind, and the wind cannot be resisted — only declined to be argued with.

The settlements sit along the belt's night-ward edge, behind the standing rain — the last territory with liquid water, the first with bearable wind. The architecture is **wind-scooped**: low, keeled, faired structures with no flat face to the flow and every entrance in the lee, shaped less like buildings than like stones the river has already finished with. The wind does the maintenance — a correctly shaped house is self-clearing, scoured smooth rather than scoured away — and a wrongly shaped one is corrected by the same authority, faster. Nothing stands tall, nothing stands square, and a Quernish settlement seen from orbit reads as a reef, not a town.

Power is the reason anyone came. Quern's two hemispheres are the largest temperature difference in charted space with a habitable seam between them, and the settlement drinks directly from the gradient: buried conductor loops run day-ward into ground hot enough to scald and night-ward into ground cold enough to shatter, and the heat that flows between them is tapped for baseload that has never once, in the settlement's history, been the limiting factor in anything. The founding was an energy concern with refining ambitions, out in the first decades of fold transit, when a great deal was financed on the strength of a route having been confirmed; the concern did not outlast its founders, and the people did. The same surplus makes the air: Quern's own atmosphere is nitrogen and carbon dioxide, unbreathable, and every lungful under a Quernish roof is electrolysed out of the rain. Outside, everyone is masked, always. The settlers are a people of the wind who have never once felt it on their faces, and their word for removing a mask indoors is the same as their word for coming home.

Quern holds no charter and belongs to no polity. The population has never exceeded the low tens of thousands, and the settlement has declined, politely and repeatedly, every suggestion that it is a hardship posting: the standard reply on file is that the weather is the most reliable thing any of them has ever lived under.

## The Burn, the Howl, and the Hush

The settlers' account of their world divides it into three domains, and the record files the account as theirs without ruling on its content, as it does elsewhere. **The Burn** is the sunward hemisphere: the domain of what cannot be looked at, spoken of with respect and visited by nobody. **The Howl** is the belt and everything alive: the domain of work, weather, argument, and each other. **The Hush** is the dark: the domain of what is finished. The dead of Quern are carried night-ward, past the last turning-stones, and left where nothing decays and nothing arrives; what the settlers hold about that ground, they have not offered to the record, and the record has not asked.

One practice is documented because it generates traffic the survey schedule has to accommodate. In the Howl the sky is weather, permanently — no sun, no stars, ever — and a Quernish coming-of-age is the **two witnessings**: a journey day-ward until the cloud thins and the sun shows, low and vast through the last of the rain, and a journey night-ward past the ice ranges until the sky opens and the stars stand still. Having seen both roofs of the house, the witness is an adult, entitled to stand a rain-watch and to be believed about what the stones show. The settlers do not describe the two journeys as devotional, and the record observes that they do not describe them as anything else either.

## Balance or Collapse

One measurement gives Quern its standing scientific interest, and the record is careful about what the measurement does and does not say. Nitrogen is leaving the atmosphere. The snowfall in the Hush is deposition, and deposition is measurable: year on year, the antistellar cap gains, and the air is the only place the gain can come from.

Two readings of that fact are held on file. The first is equilibrium: ice under its own weight flows, however slowly, and the cap's slow spread back toward the light may return frozen atmosphere to the Millrace as fast as the dark lays it down — in which case the storms are a cycle, and Quern keeps its air indefinitely. The second is collapse: the return flow may not balance the fall, in which case the atmosphere is spending itself, the Howl is the sound of it being spent, and Quern is partway through becoming an airless world with one bright molten eye — on a schedule of millions of years, which the world can afford and a survey baseline cannot resolve.

The Archive holds both readings and has not been asked to choose between them; on the present data it could not, and says so. The best data there is, it owes to the calendar: the turning-stone records, kept by eye and by watch for the whole life of the settlement, are the longest continuous measurement of the belt's position in existence, and the Survey Corps — which could not build an instrument that survives the Howl — transcribes the settlement's instead. The settlers, asked which reading they hold, are on record declining the question in almost the Archive's own voice — [the line-keepers' own teaching](/star-rangers/codex/what-the-stones-say/) instructs every new watcher in the reply: the stones say where the rain is, not where it is going.

## Survey Status

Survey passes are periodic and orbital, supplemented by the settlement's surface records under a standing transcription arrangement. The one operational note the record carries forward from every pass is the first crew's, filed before anyone lived there and never improved on: *the world is not hostile; it is merely entirely occupied.* The settlers, informed of the line, are said to have approved of everything in it except the tense.
