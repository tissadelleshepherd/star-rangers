The Survey Corps' first orbital pass over Winterbourne logged a settlement pattern regular enough that the duty officer flagged it as deliberate planning. It is deliberate, in the sense that people chose it. It is also, to within a few hundred metres, exactly where the water comes out of the ground.

## The Spring Line

Most of the settled continent is a carbonate platform some four hundred metres thick — a chalk analogue, laid down under a shallow sea long before anything walked on it, then uplifted and tilted. The result is the shape the whole world is organised around: a long **escarpment** with a steep face, and behind it a gentle **dip slope** running away for sixty kilometres.

Chalk holds water rather than shedding it. Rain falling on the high down percolates straight through, travels down through the mass, and meets the impermeable clay the platform sits on. Having nowhere further to go, it runs sideways and emerges at the foot of the scarp in a line of springs, spaced by the local geology at roughly two to three kilometres.

Every one of those springs has a village on it.

The streams they feed are **winterbournes**: they flow from midwinter through to early summer while the water table stands high, then fall back to dry gravel by late summer and stay that way until the rain returns. Nobody on the planet finds this remarkable, which is why the name stuck to the world rather than to any one of them.

## The Villages

West to east along the scarp foot: **Ashenden**, **Nether Wickham** and **Over Wickham**, **Chilverstoke**, **Marlbrook**, **Bramblecote**, and **Hazelcombe**, with the market town of **Downminster** where the lanes converge on the one crossing point of any size. The roads run along the spring line rather than over the down, for the same reason the villages do: that is the easy going, and it always was.

The parish boundaries are the part that repays a second look. Each runs as a long, narrow strip *perpendicular* to the line of villages — from the wet meadow at the bottom, up across the arable of the lower slope, to the open grazing on the high down. No parish is compact. Every parish has a share of all three. The shape is not aesthetic and was not decreed: it is what you get when a settlement wants water, ground it can plough, and somewhere to run sheep, and the three things lie in bands one above the other.

The downland turf itself is a product of the sheep and not a natural state. Left ungrazed it goes to scrub within a decade and to woodland inside a century, which has happened twice on record where a parish lost its flock, and been reversed both times by putting the flock back.

What the world supplied was air, water and chalk, and that is the whole of it. Winterbourne's atmosphere is breathable because something made it so, and that something built the carbonate platform under a sea and has never been found on the land: the Survey Corps' ecology branch logs the native biosphere as marine and the downs, before the founders, as bare rock under a crust of nothing taller than a lichen. Everything green on the scarp came off the ship. The turf, the arable, the reed in the thatch and the sheep that keep the turf are Terran stock, set into the chalk's weathered mantle with the soil life they needed over the first generations, which is the labour the world asked of its founders in place of a dome. Nothing native is eaten, and nothing native was cleared to make room; the world is not in [Tír na nÓg's](/star-rangers/lore/planets/tir-na-nog/) category, and the entry says so because a reader of this page could otherwise take a downland village for a second miracle.

None of this is quaint in the sense of being backward. The thatch is certified and periodically recertified; the medicine is current; the fold route to the nearest transit point is a day's run. As [What the Record Refuses](/star-rangers/lore/what-the-record-refuses/) puts it about places like this one, technology that does not announce itself does not reshape what a place looks like — and nothing about the last several centuries has made a reed roof stop working.

## Winnow Hanger

One settlement is not on the spring line. **Winnow Hanger** sits high on the dip slope inside a hillside wood, with no spring within four kilometres and no winterbourne at all. It keeps a **dew pond** instead: a shallow clay-lined hollow that has supplied the place, with two interruptions, since it was dug.

The name is a misnomer and the Archive is content to say so. Dew contributes almost nothing. The pond fills from rain, and from mist condensing on the trees of the hanger above it and dripping clear of the canopy — which is precisely why the wood is not cut, and why a settlement that appears to have been sited carelessly was in fact sited on the only part of the high down where that works.

Winnow Hanger is also the only settlement on Winterbourne that has ever had to be supplied from outside. Both occasions were droughts, both are in the parish record, and the second is the reason the pond now has a certified liner under the clay.

## What the Record Will Not Settle

Whether any of this was *intended* is a separate question from whether it works, and the Archive has not been able to close it.

Three accounts are held. The first is that the pattern was prescribed — a founding instrument is said to have set out parish layout, village spacing and the strip boundaries in advance, and the settlement followed the document. The second is that no document was needed: the same rock, the same water and the same animal will produce the same answer anywhere they occur together, and the names followed the shapes afterwards, as names generally do. The third disputes neither, and asks instead about the instrument — the copy held on-world is a fair copy of uncertain date, in a hand that is not the one it claims, and what it records may be a later description of an arrangement that had already settled itself.

All three are on file. None has been adjudicated, and the Archive has not been asked to try. What the file does note is the one observation none of the three has to explain away: wherever the spring line runs, the villages are on it, on a world where nobody had to be told.

## Not a Union World

Winterbourne is not a member of the [Celtic Union of Planets](/star-rangers/lore/celtic-union-of-planets/), whose charter traditions are Irish, Welsh, Cornish, Scottish, Manx and Breton, and which Winterbourne's founders were none of. It holds no Solar System charter either, which puts it in the same administrative category as the [Kingdom of the Five Islands](/star-rangers/lore/planets/kingdom-of-the-five-islands/) for entirely different reasons — the Kingdom because it was lost and found again, Winterbourne because the question has never been pressed.

Union membership has been raised informally more than once. On each occasion the parish councils have undertaken to consider it at the next meeting, and on each occasion they have.
