Suvra Kel kept the boundary watch of a geothermal province on Sardain before the survey came: three-day vigils over warm ground under a sky that drinks heat, counting what moved in the dark by its glow. She was among the first [Ilveth](/star-rangers/lore/ilveth/) to take Corps service, and the record's phrase for humans — *lanterns that cannot be put out* — is hers, offered without malice in her first season among them and impossible to improve.

She is Ilveth, cold-bodied, and **not humanoid**; her mirror-pit sense reads presence, temperature and flow in no light at all, and fails honestly where her species' page says it fails — rain, mist, steam, and any room kept at everyone else's comfort. Aboard station she wears the modular shell as a cooling shell, keeps chilled quarters, and speaks click-speech only, the thermal register being unusable in a warm hull. *Suvra Kel* is itself the click-speech handle: her full name is a warm-write pattern, and the record cannot carry it.

Her work is search and rescue, and the placement is not sentiment. In smoke, blackout and collapsed structure she finds the living the way anyone else finds a lit window, and she reads a casualty before instruments do — warmth pooling where it should not, extremities going quiet, the difference between unconscious and going cold. One operational habit follows from the physics and the teams she serves with have learned to plan around it: **she goes in ahead of the suppression**, because the water that saves the compartment is the one thing in it that blinds her.

## Character Notes

Suvra does not tire on a watch, and she prices effort the way her world does. Asked whether she will look for someone, she does not say yes; she says how long she can watch, and it is always longer than anyone thought to ask. Her one recorded maxim is heat-thrift applied to rescue: *"Do not spend what the dark will take anyway. Spend where it is still warm."*

Deprived of the costly register aboard ship, she has made one of her own: she writes almost nothing, and what she writes she means at the weight a warm-write would carry on Sardain. Crewmates keep her notes.

She sees more of every room than she answers to. A fever, an exhaustion, a fear held behind a steady face — all of it is visible to her and none of it is replied to, because Sardain's oldest courtesy came aboard with her: what a body says was not addressed to you.
