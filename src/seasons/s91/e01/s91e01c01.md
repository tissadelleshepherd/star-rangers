::: pov sile-ni-bhraonain

The valley went dark from the bottom up, the way it always did, because the bottom farms had the furthest to walk to the good ground and liked to be out early.

Síle watched it come. The long glasshouses down by the river went first, one panel of light folding after another, then the hall, then the houses along the warm road, until the dark reached her own gate and she went in and did what everyone in the valley was doing at that hour. She turned off the grow lamps over the seed trays. She turned off the heat-tell on the door. She stood a moment over the switch for the frost line, which was the one light the standard said a keeper might leave, and left it off, as her mother had, and her mother's mother, who had argued with the standard about it for forty years and won.

Then she went out and stood in the snow with her back to the glass, and it was oíche dhrithle, and the sky began.

The first sparks always came singly. One, high and to the north, white-gold, falling so slowly you could look away and find it again. Then three. Then too many to count, which was when the children down the road stopped counting out loud and started saying *there* and *there* instead, and the little ones were lifted onto shoulders, and nobody said anything that mattered for an hour.

She did not look at the ridge until the second hour. She never did; she had learned not to.

It was there. Up past the grazing, where the slim mast stood against the stars with its armour on, one small yellow lamp was burning in the window of the beacon hut, as it had burned every crossing night since she had been tall enough to see over the wall.

When she was a girl she had thought it was for safety, and been proud of it. When she was older she had thought it was rudeness, and been angry. For a few years in between she had thought it was somebody's grief, and been careful not to look at it at all. Now she was sixty, and the beacon crew had changed four times that she knew of, and the lamp had not, and she had stopped thinking anything about it and simply kept it company. It did no harm. You could see the drithle perfectly well with it there. It was only that the whole world was dark, and there was one light, and it was the ridge's.

A spark crossed directly over the mast and went on, slow, all the way to the southern peaks.

"Oíche mhaith," she said to it, the lamp or the spark, she was never quite sure which, and went in at the end of the crossing hour to put the kettle on in the dark.

::: pov tadhg-mac-giolla

The beacon hut had one window, and Tadhg had put the shutter across it at the crossing hour, and then, because it was his eleventh year on the ridge and he knew himself, taken the shutter down again.

The hut was dark. He had checked that twice, by feel. The telemetry board was hooded, the stove was banked, the lamp on the hook was cold and had been since the autumn crossing season began. The relays above him were running on their own, which was what the armour was for, and on his board under its hood he could hear the small dry patter of the fine stuff on the shielding, like sleet that had decided to be polite about it.

He sat on the step with a blanket and watched the valley go out.

It went from the bottom up. It always did. He liked to see it, the long glasshouses down by the river folding away panel by panel, the hall, the warm road, until the whole chain below him was snow and starlight and the shapes of people standing still.

All but one.

At the head of the valley, where the last holding sat against the rock, there was a glasshouse that did not go out. Not the house; the house went dark with the rest. The glass. One low green-gold line, along the bottom of it, like a frost line left on, burning through every crossing night he had sat on this step to watch.

His first year he had nearly gone down to say something. His second year he had asked the woman before him on the ridge, who had laughed and told him the keeper at the head of the valley had been arguing with the standard since before either of them was born, and to leave her be. He had left her be. And somewhere in the third year, or the fourth, he had stopped being curious about it and started, without ever deciding to, waiting for it. The whole world was dark, and there was one light, and it was hers.

He had never seen her up close. She sent a jar of preserved bilberries up with the spring grazing, every year, labelled in a careful hand *for the ridge*, and he sent down the crossing calendar for the coming year, which she had no need of, because nobody born on Drithane needed it.

A spark came over the head of the valley, very slow, and crossed directly above him, and went on south.

He logged the crossing at the end of the hour, because the relays wanted it: the band density, the shielding wear, the beacon timings. Under *exterior lights, station*, he wrote *none*, as he wrote every year, and it was true.

Then he sat a while longer on the step, by the one light in the valley, and said goodnight to it in the old way, under his breath, and did not wonder, any more, what it was that he was keeping company with.
