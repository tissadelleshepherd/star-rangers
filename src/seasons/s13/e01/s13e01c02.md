::::: scene 1

::: pov bram-tollick

The season's first calm came the way it always came, as a morning that did not know what to do with itself, and with it, round the lee side of Casimor where nothing lands on Krilzat, a boat.

Bram saw it from the wall. A boat in the first calm meant the court, because nobody else had a reason, and the court meant a letter, and a letter meant his father reading it at the gate with his lips not moving and then saying one sentence about it, which was the whole of what the watch did with the court. He went down to the gate to be there for the sentence.

The letter was the usual size. What came up the path with the runner who carried it was not usual: a case the length of a forearm, grey, sealed, with no mark on it the Kingdom made, carried the way you carry a thing you have been told not to drop and not told why.

"From the strangers," said the runner, meaning the delegation, which was what Ostrel called them. "The court let it come. The letter says."

His father read the letter at the gate with his lips not moving, and then read it again, which he did not do, and then said the sentence.

"The keeper decides."

That was all of it, and Bram waited, and there was more, which there never was.

"They asked for one thing," his father said. "The strangers. The court says it is the first thing they have asked for in three years and the court has let it come as far as this gate and no further unless the keeper says. It goes where the keeper puts it. It goes nowhere the keeper does not. It is theirs and it listens, the court says, and keeps what it hears, and does nothing else, and they are not coming for it, and the court does not know when they will." He looked at the case the way he looked at the keep, which was not often and not for long. "The keeper decides. I am the keeper. I have never decided anything about the Castle in my life and I am not going to start with a thing I cannot see inside."

He gave it to Bram.

"You keep the door," his father said. "Decide."

::::: scene 2

::: pov wren-pellam

They went down the right side, because the right side was Hallin's, and Hallin went all the way to where the lamp stopped, and Wren had the chronicle's right-side names in order and Bram had the ways, and between them, she had said, they could count.

That was the idea. You count the doors on the right against the names, and where a name has a door and a way, that door is in the chronicle; and where the doors go on past the names, that is the Castle going on past the chronicle, which the chronicle says it does, in the line that every reciter says fastest because nobody likes it: *and the rooms are more than the names.*

Bram carried the lamp and the grey case. Wren carried the names.

The first door on the right was Hallin's morning, and the fourth was a name, and the seventh was a name, and the ninth was the last one on the right the chronicle had anything to say about, *Ceres, who turned back*, which Wren had always taken for a story and which Bram said was a way: a figure at the ninth door, right side, that came out and went back in, twice a day, the same. Past the ninth the notches on the post upstairs had been cut by keepers who went further than the names, and the last of those keepers was Bram's grandmother, and past her, nobody.

They went past her. Bram said so, quietly, as if the post could hear.

The hall went down. The doors went on, right side, all shut, all the same height, the old stone taking no mark. The lamp made a room of light that walked with them and the dark closed up behind, and Wren found she was counting under her breath in the chronicle's voice, *ten, eleven, twelve*, with no names to put to them, which was the strangest thing she had done on Krilzat, stranger than Aldous: saying a count with nothing in it.

Then a door that was not shut.

It stood open. Not ajar: open, the way a door is open when somebody has gone through it and not come back to close it. Every door in the Castle was shut; Bram had said it the first evening and every evening since, *they're all shut, they're always shut, nobody has ever seen one open*. This one was open onto a room, and the room was dark, and when Bram lifted the lamp the room was not dark, and someone was standing in it.

::::: scene 3

::: pov bram-tollick

Standing. That was the thing Bram could not get past, and he had seen the Castle's people four thousand times.

They walked. Every one of them walked, or stood for as long as it takes to bar the gate and then went; they had ways, and the ways went somewhere. This one stood in the middle of a room past the notches, in the lamp's light, the lamp's own colour and more of it than the lamp had, no shadow under it, facing the door, and did not go anywhere. He stood in the doorway with the lamp up for as long as it would have taken Mereth to stand and go, and then as long again, and it stood.

"Lower the lamp," said Pellam's girl, behind him, very quietly.

He lowered it. The room went dark. There was nothing in the room.

He raised it. There it was, standing, exactly where it had been, as if the dark had been a door it had gone through and come back from without moving.

"It's the lamp," she said. "It's always been the lamp. Your father said: bring no lamp and see nothing. They aren't there in the dark. They're there in the *light*. We bring the light and they're in it." Her voice was doing the thing the court's children's voices did on the first evening, and she was fifteen and had been on Krilzat for the length of a storm, and he thought that she was managing it better than he was.

The case was under his arm. *The keeper decides.*

He thought about it for the length of one more Mereth. The strangers had asked for one thing in three years and the court had let it come to the gate and his father had put it in his hands and said *decide*, and the thing listened and kept what it heard and did nothing else, and here was a room nobody had been in, with a door nobody had seen open, and one of the Castle's people standing in it at no hour with no name.

He went in. Three steps, no more, the lamp held low so that the figure stayed in it, and set the case down on the floor just inside the door, against the wall, where it would be out of the way of anything that walked and would hear whatever there was to hear. He did not look at the figure while he did it. You do not look at them while you are doing something. It did not look at him; they never did.

Then he came out, and took the lamp with him, and the room was dark, and the door stayed open.

"Nobody closes that," he said. "It was open. We leave it open."

::::: scene 4

::: pov wren-pellam

The chronicle has one way to say that the Castle has done a thing, and Wren had known it her whole life and had never once needed it.

*A door was given.*

That is the line. That is what the chronicle says of Aldous, and of every door that has a name: the founding monarch *was given* the Castle, and the Castle, since, has given doors, and a keeper finds one that the last keeper did not record, and the reciters say *a door was given*, in whose time, on which side, and what stands in it. It is not a way of saying *found*. Wren had thought it was. Standing in the hall past the notches with the dark closed up behind and a door open in front, she understood that the chronicle meant exactly what it said, and that it was her house's turn to say it.

"I have to carry it back," she said. "The line. That's what I'm for. A house's youngest learns the names, and if a door is given in her time she carries the line to the reciters and they say it under the Grove at the next crowning and it's in the chronicle. That's what *in Pellam's time* means. I thought it was a way of dating things."

"What's the line?"

She said it, the way the reciter on Vellamar had taught her to say lines she did not yet have, slowly, so the shape would hold. "*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

Bram waited.

"And then the name," she said. "Only I don't have the name. Aldous came first and Mereth stood at the door and Hallin went down and was long, and they all have names because somebody carried the name back. Nobody carried this one. There isn't one. The chronicle can say that a door was given and that one stands in it, and that's all it can say, and it will say that under the Grove until somebody has a name, and nobody will, because the chronicle only knows the names it was given, and this one it wasn't."

"So it stands there with no name."

"In the chronicle. Yes." She looked at the open door. "What does the watch say?"

"The watch says it stands," said Bram. "Right side, past the ninth, still. We don't do names."

She thought about the strangers' grey case on the floor of the room, listening, keeping what it heard, doing nothing else, in a room with somebody in it who had no name on one record and no way on the other and was, as far as the court's record and the watch's between them could say, the only person in the Castle who had ever stood still.

::::: scene 5

::: pov bram-tollick

He cut the notch that evening, after the door was barred, with his father watching and not saying anything, which was his father's way of saying a great deal.

The post was old wood, grey as the keep, cut from the top down by every keeper who had stood in a room, and the last notch was his grandmother's, and he put his below it, one cut, with the knife the watch used for nothing else, and it was the first cut in the post since before he was born. His father looked at it for a while.

"Right side," his father said. "Past the ninth."

"Past the ninth. Open. One in it, standing. I put their thing inside the door."

"Standing."

"Standing."

His father nodded, slowly, the way the watch nodded at the Castle, which was the way you nod at weather, and went to see to the wall, and that was the whole of what the keeper said about the keeper's son having decided.

Pellam's girl went in the morning on the same boat, because the calm would not hold and she had a line to carry. She stood at the gate with her bag that was mostly cloak and said the line once more so that he would have heard it twice, *a door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time*, and he gave her the way once more so that she would have it, *right side, past the ninth, standing*, and that was the whole of what the two records had between them, and neither of them had a name, and the boat went.

He went down the right side that evening with the lamp, past the notches, past his grandmother, to the open door, and raised the lamp.

Standing. Where it had been. The grey case on the floor against the wall, inside the door, doing whatever the strangers' things did, which from outside was nothing. He stood in the doorway for as long as it takes to bar the gate, and it stood, and he lowered the lamp and it was gone, and he raised it and it was there, and he understood that he could do that all night and it would not change, and that this was the first thing in the Castle he had ever been able to do twice and have come out the same, and that it told him nothing at all.

Nobody has walked to the end of the Castle. He had gone one door further than anyone and had found a room with one standing in it, and the hall went on past the open door, down, in the dark, the way it always had, and he did not go on.

He went up, and barred the door, and the count was one more.
