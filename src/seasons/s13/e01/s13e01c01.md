::::: scene 1

::: pov wren-pellam

The first thing anyone from Ostrel tells you about Casimor is that nobody goes there at the start of a storm season, and the second thing is that the boat leaves at first light.

Wren had the first names by heart. That was what she had, crossing: a bag that was mostly cloak, a letter from her father in the form the court requires and nobody reads, and the chronicle's first names, learned on Vellamar from a reciter who had never once seen the Castle and said so, and said that this was the ordinary way. You learn the names on Vellamar. You learn what they are *of* on Krilzat. Every house sends its youngest, every generation, so that when the next coronation comes there is somebody under the Grove who can say the chronicle through from the first name without being prompted, and a house that cannot has let its part of the record go, and Pellam, which is a Knight's house and presides over nothing and administers less, had never once let it go.

So it was Wren, because she was the youngest, and because Knight Pellam had said, in the voice he used for the one thing their house still had, that she was the one who could hold a list.

Casimor came up out of the weather grey and low, and the boat went round to the lee side, because nothing lands on Krilzat. She walked across. It took the rest of the day. The island leaned; everything on the islands leans, every tree, every roof, every wall that has stood more than a season, bent the one way the wind comes from, so that a child learns which way is windward before she learns her letters. On Krilzat there were no trees to lean. There was rock, and wind, and at the far end of the rock, where the ground gave up, the watch-post.

And inside the watch-post's wall, standing up out of it like something the wall had been built to hold rather than to keep out, the Castle.

It did not lean.

Wren stopped on the path with the wind at her back trying to put her down, and looked at it, and understood for the first time what the reciter had meant, saying *you learn what they are of on Krilzat*. It was grey, and it was smooth, and it had no windows, and it went up into the weather and did not lean, and nothing she had ever seen in fifteen years on four islands had not leaned.

The chronicle said the founding monarch was given it. She had always taken that for a way of saying *built*. Standing on the path, she was no longer sure it was.

::::: scene 2

::: pov bram-tollick

Bram had the bar of the door in his hands when she came up the last of the path, which was where he had the bar most days, and he did not put it down, because the storm was two hours off and the door is barred before a storm, not after.

"Pellam's," said his father, from the wall, and that was all his father said, and it was all that was needed. A Knight's house's youngest, sent to learn the chronicle; the post had had one every generation since there had been a post, and Bram's father had had one in his own time and Bram's grandmother in hers, and none of them, as far as the watch could tell, had ever come back after. You learned the names. You went home. You said them under the Grove when the time came. The watch kept the door.

She was his age or a little more, soaked, with a bag that was mostly cloak, and she was looking past him at the Castle the way they all looked at it, the way he had stopped looking at it before he could remember.

"It doesn't lean," she said.

"No."

"Everything leans."

"Not that." He shifted the bar. "Come in before the wall gets it. You can look at it from inside; it looks the same from inside."

That was not true, and he knew it was not true, and said it anyway, because it was what the watch said to the court's children when they arrived, and the court's children always found out for themselves on the first evening, and his father said that finding out was the point.

Here is what the watch is. The post holds the Sergeant tier; it is not a house, it has no title, it is filled the way the Sergeant tier has always been filled, which is that somebody does the work and their children learn it, and the Kingdom honours it above almost everything that is not a house, and the work is the door. One door. The old keep inside the watch's wall has one, and the watch has kept it since before the chronicle's count of kings begins, which the court knows and the watch does not say, because the watch keeps no chronicle. The watch keeps the door.

And a count. Bram showed her the door-post as they went in, because they always asked eventually and it was easier before: a post of old wood, grey as the keep, cut from top to bottom with notches, more than he could count, each one a room a keeper had stood in, cut on the day they stood in it. The last was his grandmother's. His father had cut none, and said that a keeper who has nothing to add has kept the count honest.

"How many rooms are there?" she said.

"Nobody knows."

"Has anyone gone to the end?"

"There isn't one," said Bram, and barred the door behind them, and the wind stopped as if a hand had been put over it.

::::: scene 3

::: pov wren-pellam

It did not look the same from inside.

The watch-post's own rooms were rooms: stone, and fire, and the smell of wet cloak, and the keeper's wife, who was called Tollick too and gave Wren bread without asking her name, because the court's children's names were the court's business and the post had its own. Then a passage, and a second door, old and plain, that Bram opened without a key, and then the keep.

The chronicle has a line for it, and Wren had said the line a hundred times on Vellamar and had not known what it meant: *and the hall is longer than the keep is wide.*

It was. She stood at the end of it with Bram's lamp and looked down a hall that went on past where the Castle's outer wall had to be, straight, grey, with doors in its sides all the same height and all shut, and the floor sloping, very slightly, down, so that the far end of it was below the ground she had walked across to get here, and it did not stop. The lamp stopped. The hall did not.

"The rooms are all the same," said Bram. "Same height. Same door. The founders' stone, the court calls it; we call it the old stone. It doesn't take a mark. You can't cut it, you can't chalk it, the notches are on the post because the post is wood." He said it the way the reciter had said the names, flat, a thing that was so. "There's no window in any of it. There's no stair that anyone has found. It goes down."

"How far?"

"Further than anyone has gone."

She thought about the chronicle, which says the founding monarch was given the Castle, and does not say by whom, and goes on at once to the first names, as if the names were the answer to the question the line had just declined to ask.

"The chronicle says," she began, and stopped, because he had put a hand up, not far, the way the reciter did when a name was coming.

"It's the hour," Bram said. "Stand still. Don't go toward it. They don't mind, but don't."

She did not know what *they* were. She had the names. She had never once, in all the learning, been told what the names were *of*, except that you found out on Krilzat.

Down the hall, at the far edge of the lamp's reach, where the light was thinnest and there should have been almost nothing to see, something was walking, and it was easier to see than the wall beside it.

::::: scene 4

::: pov bram-tollick

He had seen that one more than any of the others, because that one came at the evening hour, and the evening hour was when the door was barred and he had nothing to do but watch.

It walked the way it always walked. Up the hall from the dark end toward the door, on the left side, at a walking pace, the size of a person, and made of the light the way a person is made of what they are made of: not lit, made. It was the lamp's light, the colour of the lamp, and more of it than the lamp had to give at that distance, as if the hall had been saving what reached it and had put it all in one place. His father had taught him that first, before anything: bring no lamp and you will see nothing; bring one and look at the floor under it. There is nothing on the floor under it. It throws no shadow. A person has a floor under them.

It came to the fourth door on the left, and turned, the way it always turned, as if the fourth door were open, and went where the door was, and was not there.

Bram let his breath out. He had not held it on purpose; he never did, and it always went.

Beside him Pellam's girl had not moved. He had told her not to and she had not, which was better than the last one had managed, and when he looked at her she was not frightened. She had the face of somebody who has just been handed the end of a sentence.

"Aldous," she said.

"What?"

"That one. That's Aldous. The first name. *Aldous, who came first, who was given the Castle and gave it to the kingdom and went in.* That's the first line of the chronicle. That's him." She was looking at the fourth door on the left, which was shut, as it was always shut. "He went in. That's what the line means. He goes in. He's been going in every evening since the chronicle began and nobody told me, because nobody who told me had seen him."

Bram looked at the fourth door on the left. He had watched that one go into it four thousand times.

"It goes to the fourth door," he said, because it was the only thing he had that she did not. "Every evening. Left side. Fourth. Never the third, never the fifth. The one at the morning hour goes the other way, down, right side, all the way to where the lamp stops, and the one at midday stands at the second door for as long as it takes to bar the gate and then isn't. We don't have names. The watch doesn't keep names. We keep which way they go."

She was quiet for a while. The hall was quiet; it was always quiet, it was the one place on Krilzat with no wind in it.

"The second name is Mereth," she said. "*Mereth, who stood at the door.*"

"Midday," said Bram, and found that his voice had gone somewhere, and brought it back. "Second door. Stands."

::::: scene 5

::: pov wren-pellam

The storm came onto the outer works in the night and the watch-post took it the way it took everything, with a noise like the sea being dropped on a roof, and Wren lay in the keeper's wife's spare bed with the whole post shaking round her and listened for the keep.

It did not make a sound.

She had grown up in a Knight's house on Ostrel in a hall that creaked in a light wind, and she knew the sound of every building on four islands under weather, and the keep made none. The post shook; the wall sang; somewhere a slate went. Inside the wall, inside the post, the old stone stood in the storm the way it had stood on the path with the wind at her back, and did not lean, and did not speak, and the chronicle's first line for it is *and it did not lean*, and she had said that line a hundred times as if it were a way of saying *strong*.

It was not a way of saying anything. It was a thing that was so.

She lay in the dark with the names going by in order, the way they went when she could not sleep, and this time they had ways to go with them. Aldous, who came first: up the hall, left side, fourth door, evening. Mereth, who stood at the door: second door, midday, as long as it takes to bar the gate. The third name, Hallin, who the chronicle says *went down and was long*, and Bram had said, when she gave it him, *morning, right side, all the way to where the lamp stops*, and had gone quiet, and she had understood that the watch had been watching Hallin go down every morning for longer than the Kingdom had been counting kings and had never had a word for who it was that went.

And the court had had the word, and had said it under the Grove at every coronation, and had never once seen anyone go.

That was what you learned on Krilzat. Not the names. The names were Vellamar's. What you learned was that the chronicle was kept in the Castle's rooms because it was kept *about* them, and that a list of who came first was nothing without somebody who had watched them come, and that somebody watching had been nothing, all this time, without a list. Two halves. The court's and the watch's. She had been sent to learn her house's half and had found the other half holding a door.

Nobody has finished the Castle. The chronicle says so and the watch says so and they mean different things by it and both are true, and Wren lay in the storm and thought that she understood, now, why no generation had: because the keeping was the finishing, and it was not done, and it would not be done when she went home with the ways learned, and would not be done when Bram cut his first notch, and was not meant to be.

In the morning the storm had gone over and the hall was there, and so was the one who goes down.
