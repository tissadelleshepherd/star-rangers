The reciter came up the terrace steps at the midday office, which was wrong, and waited at the top of them until the office was over, which was right, and Oriel, who had been a novice for the length of a storm season, understood from the sisters' faces that this happened and that it did not happen often.

A reciter is a person who carries the chronicle. Oriel had grown up knowing that the way she knew the tide: the court kept its record by saying it, and the people who said it were reciters, and they came to the Abbey, her father had told her once, *when there's a new bit*, and she had thought he was joking. He had not been. The court cannot check itself. When a line is new, somebody carries it to the one record on the islands that is written down, and says it at the top of the steps, and the Abbey answers what the Accounting states.

This reciter was old and wet and had come from Ostrel by the first calm, and said the line standing, in the voice reciters use, which is not a speaking voice.

"*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

He stopped.

The Abbess, who had come out with the book, which was not the Accounting, waited.

"That is the line," the reciter said, in his own voice. "There is no name. Pellam's youngest carried it back and there was no name to carry. The court asks whether the Accounting has the room."

"The Accounting has what it has," said the Abbess. "Come in out of the wind and I will have it looked for." And then, turning, in the voice she used for the thing she wanted done rather than discussed: "Fenwick. The Castle's count. Charter hand. The right side."

Oriel had not known the Accounting had a count of the Castle. Nobody had said. She went in to find it, with the whole of the scriptorium watching a novice go to a shelf, and the corrector who had never told her name moved aside from it without a word, which Oriel understood, by now, to be the Abbey's way of saying *this is yours*.

::::: scene 2

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

Emma had heard the line from the oldest shelf and had written it down before the reciter had finished, in the reciter's words, with the reciter's pauses marked, because a chronicle line was said in a shape and the shape was part of what was said.

*A door was given.* She had the band for that already; it had sat at the bottom of her register for two years, under the Kingdom's word for *give*, which the court used of the Castle and of nothing else, and which she had asked about once and been told meant *give*. She did not move it.

What she noted, without a band, because it was not a reading, was the Abbess's answer. *The Accounting has what it has.* Not *yes* and not *no*. The Abbess had answered a court reciter at the top of the steps the way she answered the delegation, which was with the record and not with herself, and had then sent the new novice to a shelf Emma had never been let near, and said *the Castle's count* as if everyone had always known there was one.

Emma had not known there was one. She wrote that down too, under her own name, because a thing the delegation had not known for three years was a reading about the delegation and belonged in the file.

Then her reader chimed, once, the way it did when the relay had passed over and left something, and she opened it under the desk because the scriptorium did not care for the reader and she did not care to make it.

The instrument log. From Krilzat. Three weeks of it, in the delegation's flat format, from the unit de Courtney had asked for and the court had let go as far as a keeper decided, which Emma had thought at the time was the most the court would ever grant and had been right.

*Placed. Interior, floor level, against a wall. Optical channel: a standing figure, outline consistent with the population's, at lamp wavelength, present for 00:04:11. Thermal: nil. Sound: nil. Absent thereafter.* Then nothing for a day. Then: *Optical: standing figure, same position, lamp wavelength, 00:02:50. Absent thereafter.* Then nothing. Then once more, shorter.

Present for exactly as long as a lamp was in the room. Absent otherwise.

She read it three times, and did not file anything, and looked up to where the new novice was standing at a shelf with a loose page in her hands and the face of a person who has found a thing she was sent for and did not expect to.

::::: scene 3

::: pov oriel-fenwick

The Castle's count was a page like the island page: a list, in the hand, in the charter stratum where the ink had gone the colour of the sea. Rooms. The left side, and the right, each door a line, and beside most of the lines a name, and beside the name what the one in it did, in two or three words, the way the Accounting did everything.

She had learned to read the charter hand in a season, badly, the way you learn to walk on a terrace in the dark. She read down the right side with her finger not touching the page.

*The ninth door past, right side. A room. One standing in it: Orrin, who waits.*

And a date, in the Abbey's reckoning, which she could read now and which was before she was born, and before her mother was, and she did not go further back than that because the hand did not change and there was no way to tell from the hand how much further it went.

She stood at the shelf with the page and did the arithmetic the Abbess had taught her not to do, which was to put two records side by side in her head. The court said *a door was given, in Pellam's time*, this season, this storm, by a girl her own age who had carried the line across the water. The Accounting said *the ninth door past, right side, a room, one standing in it, Orrin, who waits*, in a hand nobody could date, on a page that had been on this shelf since before anyone now living on Aduine had been born.

The court said the door was new and the one in it had no name. The book said the door was always there and the one in it was Orrin.

She took the page to the Abbess, who was sitting with the reciter in the long room with the shutters open, and held it out, and the Abbess read it without taking it from her, which Oriel understood was so that the reciter could see that the Abbess had not touched it.

"The Accounting has it," the Abbess said to the reciter. "The ninth door past on the right side. A room, and one standing in it. It was always there."

The reciter said nothing for a moment, and then, carefully, in his own voice: "The chronicle says it was given."

"The chronicle may say so. The Accounting says it was there. Those are the two records, and they have said different things before, and neither of us has pressed it, and I do not expect the court to begin with a door." The Abbess looked at the page again, and Oriel saw her decide something. "The Accounting also has a name."

"The court has no name."

"Then the court has no name," said the Abbess, "and the Accounting has one, and you may carry back that the Accounting has one, and that the Abbey does not offer it, because it is the Accounting's and not the court's, and the court did not ask for it. The court asked whether the Accounting has the room. It has the room."

::::: scene 4

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

Emma filed that evening, at the compound, under the lamp, with the log open on one side and her own notes on the other and the band register between them, which was where it lived.

The fact first, as the delegation filed facts: *Delegation instrument, placed by the Kingdom's keeper at a location the court chose, interior, Krilzat. Optical return: a standing figure, outline consistent with the population's, at the wavelength of a hand lamp, present on three occasions for the duration the lamp was present and absent otherwise. Thermal nil. Sound nil.* Then the two Kingdom records, each under its own name, each in its own words, as given, and nothing joining them: the chronicle's line, from the reciter's mouth, with the pauses; the Accounting's entry, from the Abbess's mouth, with the name the Abbess had said once in the long room and had not offered the court, which Emma had written down because it had been said in her hearing and had not been said to her.

Then the readings. Three, because three was what she had and she did not have a fourth.

That it was an optics of the place: the old stone, a lamp, a room shaped to gather. She could not rule it out and did not think it was so.

That it was the thing the delegation's own word was for, *photographic*, Strozzi's rendering of the court's word for a likeness drawn in light by nothing, which was a rendering and not a reading, and sat at its own band, and she did not raise it by putting it beside a log.

That it was a thing the Kingdom's two records each had a name for, one of them *nothing* and one of them *Orrin, who waits*, and that the delegation's instrument had recorded a third thing, a wavelength and a duration, and that none of the three was a reading of either of the others, and that this was not a reading the delegation was equipped to make.

She filed all three at the lowest band the register admitted. She adopted no name.

Then she sat for a while with the one thing she had not written, because it was not a reading either. The Abbess had sent a sixteen-year-old to a shelf, and the sixteen-year-old had come back with a page that said the room had been there since before her mother was born, and had held it out, and the Abbess had read it without taking it, in front of the court's reciter, and had answered the question the court asked and not the one it had not. Emma had been on this world three years and had never once seen the two records put beside each other in one room, and it had been done by a novice with a loose page, and nobody had pressed anything, and the delegation's instrument had recorded a figure for four minutes and eleven seconds and had not been asked what it thought.

*It was always there.* She wrote that down as given, under the Abbess's name, and closed the file.

::::: scene 5

::: pov oriel-fenwick

The reciter went down the steps at the evening office with the room and without the name, and Oriel stood tideward at the back with the novices and did not know the words yet and had stopped pretending otherwise, and the tide came in over the catchment stones the way it had come in on her first day, and nothing anyone said made it come.

Afterwards the Abbess did not go to the book. She stood at the top of the steps with the page in her hand, the loose one, the Castle's count, and when Oriel came up she gave it to her.

"Copy it," the Abbess said. "The right side. Tonight, before the light goes. In the hand. The ninth past, the room, the one standing in it, the name. You will not get it right and you will do it again tomorrow."

"It's the same page the court asked about."

"It is."

"The court said the door was given this season. You said it was always there. Both of you said it in front of me." Oriel heard her own voice doing the thing it did, going at a thing straight because she did not yet know how to go round it, and did not stop. "Which is true?"

The Abbess looked at her for as long as it had taken, that first morning, to finish a line.

"The court keeps a record of what was given," she said. "The Abbey keeps a record of what is there. Those are different records, Fenwick, and they are kept by different people for different reasons, and they have never once been the same record and were never meant to be, and the Kingdom has lived between them for longer than either can count. The court will say under the Grove that a door was given in Pellam's time. The Accounting will say the room was there. A girl carried the one line across the water and you carried the other page across a room, and you are both right, and neither of you has finished anything, and that is what a record is."

"And the Ranger wrote both down."

"The Ranger writes down what she is told. She was told two things. She will have written two things." The Abbess turned to go in. "Copy the page. Leave the column empty."

Oriel went in, and sat at the practice desk with the shutters still open and the light going grey off the water, and ruled the column, and left it empty, and wrote the ninth door past, and the room, and the one standing in it, and the name, in the hand, as it was written, so that nobody would be able to tell hers from the one that first wrote it, and got it wrong, and would do it again tomorrow.

Three records. One room. A door that was given and was always there and had someone standing in it for exactly as long as somebody held up a lamp.

She did not pick one. She was learning not to.
