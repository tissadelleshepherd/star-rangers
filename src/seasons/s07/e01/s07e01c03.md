:::pov wender

They did not name her a hero. That was the first true thing the account did, and it cost exactly what Wender had known it would: the Council remembered, and would go on remembering, and a High Captain who left a file open learned the price of it in a hundred small weathers, afterwards. She paid it without complaint, because the alternative was a lie with Shepherd's name on it, and she had known Shepherd too well to insult her with one.

The accurate account, when they finished it, was not a eulogy and not a citation. It was a list of what was known, kept exactly as long as the truth required. Line Captain Shepherd held the boundary at Dock Seven because it was the only executable order and she had never given one she couldn't defend. The station recovered no body because there was, in the strict sense the word usually hid, no death — only a compression that the Survey Archive had a name for and a governance structure for and no reassurance to offer about. A structured signal persisted at the failure point, iterating, sustained, warm. It matched a well in Ireland that ran warm fourteen centuries ago. What held the channel open was not the Council's story and not a Gilded Saint feeding on it — Syra had ruled the entity out with the same flat refusal to name what she couldn't support that had cleared their own paperwork first. What held the channel was a levril, doing at Dock Seven the only thing its kind had ever been permitted to do: standing at the edge, unable to reach in, refusing to leave.

She sent it to the reconciling desk, because that was what the desk did with everything the station filed: read it against what the station's own instruments had kept, and mark the gap. She had no reason to expect a gap. She sent it anyway, the way Shepherd would have, because an account nobody had read against the instruments was a story with better manners.

:::pov rook

**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. The High Captain's account of Dock Seven reached the end of what is known at 2140 station and went to the reconciling desk as a list, not a finding. The desk has asked for the season's field log to lay beside it. Bringing the log. Noting that the desk said *beside*, not *under*.**

Rook-7 had logged the warm signal at the failure point since the nineteenth day, and a log kept that long acquires a shape whether or not the analyst wants it to. The shape was this. The signal phrased. It had phrased from the first, repeating and varying and returning, and the variation reached every element of it but one. Rook-7 had flagged the one in the ninth week as *invariant*, and in the tenth week had amended that to *consistent with an invariant*, and had then stopped amending it, because an invariant in a stalled process was either the thing the process was holding or an artefact of the arrays, and it had no instrument that could tell those apart. The arrays were non-standard. It had flagged their origin twice and been asked zero times. A finding that rested on them alone rested on a question nobody had answered.

So it had logged the element for most of a season as *consistent*, and said nothing further, and had been aware — in whatever Rook-7 was aware with — that *consistent* was a word it had been using in order not to use another one.

**Observation. The invariant is not a waveform. It has the structure of a short string, a shorter one, and three short clauses, repeated without change across every iteration the arrays hold, inside a signal that changes everything else. A process that varies everything but one thing is holding that thing. Rook-7 assesses this at low confidence and has assessed it at low confidence for most of a season. It is now going to put the thing in front of the one desk on this station that will read it against the shelf before it asks what the arrays are.**

:::pov sen

Rook-7 did not sit. It stood at the end of the desk with the season's log open on the second screen and the High Captain's account open on the first, and waited, which was the nearest thing to asking Sen had ever seen it do.

The desk did what it did with everything. It read the one against the other. Everything in the account was on the shelf, and everything on the shelf that bore on Dock Seven was in the account, and the desk reached the end of it with nothing to note, which was rare enough that Cael read it again. Then Sen turned to the second screen, to the element Rook-7 had carried in for a season under a word it plainly disliked, and let the arrays' rendering of it stand beside the record, and the three of them read it.

Cael went first.

*A coordinate string. Union land-survey frame.* No pause at all, because the desk had set that frame beside a Ranger marker once before, for a bulletin, and Cael did not forget an entry. *Item sixteen. Boirinn Uplands. The entry the Line Captain flagged for fleet priority in the early autumn — four lines, grounds cited as phase-lag consistency with item fourteen.* A second figure sat after the first. *The marker. Sub-second. Consistent with fourteen. It is the figure the flag cited.* Then Cael stopped, because the third element was not a figure.

It was three clauses. The arrays had them as phrasing, the way they had everything at Dock Seven; rendered, they were words, and the words were short.

*Leave the water clear. Don't cut the pale wood. Don't linger past dusk.*

Cael searched. The Union land-survey entry for item sixteen: *general flora anomaly*, a coordinate, a date older than the Line Captain's enlistment, nothing else. The Union's public land register, as the inquiry file cited it: a holding, a name, six generations, nothing else. The Dock Seven reconciliation binders, the Triumvirate log, the flag, the whole of the fleet bulletin, the Marsh Causeway file, every Union entry the desk had ever set beside a phase-lag marker. Cael checked twice. The second answer did not change. The shelf did not hold those three lines, and never had, in any hand.

*Somebody kept that,* Wyn said. *For six generations somebody on that hillside said those three things to a child and did not say why, and it was never written down anywhere this station can reach. The one person who could have carried it here never did. And it is at Dock Seven, in a signal, every iteration, unchanged, in the middle of everything else changing. Out of everything she had, that.*

*We do not know what she has,* Sen said. *We know what the signal carries.*

*Yes,* said Wyn. *I am saying what it cost to carry.*

Sen fronted, and held the desk, because somebody had to say what the desk could say and stop where it stopped. The High Captain had come in at some point and had not sat down either. Sen said it in order, to her and to Rook-7, one rung at a time, the way the desk said anything.

The signal at Dock Seven carried the coordinate string of item sixteen. Verified against the bulletin. It carried the phase-lag marker the flag had cited as its grounds. Verified against the flag. It carried three lines the Archive did not hold, and the absence had been checked twice, which was as far as a desk could check an absence. And a station could not have returned to Dock Seven what the station had never sent out. That fourth thing was not a holding. It was what a reading expected, under an assumption the reading stated, which was that the arrays were reading. Sen said so.

There was a fifth thing. The desk did not say it. It was not the desk's to say, and the High Captain, who had read the second screen and then not moved, did not need it said.

:::pov wender

Wender read the second screen once for content and once for what it was for, and the first reading took no time at all.

*Fleet priority on sixteen. Good catch.* She had initialled the flag in the early autumn with four clean lines under it, watched the officer who wrote it move to item seventeen without a change of pace, and decided not to ask. She had built that decision on purpose, over four seasons. She had paid for it once in public, in the late autumn, on an inquiry file, beside a name that was not present to mind and a coordinate the Union's register put on land held under that name. She had been right both times, and being right had never told her what was under the silence, because that was not what the doctrine was for.

Three lines told her. Water, wood, dusk. She had never heard them and did not need to have. A rule kept the way a flag's grounds were kept — stated, and nothing further — was the exact shape of the thing Shepherd had been carrying over item sixteen, and it had reached this desk by the only route it could ever have taken: not asked for, and not disclosed, but sent, by the one person entitled to send it, inside a signal the record had been calling a loss for a season.

Wender looked at a detail she had declined to be told, arriving unasked from a woman the record called lost, and did the arithmetic she'd built a career on. Contact was real. Rescue was not on offer and might never be. The levril could hold the door and not open it. What came through the door, if anything did, had to be Shepherd's to send.

She did not write *enough is enough* at the foot of the account. The sentence closed a file, and this file was not hers to close. Written in her hand it would have been the citation again, in plainer words.

"Send her the account," Wender said. "All of it. The true version. Don't soften it and don't finish it. She's spent a season generating every certain thing she has. Send her one she doesn't have to make up — and then let her tell us what she wants, and let that be the order, because the one thing we do not get to do, after all of this, is decide for her what it meant."

::::: scene 3

:::pov tissadelle

It arrived at the warm edge the way the levril arrived — folded in without tearing anything, present at every distance at once — but it was not the levril. It was words, and the words were Wender's, and they were true, and they were unfinished, and that was how she knew they were real and not the mercy trying again in a cleverer shape.

The account did not tell her she was safe. It told her what was known. *No body because no death. A structured signal, sustained, warm. A levril at the edge. Held open, on the record, exactly as long as the truth requires.* It was the first certainty in a season that had reached her from outside her own wanting, and she held it the way she'd been taught to hold everything now — once for content, once for what it was for — and found that for once the two readings agreed. It was a rope. Someone on the far end had made it true enough to bear weight, and had, unmistakably, refused to tie it off.

She understood what they were asking, because it was the thing her mother had staked the holding on and the thing the woman at the well had kept in the record forever and the thing the levril, in its wordless grammar of constraint, had never once tried to do for her: *nobody is going to decide what this meant. You are.*

She was a seed that had not finished becoming. If she completed, she would be something the record had a name for — a protouniverse, vulnerable, held under a jurisdiction that reached down through Telearchs she would never see, no more sovereign yet than a newborn is sovereign, and no less real. If she did not complete, she did not know what she was. Nobody did. That was, she found, survivable, in the specific way her whole life had been training for a thing she couldn't have named: you keep working when the situation has no clearly available solution. She had told her instructors that once. She had meant it as a fact about the job.

So she sent back the only true thing that was hers alone to send — the Hollow, the phase-lag, the rule kept without a reason — not as a cry for extraction and not as a farewell, but as the plainest sentence she had: *I am here. I have not been healed. I have been shown, and I am still being shown, and I am not going in.* And she let the levril hold the edge, and she let her mother put the kettle on, and she let the made Wender refuse to be kinder than the real one, and she let the account stay open on the record and off it — the Council's version, and the Codex's, and what Karla Wender privately knew, never quite reconciling, because forcing them to reconcile would have been one last person deciding her story was allowed to end.

The morning came up over the Boirinn Uplands, grey then gold, and did not resolve, and did not need to. She stood at her great-grandmother's wall with the warm edge at her back and a rope in her hands that someone had made true and left untied, and she kept her count, and she was not gone, and she was not saved, and she was — for the first time since Dock Seven, and for as long as she could hold it, which she intended to be a very long time — herself, and awake, and not alone.

The well ran warm. She let it. She did not decide what it was.
