::: pov aravena

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. This time nothing else was, by order, and so what follows is in four people, and in nothing that hums.

Aravena knocked.

Shift two-three-four. The stone, the size of two hands, picked at the junction without looking, the way you pick a thing your hand knows. The squeeze, sideways, the stone turned lengthwise, the handheld's hard square against the breastbone, its sound off. The silt. The one on the line, in the open, still, grey, large. Beside it, nothing, and Aravena looked, and did not file the looking.

Kneel. Set it down, a hand from those feet. Step back. Say the thing, going up at the end.

Wait.

The one on the line bent, the way it bent every shift, and put a hand on the rock at the edge of the silt.

And did not move it.

Aravena waited the interval. The party had measured it from the other side a hundred times before anyone knew what it was, and Aravena had stood inside it on more shifts than Aravena had counted, and it had a place in the body now, the way a step you take every day has a place in the leg: a point at which you turn, because by then the rock has always spoken. The point came. The light lay low on the silt, on the feet, on the rock, on the hand on the rock. The hand had not moved. The point went by.

The order was short and Aravena knew it the way you know a thing you have kept two hundred times. *Set it down. Step back. Wait the interval. Whatever happens in it, or does not, come back.* It had been given at the station on the morning of the first stone, and nobody had touched it since, and Aravena had never once wanted to.

Aravena stood on the silt and thought it through once, because there was nobody else there to.

The interval was for the stone. That was all it had ever been for. The party had measured it before it knew it was an answer, and it was the time an answer took, and the answer had come inside it every time because every time it had been begun at once. This time it had been begun. Aravena could see it begun: a hand, on a rock, at the edge of the light. It had not been finished. A hand on a stone is a stone that is going to move. You do not leave a door while somebody's hand is on it.

So Aravena stayed.

Not deciding to. By the time Aravena noticed the staying it had been done for a while, which is how Aravena had decided most things on this silt, and had once said so to Ferrant, and Ferrant had written it down.

Then the one on the line said something.

Not the ground. Not a rock. A sound came from the one on the line, low, not loud, and it had a shape, the way a voice has a shape and a rise does not. Aravena's own thing, said at the end of the knock, was one shape going up. This had a great many, going up and down and along, one after another, and it did not stop at the end of the first of them, or the tenth.

It was said to Aravena. The light was low and Aravena did not lift it, and did not need to. A thing said at you from an arm's length is said at you.

Aravena did not know one word of it, and knew it was being said, and stood for it the way the one on the line stood on every shift, which Aravena had watched for two years and never once done: with the whole body still, so that nothing Aravena did would get into it by mistake. The breath came back from the squeeze and went ordinary. The light stayed where it was. The handheld pressed its square into the breastbone and kept whatever it kept, pointed at a hand on a rock.

And it went on.

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa had told the unit the channel was off, and the time, and the unit had hummed once and gone quiet, and Aravena had gone down the third passage with the light held low, as on every shift since the order.

Ferrant was at the junction. She had taken to coming down on some shifts to countersign at the junction instead of at the table, which she said was the difference between a gap you had stood in and one you had read about, and nobody had argued, because it was the kind of thing she was right about. She stood at the edge of the light with the handheld's feed on the junction screen and her hands empty. Egede stood where Egede stood. Nakagawa stood by the unit, with the timepiece running, because the column needed the interval.

The rise came up the third passage as it always did now, a shape with nothing in it, and reached the junction, and the unit on its base heard it and had nowhere to put it.

Then the wait.

Nakagawa knew the interval without the timepiece. Everyone at the junction did; it was in the boots. The point came where the floor had always moved. The floor did not move. The point went by, and Egede shifted weight, and nobody said anything, and the third passage stayed dark where the light should have been coming back up it.

"The order says come back," said Egede, quietly. It was not an objection. It was the instrument specialist reading the one instrument at the junction that still had a channel open, which was the file.

"Yes."

"Aravena isn't coming back."

"No."

Then the sound.

It came up the third passage and through the crack the way the rise came, and it was not the rise. It was lower. It was longer. The crack took the words, if there were words, and kept the shape, and the shape went on: up and down and along, one piece after another, with gaps in it the length of a breath and then more of it, and it reached the junction and stood in the fungus-light with the three of them, and the unit on its base had no channel open for it, and nothing on the roof did.

Egede looked at the unit. Then at Nakagawa. And said nothing, because the objections had all been had, in order, on the night of the argument, and were in the file, and this was the thing they had been about, and Egede knew it, and did not say so, which Nakagawa noticed and was not going to forget.

Nakagawa could have gone down. The thought came and Nakagawa had it properly, standing still, the way you have a thought you are going to decide against. One person at a door is a knock. Two is something else, and the people below had a word for it, and the party had decided two years ago not to find out what. The order had been written for a person at a door with nothing happening on the other side of it. It had not been written for this, because nobody had known what this would be. And the one person on the planet who could read the order against the thing in front of it was standing on the silt with a light held low, and Nakagawa was not, and that was where the decision was.

Nakagawa left it there.

The timepiece ran. Nakagawa did not look at it. The column would need the number and the number would be whatever it was.

::: pov odile-ferrant

Ferrant watched the frame.

It was her instrument and she had asked for the feed, three seasons ago, and had watched it every shift she came down, and it had shown her the same thing every time: silt, two feet, a rock, a hand coming down to the rock, the rock moving, the frame turning away. Today it showed silt, two feet, a rock, and a hand on the rock, and then it showed that, and then it went on showing it.

She had watched an instrument hold a frame before. Every station she had served on had one that would sit on its shelf and say *nominal* to itself for a year, and she had learned to be careful of exactly that: the quiet one, the one that works. This was not that. This was a frame holding because the thing in it was holding, and she knew the difference, and could not have written down how.

Above the top edge of the frame, now and then, there was movement. Grey. The handheld was pointed low, by order, and what it could see of the one on the line was the feet and the hand, and above that a grey that shifted the way a body shifts when a voice is coming out of it, and the optical made nothing of that and neither did she. She did not try. It was not boundary data. For once she did not even reach for *consistent with*, because there was nothing on her page it was consistent with and she was not going to pretend one.

What she did instead was take out her own timepiece and start it.

Nobody had asked her to. The survey lead had one running. But she had said, on the night of the argument, that a gap must have a name on it and an interval, and she was going to countersign this one, and she found she wanted the number to be one she had stood through and not one she had been handed.

The sound came up the passage. It stood in the junction with them. She had heard the rise from here once, from the edge of the light, and called it *the shape of it*, and been proud of the phrase. This was not a shape she had a word for. It had too many pieces.

A boot keeps nothing. She had said that too, that night, as the cost. Standing in the cost, with a frame of a hand on a rock on a screen she was not going to be able to file and a sound in the room she was not going to be able to grade, she found that a boot was the whole of what she had brought down the passage, and that nobody had asked her for anything else, and she stood in it.

It was the first thing on this planet she had been present for and had nothing to say about. She did not want a page.

::: pov aravena

It stopped.

Not the way a thing stops when it is interrupted. The other way. Aravena had never heard the one on the line say anything and knew the difference anyway, the way you know the end of a passage by the air changing before the light does. There was a quiet after it, and the quiet was the kind at the end of something.

Then the hand moved the rock.

Once. A good one. The ground said so, through the silt and the boots, the way it had said it on every shift since the first stone, and Aravena heard it and understood exactly as much as on the first day, which was that it was an answer, and that it was to Aravena. And one thing more, that had not been true on the first day or any day since: that the stone had come at the end.

Not at the beginning. At the end.

Aravena stood for as long as it takes to say a name. Then went back through the crack, slowly, with the light held low, not scraping more than they had to, and did not run, and it was not the hardest thing Aravena had done at the line. The hardest thing had been staying. And that had not been hard either, which Aravena would not be able to explain and was not going to be asked to.

The junction. The fungus-light, the unit on its base, and three people standing in it who had been standing in it for a long time, by their faces. Nakagawa knelt to the unit and told it the channel was on, and the time, and the unit hummed.

Aravena knelt.

"Log. Shift two-three-four. Upper junction. Party of three and one. Knock answered."

The unit hummed. It had heard that. It had heard nothing else, and would not.

Aravena stayed kneeling. "Something was said to me at the line. By the one who answers. For a long time. I don't know what. I stayed for it. Then the stone."

The unit hummed again, low, once, which was all it had.

"Tell me," said Nakagawa. "In order."

Aravena told it in order. The stone, a hand from those feet. The step back. The thing said. The hand going down to the rock and staying there. The interval, and the point in it where you turn, going by. The light low on a hand that had not moved. The staying. The voice, if it was a voice; it was a voice. How long, which Aravena could not say and did not guess at. The stop. The rock. The going.

"The order says come back," said Nakagawa.

"The order says wait the interval. I waited it." Aravena had thought this through once on the silt and had not thought about it since, and it came out the way it had gone in. "The interval's for the stone. The stone hadn't come. It was coming. The hand was on it. You don't walk away from a door while somebody's hand is on it."

Nakagawa was quiet for as long as it takes to say a name.

"That's a reading."

"It's mine."

"Then it goes in under your name, and I am not going to rewrite an order standing up on the day a thing happened. If it needs rewriting it will look the same in the morning, and it will be rewritten then, in the file, with the reason." A pause. "It may not need it. The interval may always have been for the stone. The order never said what it was for."

"What did it sound like?" said Egede, who had been going to ask since the junction had first heard it, and had waited, which was not a thing Egede was known for.

Aravena thought about it, because Egede had waited.

"Like somebody telling you something," said Aravena. "That's all. Like somebody telling you something you don't know yet."

Egede made the sound Egede makes when a thing is going to end up in a document, and did not reach for anything to put it in, because there was nothing at the junction to put it in, and Egede knew that better than anyone on the roof, and stood there knowing it.

Nakagawa wrote the log at the junction, standing up, in the survey lead's own hand, because this one was not Aravena's to write, and the unit had been told nothing it could hold.

*Party announced itself at the line: one member, one stone of the upper levels, set down and left; handheld carried, pointed low, sound channel off. The one who answers was present, in the open, and put a hand on the rock at the edge of the silt and did not move it. The one who knocked stayed past the interval, by the one who knocked's own reading, entered under that name. Something was then said at the line by the one who answers, to the one who knocked, and went on, and ended, and the rock was then moved once. Nothing said is recorded. Nothing on the roof could have recorded it. The line was not crossed by any party.*

Under it, in the survey lead's own column: *Sound channel at the junction off for the knock, by order. Interval: forty-four minutes.* The longest number the column had ever carried, by a factor the column had never needed, and the only measure the roof would ever hold of how long the thing said had been.

Ferrant came and read it, from the edge of the light, as she did, and wrote her line under it. *Countersigned. Interval as stated. Nothing to grade.*

Then she did not go.

"One more thing," she said. "And it isn't advice. You're going to write who heard it."

"Yes."

"Put me on it."

Nakagawa looked up.

"I heard it. I was there. I've no page for it and I'm not going to pretend one, and I'm not going to write a word of it in my own file, because there's nothing in it my file is entitled to. But that line is the only true thing anybody on this roof can write down about what happened today, and I want my name on it where it belongs." She put the timepiece away. "Forty-four minutes. I kept it too. It agrees."

So the last line of the entry, in Nakagawa's hand, under the column, said: *Heard by Aravena, at the line. Heard from the junction by Egede, Ferrant, Nakagawa.*

Egede read it over Nakagawa's shoulder, which Egede did not usually do, and said nothing, and then said, "That's the entry."

"That's the entry."

"That's all of it."

"That is all of it that is ours," said Nakagawa, "and it is a great deal."

::: pov nakagawa

That evening Nakagawa read the unit's channels at the station, as on every evening for four years. Thermal. Imager: one upright shape on the line, one return, a hand from the stone. Sound channel.

*Anstolik, passing.* Two.

Nothing else, and tonight *nothing* meant forty-four minutes, and Nakagawa read it twice, to be sure that was what it was, and it was.

Then the wind, and the others asleep, and the third shelf humming very slightly in the dark.

Four years ago the party had walked into a place where a people had been knocking the whole time, and had not heard it, because the instrument that heard it filed it as a rock. Two years ago it had learned to knock back, and had been answered, every shift, by a stone. Today it had been answered by the thing itself, whatever the thing was, at length, in a voice, to one person, and the whole of the survey's record of it was that it had happened, and for how long, and who had been there. Nakagawa had never signed a thinner entry. Nakagawa could not think of one that held more.

What was said, the party did not know, and the log said so. Whether the party would ever know, the log did not say, because the survey does not file what it cannot see the shape of. What the people below had made of a small up-person standing still for forty-four minutes on the silt with a light held low, Nakagawa did not know either, and that was not a planetary survey's to know, and the log said that too.

In the morning Aravena would pick a stone, and carry it down, and say the thing, and the one on the line would put a hand on the rock, and Nakagawa did not know whether the stone would come at the beginning or the end, and found, standing at the window with a timepiece in a pocket, that this was the first thing in four years on Fliade the survey lead had not needed to know in advance, and slept on it.
