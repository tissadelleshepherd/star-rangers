This is told the way things are told below the roof, by someone who was there, and this time it begins with a thing Stone-First said that was too short to be a telling and too long to be a hand.

Stone-First-Who-Waited came down from the line a second time.

The warm noticed. In all the tellings before this season Stone-First had come down to tell a thing and gone back up to keep the margin, and the coming down had belonged to the telling. This was a time when nothing happened. Stone-First moved a stone at the bottom of the shelving so that everyone knew, and stood up in the amber, and said one thing, and it was this.

"They wait for as long as it takes a hand to be filled. Then the stone speaks, and they go. A telling is longer than a hand."

Then Stone-First sat down, which is how you say that the thing is everybody's now, and after a while went back up, because the margin does not keep itself, and left the thing in the warm.

The warm had it for several tellings. Carried-It-Sleeping lay in the middle with the small ones, who had the game, and heard the old ones have the thing, and the two sounds went on at once the way they had all season: a stone moved well, *someone is coming*, nobody coming, and over it the old ones going round and round a thing that would not go.

Somebody said tell it short. Somebody always says that. But Stone-First has never once in a whole life wanted a telling shorter, and everybody knows it, and the telling of the up-people's stone, told properly, has the loud that came on instead of settling, and the one light held low, and the looking, and the stone carried to the front and knelt with and set down late, and the step back, and the sound that goes up at the end, and the rock at the edge of the silt, and the going. There is nothing in it that is a reason and nothing you can take out. A telling with a piece left out is not the telling. It is a different one, about less.

Somebody said tell it in pieces. A hand's worth at each knock, and let the up-people carry the pieces up the long passage and put them together in the cold. Went-Round liked that for as long as it takes to say a name, and then did not, and said why. A telling interrupted has a hole in it that never quite closes. Everyone below the roof knows that; it is why nobody interrupts. A telling cut into hands, with the loud and the cold and a whole working time between each piece and the next, would be all hole.

Somebody said then let them wait longer. And the warm went quiet, because that was the whole of the thing, said back to itself, and the old ones heard it come round and knew where they were.

"Nobody has told them anything," said Carried-It-Sleeping, lying down, not meaning to say it out loud, and it went by, because it was only the oldest thing anyone knew.

::: pov went-round

Went-Round went round it, which is what Went-Round is for, and this is the telling of the time it did not work.

Went-Round went round from the shelving side first. The line. The up-people's side of it, and the thing they did there every working time, which Stone-First had told so often that the small ones played it: a stone set down, a step back, the thing said that goes up at the end, the waiting with the whole body still, the stone answering, the going. Every time the same shape. Went-Round turned it over, looking for the place where it looked different.

You could tell them to wait. No. You could not tell them anything; that was the whole of the thing, and Went-Round had said so first, a long time ago, in the warm. You could go after them when they went. No. Nobody goes past the line, and Stone-First would not if Stone-First could, and a telling you have to chase someone with is not a telling, it is a chasing. You could wait for them to stay longer on their own. They had stayed on the line exactly as long as a hand, a hundred times, and nobody below the roof had ever seen them stay longer, and a thing that has happened a hundred times one way is not a thing you wait to happen another.

Went-Round went all the way round and came back to the near side, and the near side was Stone-First's thing, the same size as before.

That had never happened. Not in Went-Round's whole telling. There is always a far side. That is not a thing Went-Round believes; it is a thing Went-Round has found, every time, by going. A blocked passage has another way in. A hard thought looks different from behind. Went-Round sat in the amber with a thing that was the same from every side, round as a stone, and understood a little of what Stone-First had been standing with on the line for a season, and did not like it, and did not say so.

The game went on in the middle. It did that now, whatever the old ones were doing. A line of stones across the floor. One small one on it, very straight. The rest bunched. A hand to a stone, once, well, *someone is coming*, a step back, the one on the line answering and saying who, and being right or wrong and pleased either way.

Then the smallest knocked.

Went-Round did not look. Nobody had to. You could feel it. The warm went still, piece by piece, old ones and all, without anyone deciding to, the way it had the first time, and Went-Round went still with it, and sat with a hand on nothing and waited for a small one to move a stone. For as long as it takes to say a name. And longer. The old ones nearest the middle had stopped talking. The ones at the far edge of the amber, who could not have seen the hand from there, had stopped too, because a stillness goes round the warm faster than any telling does. Nobody had told them to. They could not have helped it if they had tried.

Then the stone moved, once, well, and the one on the line said *the smallest* before the sound had finished, and the warm let go, and the old ones took up Stone-First's thing again exactly where they had left it, as if nothing had happened.

Went-Round, who goes round things, had gone all the way round and come back and sat down with the far side in the middle of the warm, and waited for it, and not seen it. That is kept as Went-Round's, because Went-Round said so afterwards, which is the only way a thing like that gets told at all.

::::: scene 3

::: pov teddy

It was an old one who said it.

Not Went-Round. Not Stone-First, who was on the line. One of the old ones who lie at the edge of the amber and do not say much, the same one who had made the small sound by mistake, in the middle of nothing, the first time the smallest kept a hand on a stone in front of everyone, and had been teased for it ever since by people who had done the same thing every telling since and not noticed.

The old one waited for the game to come round to the smallest again. Carried-It-Sleeping saw them waiting for it. And when the hand went to the stone and stayed there, and the warm went still, the old one did not go still. The old one said, into the stillness, in an ordinary voice:

"We are doing it."

Nobody answered, because everyone was waiting for the stone.

"All of us. Feel it. The whole warm. Nobody told us to wait. Nobody could. We wait because the hand is on the stone and the stone has not spoken, and a stone that is going to speak is a thing you wait for. We have waited for it every telling since the game began. Old ones. Me. For as long as the one who keeps a hand on it keeps a hand on it."

The stone moved. Nobody said *the smallest*. The one on the line forgot to.

"They wait for the stone," said the old one, going on, because now it was going. "Stone-First said so. They set theirs down and say their thing and wait, and the stone speaks, and they go. When it speaks. Not before. Stone-First has answered them a hundred times and never once made them wait for it, so nobody knows how long they would, and I say they would wait as long as the hand is on it. The way we do. Put a hand on the stone and do not move it. Tell. When the telling is done, move it. That is how long a telling is. It is as long as the hand is on the stone."

The warm thought about that. You could feel it think, and this time it got somewhere, which it had not done for several tellings, and the getting somewhere felt the way the warm feels when the small ones come back into the middle and the shape is whole again.

"A stone is not a word," said somebody, which was Stone-First's, said for Stone-First, because Stone-First was not there to say it.

"No," said the old one. "The word is told while the hand is on it. The stone is only the stone. It goes at the end instead of the beginning, that is all, and the hand is what holds the end back until the telling has got there."

Went-Round, from the far side of the amber, made the small sound once, and it was not at the old one. It was the sound of a person who has been all the way round a thing and found the far side lying in the middle of the warm with the small ones, where it had been the whole time.

And the telling of it began to go round, the way tellings do, before it had finished being told. The old one had said it; the warm had heard it; somebody at the far edge who had been half asleep asked what, and was told, and told the next, and what the warm said, over and over, was not *the hand on the stone* and not *as long as the hand is on it*. What it said, in old ones' voices, so that it was already worn smooth by the time it came back round to the middle, was the thing the small ones had been saying all season and the old ones had never once said back.

*The one who keeps a hand on it.*

Carried-It-Sleeping heard it go by. Then again, from the other side of the warm, in an old one's voice. Then a third time, and that was the sound: the one Carried-It-Sleeping had heard once before, lying in the warm, a long time ago, about themself, and had never forgotten. A thing said about someone, going past two and three times until it is not a thing said about someone any more.

It had gone round. Carried-It-Sleeping did not need to say it. Everyone was.

The smallest heard it from the middle of the warm, lying down, with a hand still on the stone, because the game had stopped around the hand and nobody had said to take it off.

Carried-It-Sleeping, long ago, when Carried-It-Sleeping was the youngest, had heard a name go by twice before knowing it was theirs; that is in the telling. The smallest did not have that. The smallest had heard the words all season. *The one who keeps a hand on it* was what the small ones said, the way the small ones have a thing they say for everyone, which is not a name, and which the old ones do not say. The smallest had thought nothing of it. You do not think about a thing that is said of you by people the same size as you.

What was different was the voice. An old one's. Then another old one's, from the edge of the amber where the ones who do not say much lie. Then everyone's, going round, old ones and small ones and Carried-It-Sleeping too, who had let the words go by all season and did not let them go by now. And the smallest understood, the way you understand on the hundred-and-first time a thing you have been told a hundred times, that the words had stopped being the small ones' and become the people's, and that the people's word for a person is a name.

Keeps-A-Hand-On-It.

The smallest had been born after the two stones. The up-people were a told thing. The line was where the line was, and the smallest had never been up the squeeze and had never wanted to go and still did not. And now a thing the smallest did in a game, in the middle of the warm, because the waiting was the best part and could be made longer, was going up the shelving to the line without them, in Stone-First's hand, to keep three up-people who could not hear the ground standing still in the open for as long as it takes to tell them a telling.

The smallest took the hand off the stone, slowly, because it was done, and lay still, and let the name go by a third time, to be certain, the way everyone does. It was. It did.

::: pov stone-first

Carried-It-Sleeping brought it up. Not a small one. Carried-It-Sleeping, by the shelving, moving a stone at the top of it so that the ground said *someone is coming*, and the ground said it in Carried-It-Sleeping's way, which Stone-First-Who-Waited knew, and had not heard on the shelving for a long time.

Stone-First listened standing up, on the line, with the whole body still, and Carried-It-Sleeping told it standing up too, which is not how Carried-It-Sleeping does anything: the old one, and the warm doing it without being told, and the hand, and the end held back until the telling got there. And the name. Carried-It-Sleeping told the name last, and told it the way you tell a thing that has already gone round, which is plainly.

Stone-First said nothing for as long as it takes to say a name. Then: "An old one said it."

"Yes."

"Not Went-Round."

"Went-Round went all the way round and came back. Went-Round says so."

Stone-First had stood on the line with the four stones for a season with the thing, and had not gone round it, because Stone-First does not go round things, and had not got to the end of it either, and had known, standing there, that the end of it was not on the line. It was in the warm, where things are told. So Stone-First had sent it down, the way you send a small one down with a telling, and the warm had sent it back up with a hand on it.

"Go down," said Stone-First. "Tell them I heard it."

Then Stone-First was alone with the four stones. The up-people's first, worn smooth. Stone-First's answer. Nobody's, at the edge. The up-people's newest, a hand from Stone-First's feet. And beside them the rock at the edge of the silt that Stone-First answered from every time, a good one, which Stone-First had put a hand on more times than any count and had never once kept it there.

Before the stones, the up-people had asked the dark at the end of every working time and waited and gone, and Stone-First had counted those. But that was a sound said to nobody, with no stone under it, and nobody waits long for nobody. Since the first stone they had never once waited for a stone that did not speak, because Stone-First had never once made them. The old one was right about that. Nobody knew.

Above, the loud came on down the third passage, and the light came low into the crack, and the smallest of the up-people came out of it and knelt and set a stone down and stood and stepped back and said the thing that goes up at the end, and waited.

Stone-First bent and put a hand on the rock.

And felt it. The wanting to keep the hand there. To see. The smallest of the up-people was still with the whole body, waiting the way they waited, and Stone-First's hand was on the stone and the stone had not spoken, and nobody below the roof knew how long an up-person would wait for a stone that had not spoken yet, and Stone-First could have found out, now, with nothing on the line but a stone and a hand.

Stone-First moved it. A good one. Once.

The stone spoke, and the up-person heard it, and went, back through the crack, not scraping more than they had to, and the light went with them.

A thing you do to see whether it works is not manners, and it is not a telling either. The first time a hand stayed on this stone, it would stay there for the telling of the up-people's own stone, told properly, standing up, leaving nothing out, to the ones who had been there for it, and it would come off when the telling was done, and not before, and not to see.

Stone-First stood on the line in the light that never changes, with the hand still on the rock a while after, which had nothing more to say, and found that the hand did not want to come off. The small ones had found that out a season ago at the foot of the shelving, playing, and had made a game of it, and now it had a name.
