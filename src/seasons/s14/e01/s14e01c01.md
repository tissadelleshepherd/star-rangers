::: pov meenal-raghunath

There is no third shift on the hub. Meenal had been told that on her first day, by a tender who had been drawing glass in the weightless core for thirty years and had never once, by her own account, known what time it was. The cylinders keep a day. The hub keeps a draw.

So it was not night. It was the hour the cylinders called night, when the lifts came up empty and the long gallery held four people instead of forty, and the preform in tower three had six hours in the furnace behind it and six ahead, and the fibre came off the bottom of the tower at nine metres a second, so fine she could not see it, only the place where the light caught it.

That was the whole of the work: watch the place where the light caught it.

A preform is a rod of glass the width of a wrist. The furnace softens the tip, the tip is drawn down, and what comes off is a thread thinner than hair that keeps the rod's proportions exactly, core to cladding, all the way down. Under gravity the melt settles and the draw fights it. Here nothing settles, which is why Antariksha draws fibre, and why Meenal's job, most hours, was to be present while a machine did a thing that could not go wrong.

The monitor went amber at the two-hundred-metre mark.

Not red. Amber was an inclusion — a flaw too small to see and large enough to scatter light, which in a fibre that exists to carry light is the only flaw there is. One in a kilometre was within tolerance. The standard said to note it and continue.

She noted it. The thread ran on.

At the four-hundred-metre mark it went amber again.

She looked at that for a while. Then she laid the two positions beside each other, which was not a thing the standard asked for, and asked the tower for the interval, and sat with the number.

It was not the furnace, which held its heat to a tenth of a degree and had no cycle to speak of. It was not the feed, which was continuous, nor the spool, which took the thread up at a rate that had nothing to do with two hundred metres. It was not the cylinders turning, because the hub did not turn; that was the point of the hub. She checked anyway, because the number was close to something and she wanted to know what, and it was not that either.

At six hundred metres, amber.

She did not stop the draw. A fibre with an inclusion every two hundred metres is a fibre nobody will buy, and the standard for that was plain: let the preform run out, tag the whole length *reject, periodic inclusion, cause not identified*, and file the interval. She had always taken the *not identified* field for a courtesy to tenders who could not be bothered. She filled it in with more care than she had ever given a field in her life.

She did not write what she thought, because she did not think anything. She had a number, and the number matched nothing on the hub, and the thread kept coming off the tower straight and fine and full of light, with something in it every two hundred metres that the glass had not been given.

When the preform ran out she cut the length, and tagged it, and coiled the last fifty metres by hand because the spool had already taken the rest, and the coil was so light that when she let go of it in the gallery it stayed where she had put it, turning very slowly, catching the light at one point on its circumference and then another.

She put it in the reject bin, where it belonged, and went to find tea.

::: pov arjun-velloor

The reject log came to Arjun in the morning, which on the hub meant the hour the north cylinder's lifts started running full, and he read it the way he read it every morning: for the arithmetic.

Rejects were weight. A rejected length went back to the melt, the melt was accounted for by the gram, and his job was to make the grams agree with themselves before anyone asked why they did not. He had held the post four years. Nobody had asked.

Tower three, overnight. One full preform, tagged *periodic inclusion, cause not identified*, with the interval filed.

He checked the arithmetic, which was correct. Then, because the interval had been filled in to three decimal places and nobody filled that field in to three decimal places, he searched the log for it.

It had been filed before.

Not often. Nine times in the records the hub kept, which ran back further than anyone on the hub had worked there, and never twice by the same tender, and never on the same tower, and the interval was the same to the second decimal and drifted at the third in a way that could have been the instrument or could have been the thing. Every one of the nine had done exactly what the standard said. None of them had written a cause. Two had written *checked rotation, n/a*, which made him smile, because of course they had.

He laid the nine dates out beside each other, because he was a clerk and it was what he did with nine of anything.

They were not evenly spaced. He had half expected them to be, and was relieved, in a way he did not examine, that they were not. Whatever it was did not keep a schedule. It arrived, and someone competent noted it, and the length went back to the melt, and the grams agreed with themselves, and nobody told anybody, because there was nobody to tell and nothing to say. The standard had a field for it. The field had been used.

Arjun opened the note he kept for himself, which nobody had ever asked for, and wrote the nine dates in it, and the tenth, and the interval, and *cause not identified*, and under that, after some thought, nothing.

He went down to the gallery to see the length before it went to the melt. The bin had been emptied on schedule. Tower three was already feeding a new preform, straight and fine, with the light catching it at one point.

In the tender's locker, which she had left open the way everyone on the hub left them, there was a coil of fibre fifty metres long that weighed almost nothing, turning very slowly, which was not where the standard said it should be, and he did not write that down.
