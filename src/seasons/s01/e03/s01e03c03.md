::: pov galahad

He had brought the rest of the file because he had said he would, and because a man who discloses everything except one thing has not disclosed everything.

The outpost in daylight was smaller than it had any right to be. Chalk in jars, sorted by grain. Thread on a rack, by weight. Water in four vessels, and a written note beside each saying where it had been standing and for how long. Twelve years of reading other people's field records had taught Galahad to tell the difference between a person who kept order because they had been trained to and a person who kept order because disorder cost them something. This was the second kind.

Elvira read the last of the survey pages the way she had read the first of them: fast, without comment, going back twice.

"You had all of this in the third week," she said.

"I did."

"You could have brought it in the third week."

"I could have."

She let it go, which he had expected. It was not the thing she was annoyed about, and she was precise about her annoyances.

What she was annoyed about was on the table under her own hand, in her own writing, in the plain vocabulary she insisted on: *That should have been harder. Find out why it wasn't.*

Galahad had read it upside down when he came in, and had been holding the same thought since, and was not going to say it.

Because he knew one reason it might not have been harder. It was not a good reason. It was not evidence. It was a memory of a terrace garden at a house called Dún Rí, in 2806, when he was seventeen and had gone with his father on an errand about a boundary of the ordinary sort — a field line, a wall, an argument two families had been having politely for a century.

He had wandered off the terrace, because he had been seventeen. There had been a gate at the bottom of the garden and something wrong with the light beside it, and he had said so out loud, in the word his own household used, which was a folk word and meant nothing.

And a young woman on the step had said: *No. Look at what's under it.*

He had said he did not know what was under it.

She had said, *The layer underneath. It moved. Not the ground — the layer underneath the ground. Watch it again and you'll see it isn't the light that's wrong.*

He had watched it again. She had been right. It had taken him eight more years to join the Survey Corps and another decade after that to meet anyone else who talked that way, and he had never once, in twenty years, found the phrase in a manual.

He had used it on this causeway, in the mud, at their first meeting, on purpose.

She had looked at him as though he had produced it from nowhere.

::: pov elvira

The survey man had a tell, and Elvira had been three weeks working out what it was.

It was not deception. She had grown up around people who lied for family reasons and could recognise the shape from across a room, and this was not that shape. It was closer to the way a person holds a cup they have already decided is too hot: a small, constant management of something they are not going to put down and not going to mention.

He asked questions he already had answers to. That she had noticed in the first week and filed as ordinary — half the Survey Corps did it, checking a witness against the record without saying so.

What was not ordinary was the vocabulary.

He used her words. Not the Corps' words, which she had learned to translate into and out of. Hers — the ones she had built for herself out of nothing, in an outpost, alone, over three years, because the official glossary had no term for a thing she could measure. *The layer underneath.* *Pattern substrate.* *A working that needs a monument hasn't finished.*

She had assumed, at first, convergence: two people looking at the same mechanism long enough will arrive at similar language, the way two smiths arrive at similar tongs. That was a reasonable model and she had held it for a fortnight.

It had a defect. Convergence gets you the same *idea* in different words. It does not get you the same words.

"Where did you learn to talk about it like that?" she asked. She had asked before, in the mud, without her voice, and he had declined to answer an expression, which was a thing a person could do honestly.

He looked up from the file. He took slightly longer than the question needed.

"From someone better at it than me," he said. "A long time ago."

"A teacher?"

"No." He squared the pages. "She wasn't teaching. She just didn't like the word I'd used."

Elvira waited, and he did not go on, and she found that she had stopped reading the page in front of her.

She did not experience deep fear. She had understood for years that this was structural rather than earned, and she had built a practice on top of it that required standing in a Type II oscillation and continuing to take measurements. It was very rarely inconvenient.

It was inconvenient now, because it meant that whatever had just moved through the room had to be identified some other way than by how it felt, and she had nothing to identify it with except a man being careful.

I estimate the remembered one at a considerable distance in time, on the strength of nothing better than how badly he is doing at it.

::::: scene 3

::: pov galahad

He tried once more at the door, because he was going to be gone by evening and because a hypothesis you never test is not caution, it is just a thing you carry.

"The gate at the bottom of a garden," he said, as if it were nothing, as if it were the weather. "Where the light sits wrong beside the post and it isn't the light."

Elvira had a jar of chalk in her hand. She did not put it down.

For a moment — and he would write it in no report, because a moment is not a measurement and he had spent twelve years teaching junior surveyors exactly that — something went across her face that was not politeness and was not blankness either.

Then she said, "That's a thin place. Badly sited gate. Whoever built it wasn't paying attention." A pause of the ordinary length. "Where was this?"

"Somewhere I haven't been in a long time," Galahad said.

"You should measure it," she said. "If it's still standing."

"It's still standing."

She nodded, and turned back to the rack of thread, and the morning went on being a morning.

He walked out along the causeway with the file under his arm, past the root plate where the chalk had gone grey at the edges, and did the arithmetic he had been declining to do since the third week — twenty years, and a face that had not moved a day inside them, and no instrument anywhere in the Survey Corps that measured what he was actually claiming.

*Find out why it wasn't harder*, her note had said.

He had one reason. It was unfalsifiable, unfileable, and his alone, and the correct professional response to a pattern with a sample size of one was to hold it and say nothing until the data caught up.

He was aware, walking, that he had been telling himself this for three weeks, and that a man who has to keep re-deciding a decision has not really made it.
