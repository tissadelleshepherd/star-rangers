A ship waits before it folds, at any lawful site, and the *Steerage Way* had been waiting for nineteen days.

Wender had flown the waiting before. Nine years on the one track had taught her that a fold was mostly the time spent not folding: the hull certificate checked against the hull, the route certificate checked against its date, the scaffold grown at a site flat enough to grow one, and all of it watched by people whose whole trade was declining to hurry. The [staging site](/star-rangers/lore/lagrange-fold-points/) was a trailing trojan point of a world the record did not need to name, Tier 1, where a displaced hull drifted back rather than away and nobody had to spend power holding still. She had held station there for nineteen days, and the lock margin had sat on her board the whole time, a figure in the ordinary place, the way it sat on every board she had ever flown.

The route was the certified one into [Deadwater](/star-rangers/lore/universes/deadwater/). There was no other. The certificate had been re-issued the previous autumn on the ordinary schedule, and the pilot department's copy carried the ordinary date, and she had read the date before she read anything else, because a route was certified as of a day and the day was the only part of it a pilot could check without instruments.

Then the scaffold reached threshold, and the two regions were brought into adjacency, and she flew the band.

A fold was not a thing a person felt. It was a thing instruments agreed about afterwards, in a recalibration that took longer than the drift. What she felt was the drift's end: the scaffold collapsed behind her, the gradient she had departed from was gone from under the hull, and in its place sat the gradient the far staging had been built on, which was flatter, because the far side had been chosen by people with a long time to choose. The re-sync debt came up as a queue. She started working it in the order the department worked it, which was the order that could be audited, and she had the queue half down before she looked at the margin.

The margin was not there.

Not zero. Zero was a figure, and the board could show a figure. What the board showed was the mark it showed for a value under what the instrument was built to read: not a reading, a dash. She had seen the dash in a dock, with the lattice cold. She had never seen it with the lattice warm and the hull in open space, and she looked at the lattice, which was warm, and at the fault register, which was empty, and then back at the dash, which was still a dash.

"Margin's under floor," she said, to the log.

"Yes, Senior." The relief pilot beside her had flown the run twice. "It will be under floor the whole time we are here. Log it on the hour. It is on the sheet."

It was on the sheet. Item fourteen. She had read the sheet at the staging site, as she read everything, and had read *harmonic attenuation, pilot department, under full lattice power, hourly* as a line of work and not as a sentence about where she was going.

---

The station was a Survey Corps platform at the far staging's quiet point, four years crewed, and it kept its date the way the file said it would. The *Steerage Way* had brought the date in: the ensemble's own, carried in the hull's clock across a crossing that had crossed nothing, and the station's log took it and wrote beside it the pedigree, three hulls deep now, from the last relief and the one before it. There were no couriers to loop against. Nothing here moved faster than a hull, and nothing a hull could do moved faster than light, and so the station's time was what the last ship had brought and the ship's own clock had kept since.

The sheet gave her the launch at the fourth hour.

She ran it the way the sheet said. The lattice to full power, in open space, with nothing docked and nothing within the envelope: the same work she would have done to phase-lock to a corridor signature at home, and she did it with the care she would have done it at home, because the department did not do a thing less carefully for being told it would not work. The front went out. The board showed it going out, as a front, lawful, carrying the lattice's full signature. She watched the figure that was the front's own strength against the distance it had travelled, and the figure fell.

It did not fall as a fault falls. A fault had a shape she had seen in a simulator and twice in a hull: the sharp step, the register filling, the lattice asking to be shut down. This fell the way a thing falls that is being absorbed. By the second light-minute it was below half. By the third it was below what the board called a front, and the board stopped calling it one, and the lattice sat at full power with nothing in front of it, and the fault register stayed empty, because nothing was wrong. The medium had taken the front and given back nothing, and every instrument she had reported that it had done its work and was fine.

She logged it. The figures, the time, *under full lattice power, fault register clear*. She did not log that she had sat for a further minute with the lattice warm and watched the place where a margin would have been.

Afterwards the station's chart was on the table in the common room, because the chart was always on the table, and she read it the way she would have read any system's chart: a star, its distance, its class, its route. The catalogue ran to the near stars and past them. Every one had a distance against it, and a class, and a line for the route, and in the route column every entry read *none*. Not *unsurveyed*. Not *pending*. *None*, star after star, in the same hand, as a catalogue fact beside distance and class, and she read down the column for longer than the column needed.

The nearest was under five light-years. On the home side she would have called that the better part of a week.

She put the chart down. She did not ask anyone at the table what they thought of the column, because the table had been looking at it for four years and she had been looking at it for a morning, and the asking would have been a claim about which of those was the harder.

::: pov asteria

A Star Captain had no business on a relief run, and Asteria Wessex had made a point, for most of a long service, of knowing what her business was.

The posting answered up one of her lines, through a Line Captain who had never once needed her to come and look. She had come and looked. She had told the Line Captain it was a familiarisation, which was a word the Corps kept for exactly this use, and the Line Captain had written it down and asked nothing, and she had been grateful to him for that in a way she did not examine.

The station's relief list was on her table, and she had read it twice, because the second column was the one the Corps did not discuss.

Six on the station. Three going home on the *Steerage Way*, two at the posting's full term and one, a Section Lead on the Planetology side, at four months of it. Against the Section Lead's name, in the Medical Corps' column, was the ground the Medical Corps stated: *radiation*. Asteria had the station's dose log beside the list, as she was entitled to have it, and the log showed what the log had shown for four years, which was a platform at a quiet point in an ordinary system taking the ordinary dose. She had read it twice too.

A medical finding was the Medical Corps' and no rank could overwrite it. That was not a courtesy to her; it was the rule every specialist corps ran under, and the one she would have defended against anyone. So she countersigned the list, as the ladder asked, under the word the Medical Corps had chosen, and she did not write beside it what she thought of the word, because what she thought of the word was not a finding either. The Section Lead had asked for nothing. The Section Lead had been seen, by two separate officers whose statements were in the file, standing at the viewport in the station's night with the chart in her hand, and had answered, when asked, that she was fine, in a voice both officers had described with the same word, and the word was *polite*.

Nobody on this station was afraid. Asteria had checked, which was a thing she knew how to do, and the thing she would have found if it were there was not there. They were courteous and tired and good at their work and they rotated home early, and the word for why sat in a column, and it was the wrong word, and nobody had a right one.

She went down to the pilot deck at the fourth hour because the launch was at the fourth hour, and she had no reason to be on the pilot deck that she would have accepted from a subordinate.

The Senior ran it clean. Asteria had commanded a boundary hull for most of her service and knew what clean looked like from the back of a deck: the lattice brought up in the order that could be audited, the front launched with the full signature as if it would be met, and then the pilot's whole attention on the fall, not on the fault register, which was where a less good pilot would have looked. The register had nothing to say. The medium had everything to say and said it quietly, and the Senior watched it to the end, and a minute past the end, and logged the figures.

She did not ask the Senior what she had seen. She noticed herself not asking, and noticed that the Senior had noticed, and left both where they were.

What she thought, with the hull's own clock keeping a date no loop would ever close, was the thing the file declined to think on her behalf. The design was coherent. The system around the station was as fertile as systems were; the chart on the table was a chart of worlds no more or less promising than any sky's. Nothing in it read as damage. Somebody had made a universe with no doors between its stars, and made it well, and the file on why was one line long and would stay so, because there was no instrument that read intent and she had spent a career refusing to pretend there was.

What she did not think, because it was not hers to think, was what a sky like that did to the people who had to look at it. The Medical Corps had a word. The word was wrong. She countersigned under it, and the ladder did not ask her to do more, and for once in a long service she wished it did.

The far staging was quieter than the home one. It had been chosen to be.

She grew the scaffold on the sheet's timings and the station's gradient, with the three going home strapped in behind her and the Star Captain somewhere aft, not on the deck, which was correct. The scaffold reached threshold. The two regions were brought into adjacency, and she flew the band, and the gradient she had come from was gone from under the hull.

The re-sync queue came up. She worked it in the order the department worked it.

The margin was there.

A figure, in the ordinary place, the same figure as the one she had held station on for nineteen days, and she had flown nine years on it without once looking at it. She looked at it now for the length of a breath, which was not in the queue, and then she went back to the queue, because the queue was what could be audited.

She wrote the figure. She did not write anything else.
