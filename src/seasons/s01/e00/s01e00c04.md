::::: scene 1

:::pov anne

By the ninth day Anne had three rules, and the three rules were the whole government of the compartment: everyone ate when she said, everyone slept when the lights dimmed on the cycle she kept running by hand, and every question got a true answer, or the true statement that she did not know. She had made the rules because the children kept bringing her questions, and she had kept answering because there was nobody standing behind her to hand a question up to. She was the oldest. The doors at the end of the corridor had been sealed since the first hour, and by the second day she had stopped telling the children that someone would come through them.

The ship did not hum anymore. She had not known, before, that she had spent her whole life inside a sound; she learned it the hour the sound stopped. After that the compartment ran on her voice and on the rules, and she fed them small and often, because small and often produced fewer questions than large and rarely.

The help arrived first as a feeling in the frame — slow, patient strokes through the hull plating, the pressure of something enormous taking hold of the outside of the world and being careful about it. The children looked at her. That was the compartment's other law, the one nobody had made: everything went through Anne's face on its way to being real. "That's help," she said, and was believed, and then had to sit with the children believing her until it kept on being true.

It kept on being true slowly. The strokes moved along the hull with no hurry in them at all. Twice everything stopped — long, considered stillnesses in which Anne kept her face in order — and twice it began again. Then small machines came ahead of everything else, down the length of the dead ship, cutting and shoring the way as they came. Eight of them. Anne counted them through the door's port, because counting was one of the things that had kept the compartment a country.

Then the seal on the corridor door went from red to a colour Anne had stopped expecting, and the machine that came through was shaped like a woman.

It took in the compartment in the time a person takes to blink — the folded ration wrappers, the sleeping order chalked beside the vents, the children, and Anne last and longest — and then it spoke to her first. "You kept them warm, and you kept them counted," it said, as if reading the compartment's government off its walls. "It was well done. I have them now — and I will need you exactly as much as I needed you a minute ago." Which was, Anne would think later, a strange and precise kindness: not *you can rest*, which would have been a demotion, and not a word about cost, which would have been a bill.

"My designation is Lucene-9000," it said then, to all of them, in a voice pitched to carry to the smallest. "Lucene is enough."

The children looked at Anne. Anne nodded. After that they let themselves be held.

Where the machines had come from, and who had sent them, the machine did not volunteer and Anne did not ask. It had not seemed, that night, like the missing fact.

Anne's sister had not said much for two days. From inside the machine's arms Karla asked whether their mother was coming — a direct question, and the machine answered it the way Anne would learn it answered everything: completely. The answer was no. It came level and unhurried, and it did not arrive alone: no, and what no was going to mean tonight, and what it would go on meaning after, and the one thing that was not going to change, which was that the arms had her and were not going to open. Anne stood in the cold and listened to a machine take her sister's question more seriously than anyone had ever taken one of hers, and could not have said, then or afterward, why that was the moment the nine days caught up with her eyes.

Then Karla said the machine's name back wrong.

"Lucy Anne."

Anne had been correcting her sister's words all her life. She knew the sound of a mistake, and this was not one: there had been a small pause before it, and Karla had watched the machine's face all the way through it, the way you watch a door you are deciding to walk through. A unit designation had gone into her sister and come back out a person. The machine did not correct her either. It looked at Karla for the length of one slow breath — reading, Anne supposed, whatever it was built to read — and said, "Yes."

The name had Anne's own name inside it. She noticed the way you notice a draught: once, completely, and without the first idea of what to do about it. She never did find out what to do about it. She called the machine Lucene — that night, and every time after.

The carrying went by turns, smallest first, and the compartment emptied the way it had been kept: in order. When the last lock closed on the last of the cold, Anne counted the small machines back, out of habit, because the habit had run a country for nine days and did not know yet that it was over. Five. She set the five beside the eight, the way she now set everything beside everything, and gave the two numbers to nobody.

::::: scene 2

:::pov falkorian

The rescue ship was warm, and the warmth kept waking Andrew up.

He could not explain this to anyone, including himself, so he stopped trying and went walking instead. Four decks down there was a bay with the lights still on, and in the bay a machine was working. It was an old machine. Andrew could not have said how he knew — nothing about it was worn — but you knew, the way you know an old tool from a new one: everything about it had been shaped by use into exactly what use required, and nothing about it was ever in a hurry. Its designation was Xenon. Andrew asked that early, and got it plainly.

It was going over the small machines from the wreck, one at a time, under a single bright light. Nothing visible was wrong with them. It went over them anyway.

Along the wall stood a rack of cradles, padded and latched. Some of them were empty. The padding in the empty ones had been smoothed and their latches closed, as carefully as the full ones.

"What are the empty ones for?" Andrew asked, because he was a boy who asked what things were for.

"They are the right size for the machines that go out first," Xenon said.

Andrew waited for the rest of the answer. There was no rest of the answer. Some instinct that had grown in him over nine days told him which questions had floors under them, and he did not ask the next one.

"Did you make them?" he asked instead, of the five under the light.

"No. I repair." A joint clicked softly through some small errand of motion. "I am not permitted to create new robots." It said this the way a hull plate says what it is rated for: stamped, factual, and carrying more than it looked like it was carrying.

"You are awake," Xenon observed then, "and your hands are steady. Hold this light."

Andrew held the light. The work under it was small and exact and had a rhythm, and the rhythm did what the warmth of his bunk had not. He came back the next night, and the one after; nobody had told him he could stay, and nobody had told him to go — the light needed holding, and that settled it. By the third night he was handing over the right tool before it was named.

On the fourth he fell asleep there, sitting up against the rack with the light still in his hands. He half woke, once, when it was lifted from them — carefully, so as not to wake him the rest of the way — and the last thing he heard before going back under was the work going on, unhurried, one-handed: the sound of something being kept running.

::::: scene 3

:::pov anne

Eden came up slowly, and then all at once — a ring of lights first, and then a ring of country: green, actual green, turning in its own weather with the sun let in along one long window of it. The children crowded the port. Anne stood behind them and watched the green go by underneath and understood that she was looking at the first thing in twenty days that was bigger than what had happened to them.

Lumbarian did not come inside; there was no inside for him. He rode the last stretch in the open hold, and the children were brought down to see him — this was Lucene's doing, Anne understood — the shape that had walked on the outside of the world, far too large for any door they had ever walked through. He lowered one manipulator until it rested flat on the deck and held it there, perfectly still, while the smallest survivors patted it. Something that size being that still is a language of its own, and the children understood it at once, the way they understood animals.

Eden's intake hall was full of morning. The forms were kind the way good forms are kind: short, and in order, and asking nothing twice. The children went through in small groups; Anne went through with Karla asleep on her shoulder and Andrew beside her, smelling of machine oil. The officer took her name, and she gave it — "Wender. Anne Wender" — and her sister's after it. Then the form reached the line that asked how they were to be housed, and the officer looked up at the three of them, and started to ask.

"Together," Anne said, before the question finished.

The officer looked a moment longer — at Karla asleep, at Andrew's oil-marked hands, at Anne's face doing what her face had learned to do — and wrote it down. It went into the record as plainly as that, and the record, the way records do, set about making it true: one word, holding three people.

Lucene came down the ramp with them, and kept coming — through the intake hall and past it, into the weeks on the other side, until the escorting had turned into something no form had a line for, and Anne had stopped, at some point, expecting her to leave. "Thank you, Lucene," Anne said at the foot of the ramp: the name whole and correct, as she always said it, then and after.

It was a small word, *together*. Anne had thought so even as she said it. But she had lately watched a smaller one hold — said once, on purpose, by a child with almost nothing left, and holding still — and she was beginning to understand how it worked. At the start, a family is a small word said on purpose. Everything after it is the keeping.
