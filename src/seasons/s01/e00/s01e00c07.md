The window had been due to turn by fourteen hours and had not, and at twenty hours Valerius stopped calling it late.

*Late* was a word for a thing with a schedule, and the interface his post monitored had a period rather than a schedule: windows that ran between six hours and fourteen, each one ending when it ended, alternating which side of the [Interval](/star-rangers/glossary/interval/) set the clocks. Fourteen was the longest the post had logged in the year and some he had answered for it. Twenty was a figure with no entry above it. He had the watch enter it as a figure and not as a word.

The two reference housings on the bench behind the operations desk were matched to the half-second at every turn and read, between turns, the interface's own number: twenty-six seconds, at this post, for as long as the post had kept them. He read the upper one and then the lower, which was the order he read them in, and the figure between them was no longer twenty-six. It had been fifty at the twelfth hour and two minutes at the eighteenth, and the climb had no shape he could file as a rate, because a rate assumes a baseline that holds, and that was the one thing nothing in the approach was doing.

"Say the figure," he said.

"Two minutes forty, Starwarden." Field Officer Holm said it to the log rather than to him, which was correct. "Upper leading."

Upper leading. He had never once allowed *which suggests* on this post and he did not allow it now. Upper was leading because that was where the number was. Which side of the Interval that made dominant for the window was a Safety Corps question with a Safety Corps answer, and what he owed the officers who would answer it was the figure, in order, with the time against each one from both clocks.

What he also owed them was the *Fair Return*.

She was a Union salvage hull, four aboard, working the approach's permitted edge under a permit his own readings had drawn: the edge sat where the divergence had never yet reached a figure the Safety Corps considered a hazard to a docking clock, which was an edge drawn from twenty-six seconds. He had the permit on the desk. He had, beside it, two minutes forty, and a hull whose master had called in at the eighteenth hour to say, with a courtesy that had not quite covered the question underneath it, that her own clocks had started doing something she would like the post to confirm was the post's.

It was not the post's. He had told her so, in those words, and that the figure was climbing, and that the permit stood until he withdrew it, and that he was not withdrawing it, because withdrawing a permit from a hull already inside its terms told her nothing she could use. What she could use was the callouts.

"Paired callouts from here on," he said. "Every transaction. Both clocks, upper then lower, and she reads back both."

"Starwarden."

He went back to the viewport, because the viewport was where he did the arithmetic that did not go in the log, and there was nothing to see in it, which was ordinary. The [boundary](/star-rangers/lore/boundary-zones/) his post monitored was not out there. It was not at any distance that existed, in any direction a viewport faced, and he had corrected enough filings to that effect that the watch no longer wrote *beyond the approach* and he no longer had to strike it. What was out there was an ordinary starfield and, four seconds of light to his left, a salvage hull whose master was now going to read two numbers back to him for every one she used.

He had not asked her to choose between them. He noticed that he had not, and filed the noticing where he filed the things with no scope.

::: pov sunniva-holm

Holm had stood the operations watch on three posts and had never heard a Starwarden say *paired callouts* as if it were a thing a post ordinarily did, and had understood, by the twenty-fourth hour, that on this post it was.

It worked like this. The hull called a transaction — a thruster trim, a tether length, a time to hold station by — and the watch gave her the time from the upper housing and then the time from the lower, and the hull's master read both back, and the hull held from the first time to the second and did the thing at the second, so that whichever clock her own was nearer to, the thing was done inside both. Nobody on either end said which clock was right. Holm had asked, at the sixth hour, which one was right. The Starwarden had asked her whether she meant right at the housing or right at the hull, and had waited for the answer, and she had not had one, and he had gone back to his viewport as though that had been a reply.

By the twenty-ninth hour the figure was four minutes and one second, and the *Fair Return* lost lock.

It was not dramatic. Nothing on this post was permitted to be. The hull's master said, in a perfectly level voice, that her docking clock had stopped agreeing with her navigation clock by an amount she could not hold a tether to, and that her second had just told her a thing in an order the second swore he had not said it in, and that she would like, if the post did not object, to come in off the edge under callouts and not under her own instruments.

"Come in under callouts," the Starwarden said, from the viewport, without turning round. "Upper then lower. Say both back. Do nothing on either until you have said both."

So that was what the twenty-ninth hour was, and the thirtieth: a hull coming in by numbers it did not trust, off an edge it had been told was safe by a permit drawn from a figure that no longer obtained, with a man at the desk saying two times for every one thing and a woman four seconds away saying them back and then doing the thing. Holm logged each pair. She logged the point, in the thirtieth hour, at which the master's second came on and read back a pair in the wrong order, and the master's voice behind him saying *lower, then upper, Teague, the man said both*, and the pair read again.

The *Fair Return* made the inner marker at the thirty-first hour with four aboard and a tether she had cut to come in. Eight minutes later the window turned.

Holm had read about a turn and had never watched one. It was nothing. The figure between the two housings, which had been four minutes and nine, was fifty seconds, and then thirty, and then twenty-six, and sat there, and the lower housing was leading, by the twenty-six seconds it had always led by, as if the thirty-one hours had been a thing that happened to other instruments.

She wrote: *Turn. 26 s. Lower leading.* She looked at it. The Starwarden had still not come away from the viewport, and she wondered whether he would want the turn logged against the upper clock or the lower, and knew the answer, and logged it against both.

::: pov sunniva-holm

The band came up in the hour after, and she nearly did not log it, because she had been awake for thirty-one hours and the field instruments were, for the first time since the fourteenth hour, showing her nothing wrong.

It was not nothing wrong. It was the absence of a particular kind of wrong. Every field trace on the post ran ragged near a turn, the way a sea runs after the wind has dropped, and she had watched the ragged edge settle for twenty minutes and was writing *field traces settling* when one of them did not settle and did something else. A narrow band. Harmonic. It did not wander. It sat in its band the way the lower housing sat at twenty-six seconds, as if it had been there all along and the ragged edge had been hiding it, and it was so gentle that her first thought was that an instrument had found a resonance in its own mount.

She checked the mount. The mount was sound.

She pulled the anomaly register, which was the thing a watch did with a trace it had no entry for, and the register had an entry for it. She read the entry twice. It was a field-signature entry, old, cross-referenced to boundary stations she had not served on, in two [Concordants](/star-rangers/glossary/concordant/), and it described her band to the width, and the name on it was *the Turquoise Dove*, which was not a classification and not an instrument designation. It was the kind of name a watch gives a thing on a long night and then cannot take back. She looked up from it at the Starwarden's back, at the viewport, where he had been standing since before the turn with his hands at his sides, and found she did not want to be the one who said the name aloud on his post.

"Starwarden. Field trace. Narrow band, settled, and the register has it." She gave the entry number. "Nine minutes so far."

He did not turn round for a time she could have logged and did not. Then he came to the desk and read the entry standing, and read the trace, and asked her for the mount check, and she gave it to him, and he nodded as though the mount check were the interesting part.

"Unclassified transient," he said. "Band and duration. Attach the entry."

"Under its—"

"Under the band." He said it without any weight at all. "The register's name is a name for a readout. We have a readout. We don't have the rest of what the entry is for."

She logged it as he said. Nine minutes became fourteen and then the band was not there, and the field traces were ragged and ordinary, and the post had, as far as the log would ever show, one more unclassified transient than it had had that morning, which she had been told at her briefing was the thing this post was known for. The Starwarden went back to the viewport. He had not, she realised, written anything down in the hour since the turn, and the window was his to write up, and he had never in her month on the post let a thing sit unwritten for an hour.

She wrote the name on her own pad, under the entry number, because she would want to know which one it had been. Then she tore the sheet off and put it in her pocket and did not file it, and could not have said why that was the right thing, except that it was his post.

::: pov luke-valerius

He would not afterwards describe it as a meeting, because *meeting* assumed a second party, and he had no instrument that would establish one.

What he had was the viewport, and the ordinary starfield in it, and the bench behind him with the two housings on it, which he could see in the viewport's reflection and had been watching there, because the reflection showed him both at once and he was tired enough to want them in one glance. The figure had been four minutes and nine. Then it had been twenty-six and the lower was leading, and he had felt the post come back under him the way a deck does when a vessel stops yawing, and he had stood there a while longer because the arithmetic that did not go in the log had a great deal in it that night and he had not finished.

The window had run thirty-one hours. A hull had come in off an edge he had drawn. He had drawn it from twenty-six seconds, as he was required to, and it had held until it had not, and the four aboard were alive because a woman four seconds away had been willing to say two numbers back to him for two hours when every instinct of a ship's master was to pick one and act. He had not asked her to pick. He had been proud of that at the twentieth hour and was less sure, at the thirty-second, what it had been instead of asking.

That was where he was when the reflection had someone else in it.

He did not turn round. He noticed that he did not, and that the not turning was not fear, and that it was not calm either; it was the habit of a man who reads the instrument before the thing the instrument is pointed at. In the viewport, where the bench was, between the two housings and not behind them, there was a figure. A woman, he would have said, if required to say, in no uniform he knew and no colour he would have put a name to under oath. It was the kind of green that is also the kind of blue, and the viewport had a tint, and he knew the tint and could not subtract it. She was standing where the bench was. That was not possible, because the bench was there, and he could see the bench, and he could see her, and neither of them was in front of the other.

He had read the [lore](/star-rangers/lore/levrils/). Every certified boundary analyst had read it, and most of them had read it the way he had, as a set of claims with their scopes stated, and the one line he had never known what to do with was the line that said the witness supplies the figure. He supplied, apparently, a woman at his bench who was not at any distance from him he could have stated. He noted that the question he most often corrected on other officers' filings had just become a question about the inside of his own compartment, and he did not allow *which suggests*, and it cost him more than it had ever cost him on a filing.

Then there was a question, and it was which one he had read first.

He would not afterwards say that she had asked it. He would not say anything afterwards, which was the one decision about that hour he was sure of. What he had was the question, in no voice he could have placed in the compartment, and the plain fact that it was a question and not an instruction and not a comfort, and that it waited.

He thought it meant the crew. He had taken the four statements in order, master first, and could have said so. Then he thought it meant the hull's two clocks, docking and navigation, and which the master had believed when they parted. Then he was looking at the two housings in the reflection, upper and lower, matched to the half-second at every turn for the whole of his command, and he knew which one he read first, because he had read it first that morning and every morning, and had given it first on every callout, *upper then lower*, and had never been asked, and had never once asked himself what a year of readings taken in one order did to the record they were assembled into, by officers who were not here, who would be told the figure and the time and not the order.

He did not answer. He had no answer with a scope.

When he looked at the bench itself and not the reflection there was the bench, and the two housings on it, close together and identical, with nothing to read on them from where he stood. He looked back at the viewport. An ordinary starfield. Holm, behind him, saying his rank, and a band in the field traces that the register had a name for.

He went and read the entry, because that was what the entry was for.

He wrote the window up at the thirty-fourth hour, and it took him the time it took.

It said what the instruments carried. Window of thirty-one hours against a logged maximum of fourteen. Divergence from twenty-six seconds to four minutes nine, with the time against each figure from both housings. Permit for the *Fair Return* not withdrawn, on the stated reasoning. Lock lost at the twenty-ninth hour; hull brought in under paired callouts; four aboard, one tether cut, no injury. Turn at the thirty-first hour; twenty-six seconds within the hour; lower leading. One unclassified transient in the hour after, by band and duration, register entry attached. He classified nothing. The [Safety Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-safety-corps/) would decide whether a thirty-one-hour window was a Type II interface doing a new thing or a Type II interface becoming something with a different number, and that was theirs, and what he owed them was on the page.

Nothing else was. He had considered, for the length of time it took to consider it, whether a Starwarden who had stood at a viewport for an hour and could not account for the hour owed the record a line saying so, and had concluded that a line with no scope was a sentence still waiting for its subject, and had not written it. The record of his command would carry one more transient and no account of it. That was accurate. He was prepared to be asked why it was accurate, and knew nobody would.

Holm had gone off watch. Her relief had the desk and had not been on the post for the thirty-one hours, and read the log the way a relief does, for what was still open. Nothing was still open. The master of the *Fair Return* had sent her thanks to *na Maoir Réalta*, which was the Union's own name for the Corps, and he had entered them as sent.

In the morning, which on the post was a word for the second watch, he stood at the bench before he went to the viewport, and read the lower housing, and then the upper. The figure between them was twenty-six seconds. He did not write down which he had read first, because the log had never asked for it and he was not going to be the one who added the column. He noted that he had done it. He noted that he had no procedure for telling whether it mattered, and that the absence of one was not evidence either way, and that both sentences were correctly formed.

Then he went on filing.
