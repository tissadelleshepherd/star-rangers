:::pov galahad

The dock smelled of ozone and old metal. Galahad had learned the combination years ago, at his first boundary posting, where it had meant *boundary proximity*, though nobody in the Survey Corps used the term. They called it Threshold Stink. He had stopped correcting them somewhere around his second station, when it became clear that the people who said Threshold Stink were the same people who could tell you, to the day, when it had last got worse.

Dock Seven was the furthest point out. The instruments here never quite agreed.

Two chronometers on the wall showed different readings — the one above the pressure seal and the one above the inner hatch, barely forty seconds apart, but you noticed forty seconds when you lived here long enough. The survey office had filed three incident reports about it. The station administrators had filed two responses explaining there was nothing to file reports about. He had read all five on his first night, from the earliest, which was how he read anything. The reports were careful. The responses were brief.

He adjusted his coat and watched the new arrivals come through the pressure seal.

The tall woman came first — pale, unhurried, moving with a quality of attention he had learned to recognise at boundary postings. She was not scanning for threats. She was *reading* the space. Her eyes moved across the dock like a hand moving over a page in the dark, feeling for the letters. They went to the two chronometers once, both of them, and did not go back, which told him she had the number already and had come anyway.

*Krenyi.* Certain. He had worked with three before, and had learned from the first of them, expensively, that the thing to do with a Krenyi on arrival was nothing: no welcome that claimed more than it had, no assessment offered before one was asked for. He stood where he was and let her finish the dock.

Behind her: a bipedal robot — civilian chassis, older by the joint-casings, but with sensor arrays that were emphatically not standard. The arrays had been live since the seal opened. One of them had settled on the wall beside the inner hatch, where there was nothing to look at but a panel, and had stayed there.

He checked the roster on his handheld. Two new transfers. Both assigned to the boundary survey. He was apparently now their team lead, which the roster stated in the typeface it used for the seal pressures.

The woman stopped two paces from him and looked at him the way she had looked at the dock.

He had not said anything yet. He found he did not want to be the first to, and could not have said whether that was manners or caution, and decided that it did not need deciding on a dock at eighteen minutes to seven.

That was the station's time, the clock above the seal. The clock above the hatch made it forty seconds later. Both had been calibrated. Both were right.

:::pov syra

The station was exactly as the approach data had described and entirely unlike what the data had implied.

In a century and a half of navigating human-built environments, Syra had learned the difference. Data described surfaces. Implications described pressures. The data said: *mid-grade shore station, boundary-proximate, crew complement forty-seven, mixed species.* The implication said: *everyone here is waiting for something to go wrong, and they have all agreed not to say so.*

She could feel the [boundary](/star-rangers/lore/boundary-zones/) through the station's hull. Not as sensation exactly — more as a faint discontinuity in her own coherence. A slight lag between intention and action. A breath held somewhere behind the physical.

She filed it. Not a threat. Information.

The two chronometers were where the public survey data had put them, on the dock wall, one above the seal and one above the inner hatch, and the interval between them was the interval the data gave, to the second. She had read that number some weeks ago in a public release that classified it as a calibration issue and closed it, and she had read the closure twice, and then she had applied for the posting. She looked at the two faces once, to be sure the number was the number, and did not look again. A thing that has been classified wrongly does not need to be stared at. It needs somebody to say so, in the right room, to the right person, and a dock was not the room.

The human in the survey coat was watching her. She had been watched before — it was a standard feature of arriving anywhere — but this was the watching of a man who had worked with her kind before and was deciding whether to update his model. She found it preferable to the other kinds. He had not spoken. He had not stepped forward. He was letting her finish the dock, which almost nobody did, and she noted it and left it at that.

She noted the robot behind her in the reflection of the dock window. Rook-7's sensor arrays had been active since they cleared the pressure seal. Good. That was the correct response. One array was holding on a point on the far wall that she had not yet read herself, and she did not turn to read it, because a robot that has found something will say so when it has finished counting, and a Krenyi who turns to look at what the robot is looking at has told the whole dock that the robot has found something.

"You're the team lead," she said to the human. The posting order had said so, and she had read the posting order. She wanted to see what he did with being told a thing he already knew.
