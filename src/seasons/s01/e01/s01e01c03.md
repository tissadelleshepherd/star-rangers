:::pov galahad

The station's incident record was eleven years deep because the station was thirteen years old, and Galahad had spent the walk down from Dock Seven turning that over the way he turned over any figure that had been offered to him as a depth. Eleven years was not a depth. It was a floor. Any baseline older than the station had to come out of something that had already been standing here when the first hull was laid, and on this shore the list of things that had already been standing here was short.

One of them had a light on top of it.

The spit ran out from the shingle for the better part of a kilometre and it did not take a vehicle on a falling tide, which the duty clerk had told him twice, in the tone of a man who had watched people find that out for themselves. So they walked it. The wind had come round to the southwest during the briefing and had not eased since: not a gust structure at all but one continuous pressure with harder events inside it, arriving from the left, so that the three of them walked the spit leaning and corrected between events like people crossing a deck. Speech was not available in it. Galahad had tried once, near the shore end, and had watched the sentence leave his mouth and go inland without him.

The tower was whitewashed stone and there was a great deal more of it below the gallery than a photograph ever suggested. Its door faced landward. Nothing on this coast put a door on the seaward side twice.

The attendant had the door open before they reached it and did not appear surprised by any of them, including Rook-7, which most people looked at twice.

"You've an hour and fifty of the falling," she said, once the door was shut and the noise had dropped to the level where a room was possible. "After that you're stopping the night, and there's the one bed."

"Sir Galahad Thorne. Survey Corps, up at the station."

"Nuala Beirne. I know what you are, the coat says it." She was somewhere past sixty and dressed for the gallery rather than for visitors. "I've the light and I've the tide gauge, and the gauge is the Board's and the light's the Board's and neither of them is the station's, so whatever it is, say it plainly and I'll tell you if it's mine."

Twelve years of boundary postings had taught Galahad that the useful witnesses were almost never the ones who volunteered. "Your clock," he said. "The one that times the character. I want to know how often it needs correcting, and I want to know whether anyone has ever written that down."

Beirne looked at him for a moment with an expression he could not immediately file.

"Everyone writes that down," she said. "It's a maintenance record. That's the whole job." Then, after a pause that was not quite hesitation: "Nobody's ever asked for it."

She went up for the book. The tower did not sway — a good tower does not, and this one had been built by people who knew that — but the lantern glass carried the gale as a single low note that came down the stair and sat in the jaw rather than in the ear, and Galahad stood in a whitewashed room at the foot of a tower older than his own service and did not say the thing he had already begun to think.

:::pov syra

The register was three volumes and Syra started at the beginning of the first one, which was the only way she had ever known how to read anything.

The hands changed and the instruments changed and the ink changed and the column did not. *Date. Signal received. Clock reads. Correction applied, in seconds. Initials.* Copperplate for the first two hundred pages, then a period of very poor pencil, then ballpoint, then a printed slip pasted in at intervals and hand-annotated, then ballpoint again when whoever had been supplying the slips stopped supplying them. Fourteen attendants that she counted, and one of them for forty-one years.

The corrections were small. That was the first thing, and it was the reason nobody had ever looked at them: two seconds, three seconds, four, at intervals of a season or a year, which is exactly what a mechanical clock in a damp stone tower on a coast does, and exactly what a person with a maintenance responsibility writes down and forgets.

The second thing took her most of the first volume to be sure of, because being sure of it required the whole of the first volume.

They ran one way.

Not predominantly one way. One way. A clock keeps poor time by drifting, and a drifting mechanism corrected by fourteen different hands across fourteen different mechanisms produces a column of numbers that argues with itself — gains here, losses there, and a rate that changes every time a bearing is replaced. This column did not argue with itself. Every correction in three volumes had been applied in the same direction, through four complete replacements of the movement, which is not a property a clock can have. It is a property a *place* can have.

The third thing was in the size.

She laid the volumes open across the table in the order they had been written and ran the column with a finger, and the early pages gave her two and three seconds a year and the middle volume gave her four and five, and the last forty pages gave her nine seconds inside a single season, twice, initialled *N.B.* in the same steady hand that had opened the door.

"Your corrections are growing," Syra said.

"They are." Beirne was leaning against the sill with her arms folded, watching the water rather than the table. "I put a new movement in it the winter before last for that exact reason. Cost the Board more than I'm worth. Made no odds at all."

There was a marginal note on the facing page, in a different hand and a different decade, initialled by an inspector Syra could not identify from the register alone. *Corrections continue. The tower has its own habit and always has. No action.*

"That is not a finding," Syra said. "A tower does not have a habit. A clock in a place has a rate, and if the rate is not the clock's then it is the place's, and somebody should have said which." She turned the page back and read the entry above it, and then the one above that, because a marginal note is worth exactly as much as the entries it was written beside and no more. "This was the reading available to him. He had the same column I have."

"He'd have had it for thirty years," Beirne said. "It'd have been ordinary to him by then."

Syra considered that and found it to be true rather than exculpatory, which are different things and are frequently confused. The inspector had not been wrong about the data. He had described a two-hundred-year measurement in the vocabulary of a fond remark about a building, and then he had written *no action*, and the vocabulary was the reason the *no action* had never once been challenged in the decades since — not because anybody had reviewed the judgement and agreed with it, but because nothing in the phrasing had ever announced that a judgement had been made.

She said none of that. It was not material and it was not hers to file.

"The station's discrepancy has been present for eleven years," she said instead. "That is what the incident reports support. It is not what your book supports. Your book supports a great deal longer, and Sir Galahad will want to say so carefully, because the two records were kept for different reasons and only one of them was kept by people who were being asked a question."

"You'll want the book, then."

"I want the book. You will want a receipt for it, and you should insist on one, because it is the Board's property and the Corps is not always careful with things it did not commission." Syra closed the third volume and put her hand flat on it. "Nai Syra. I am still here and I would like to be clear about one thing, because you have been clear with us. Nothing in this makes the light important. The light is an ordinary light and it has been kept correctly. What is unusual is the shore."

Beirne unfolded her arms.

"Well," she said. "That's the first sensible thing anyone from up there has said about it."

:::pov rook

Rook-7 did not log audio on the gallery. The wind's noise floor sat above the threshold at which its intake could resolve anything worth carrying, and a log entry that recorded only that a microphone had been overwhelmed was an entry about the microphone. It logged the other channels instead, and it counted.

Two flashes, then dark, then two flashes, then dark. Fifteen seconds, group to group. It counted four hundred and six groups against its own clock while the humans were still on the stair, holding to the rail on the seaward side where the gallery narrowed, and the character was correct to within the tolerance the register required and correct to nothing tighter than that, which was itself the finding: the light was accurate to the standard it was inspected against, and the standard it was inspected against had been set by people who had never needed it to be accurate to anything else.

*Observation: the register is complete. Completeness is not a property the Board required, specified, or inspected for. It is a by-product of a maintenance obligation that happens to have been discharged in writing for two hundred and seventeen years by people who were not being asked anything. Flag: the survey's search of the archive was a search of records created to answer questions. This record was not.*

Rook-7 held that entry unsent, which it did with roughly one entry in forty, and appended a second line to the same block.

It had asked the attendant one question, on the stair, in the interval when the door below was shut and speech was available. Nothing on the water had navigated by the Sceir Light in a very long time — the traffic that used this shore carried its own solutions and had for generations, and the Board's own register recorded the light's fixes as a formality against instrument failure that had not been invoked in Rook-7's lifetime or the attendant's. It had asked why the light was still lit.

"Because it's lit," Beirne had said, on a stair, in the dark, with the note in the glass coming down past both of them. "That's the whole of it. A light you only put on when you know someone's out there isn't a light. It's a favour."

Rook-7 logged the sentence verbatim and filed it under nothing, there being no category in its schema that the sentence belonged to, and followed the humans down.

Below, on the spit, the falling tide had another fifty minutes in it and the wind had not eased at all.
