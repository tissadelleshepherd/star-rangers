:::pov galahad

The survey office occupied a corner of the station that nobody had bothered to give a window. Galahad had decided this was intentional. Windows on this side showed the boundary shimmer on bad days, and the shimmer had a documented tendency to make new arrivals ask difficult questions before they had the context to ask them well.

He spread the incident reports across the table. Eleven years of them. He had laid them out in date order before the others arrived, the three reports and the two responses between them where they fell, because a file read in the order it was written says things a file read by its conclusion does not, and he wanted the table to say them without his help.

"The official position," he said, "is that the chronometer discrepancy is a calibration issue that has proved difficult to resolve. The unofficial position—" He paused. "Is that we stopped trying to resolve it after Year Three, because every time we recalibrated, it came back within forty-eight hours."

Syra was reading the reports in sequence. Not how most people read reports — most people skipped to the conclusion. She started at the earliest entry and worked forward, occasionally returning to re-read something. He read that way himself, and had never said so on a station, because on most stations the people who went to the conclusion first were the people who had written it. He watched her do it and said nothing about it, and was aware that saying nothing was itself a thing he had decided.

Rook-7 had not sat down. It stood near the door with its sensor arrays pointed at the wall — at the section of wall, Galahad realised, that backed onto the boundary-proximate section of the station.

"Eleven years," Syra said, without looking up. "The first report predates the last major boundary survey by three years."

"Yes."

"There is no note in the record connecting those two facts."

"No. There isn't." A pause. "I noticed that too."

He had noticed it on his first night, with the five documents on the desk in his quarters, and he had drafted the note. It was four lines. It was still in his handheld, unfiled, because a note connecting two facts on a station that had twice put in writing that there was nothing to connect was a note that got a third response, and he had wanted the connection made in a hand the administration could not answer with a paragraph. He did not say that either. It was not the kind of thing a team lead said to a transfer on her first morning, and she had not asked.

She looked up then. Something in her expression shifted — not warmth exactly, but a recalibration. A slight upgrade in her assessment of him. He found, to his mild surprise, that this mattered to him.

:::pov syra

Report 22 was the one she read three times.

Two junior surveyors, a corridor in Section G, a boundary proximity event, a word — *shimmer* — that both of them had used and neither had been asked to define. Both cleared medical. Incident closed. The report had asked a question about a corridor. The closure answered one about two people. Every sentence in the file was true, and the file had changed what it was about between its first line and its last, and nobody in it had noticed the substitution.

Syra noticed. It was the thing her kind noticed before anything else: the moment a situation stopped matching its stated terms. She filed it as drift. Not as suppression — she could not yet tell which, and she would not say which until she could.

She did not withdraw. The file was incoherent; the room was not. The human had put the five documents on the table in the order they were written and was letting them be read, which almost nobody did.

Syra was building a model of the situation as she read. Not analysis — more like listening to a room fill with sound, waiting for the point where the harmonics revealed the shape of the walls.

What the reports described, taken together, was a coherence leak. A point where the boundary between two [Concordants](/star-rangers/glossary/concordant/) had thinned, and the expressions of each were bleeding through into the other. The instruments could not agree on the time because the time was slightly different on each side of the boundary, and Threshold Station occupied both sides simultaneously.

This was not a malfunction. It was a condition.

She did not say this yet. The human was still in the part of the briefing where he presented facts and watched for reactions. She had learned to let people finish before offering interpretations they had not yet requested. More efficient. Considerably more respectful.

Rook-7 interested her more than the reports did. Its sensor arrays were aimed at the wall with a specificity that meant it had already identified something. The status light had been active since they arrived.

She made a note: *ask Rook-7 what it has found. Not here. Later.* Under it, a second: *Report 22. The two surveyors were examined. The corridor was not. Ask who decided which.*

The human's name was Thorne. She had not asked him to use a different form of address, and he had not offered one. In her experience, this was a good beginning.
