:::pov tissadelle

The instruments had agreed with each other every ninety seconds for eleven days, and on the twelfth, at ring's night, they agreed about something.

Shepherd saw it because the agreement log was the one thing on the watch a Deputy could read without being told to, and she had been reading it since the first shift — not because anyone had said to, but because it was there and it was a record. Nobody on the watch read it; Smith had said so on the first day, in the same flat voice she had used for *the event has not come in my tenure*, and Shepherd had taken that as a description of the watch and not as an instruction to her. The instruments read it themselves. That did not stop a person reading it as well.

What they had agreed about took eleven seconds. All six carried it, each on its own principle, and all six had filed it under the same word, which was not *event*. It was *transient*, with a class beside it from the second column of a table Shepherd had learned in her first week: artificial, drive-band, inside the approach. A thing a vessel does. The table did not say what vessel, because the instruments did not know what a vessel was; they knew the approach, and something had crossed it with its drive lit, for eleven seconds, at an hour for which the oversight's clearance list — which the watch held, and which was the one list a Deputy was told to check — had cleared nothing.

Fourteen minutes past three, ring time. She knew the hour. That was the part she sat with.

A year before her intake she had sung on the dock circuit, in a room under the ring where the late set ran until the dock's shift changed, because that was when the loaders came in and the room filled, and the band played its last set to people who had just stopped lifting things. She knew to the quarter-hour when the dock master's office was between its night staff and its day staff, because for a year the band had timed its break by it. Nobody was at the dock master's desk at a quarter past three. Nobody had been for as long as she had been on Eden.

So she knew what the eleven seconds meant, or thought she did, and she knew it from a room on the Compact side that no part of her posting had ever been in.

She wrote the notice. The form was the watch's and had four lines: the time, to the second; the duration; the class, in the table's words; and whether a transit had been cleared, with the list's reference beside it. She filled all four. The fifth line was *remarks*, and she had learned at the Garda college on Tír na nÓg — two weeks short of the oath, which was a thing she had stopped explaining — that the remarks line was where a report stopped being evidence. She left it empty. What she could support was that six instruments had agreed about eleven seconds inside the approach and that the clearance list had nothing in it for that hour. What she knew about a quarter past three she knew from a band, and it would have gone into a Ranger notice as a Ranger's opinion about where the Compact's dock master kept his staff, and the Rangers held no opinion about that, because they had no standing to hold one.

It cost her something to leave the line empty. She had expected it to, and it did. Then she put the notice on the desk for the Field Officer's initial, and stood a step back from it, and waited.

:::pov zoe-smith

Smith read the notice standing, the way she read everything.

Four lines, filled. The fifth empty. The time to the second, which was the instruments' and not the Deputy's, and copied right; the class in the table's words and not in English; the clearance column checked against the list and not against memory, because the list's reference was on the line, which a Deputy who had checked memory would not have thought to write. There was nothing to strike. She had initialled notices of this class before, and sent two back, both for the fifth line. The Corps' rawest Deputies wrote on the fifth line. They wrote on it because they had seen something, and the watch had not yet taught them the difference between seeing a thing and the watch having seen it.

This one had seen something. Smith could tell from where the Deputy was standing, which was a step back from the desk and very still, with the particular stillness of a person who has decided not to say a thing and is waiting to find out whether she will be made to. Smith did not know what it was. She did not ask. Nothing in the doctrine let an officer ask a Deputy for an opinion she could not then log, and the doctrine was right about that, as it was about most things it was clear on.

She initialled the notice and sent it up to the Passage oversight, where it would be read by an officer who would pass it, under the oversight's seal and with none of the watch's names, to the Compact's dock master — as the oversight passed every transient of the artificial class, not as a complaint, since the Corps did not complain, but as a fact about the fold route's safety that the people who owned the vessels were owed. What the dock master did with it was the dock master's. The watch investigated nothing.

What she noted, because it was small, was that the Deputy had never taken the step. Twelve days, and this one had not once stood where the others stood; she stood where the log was, as if the compartment had been arranged around the log rather than around the officer, which it had. Smith wrote none of that down. An assessment records. There would be time.

:::pov larsen

The notice reached Larsen the way everything reached him, which was late and through somebody else's good intentions.

The Passage oversight had sent it to Eden's dock master as a matter of the fold route, and the dock master, who had no vessel of his own inside the approach at a quarter past three on the night in question and the registry to prove it, had passed it to Eden's bureau as a matter of somebody else's vessel. Albercombe had read it, found that the only drives in Eden's berths that night which could be lit inside an approach belonged to two tugs registered at another of the five habitats, and sent it on to Calloway's office with a note that said, in its entirety, *not Eden's*. Calloway's office had sent it to Larsen with no note at all, which was Calloway's way of saying that the Commissioners had not yet been told and he would like to keep it that way.

Larsen had written notices of this exact shape. There had been a form like it on every fold-side posting he had held in the Survey Corps, four lines and a fifth for remarks, and he had been taught — by an officer whose name he could still have given, at a watch not unlike the one this had come from — that the fifth line was where the Rangers stopped and anyone's opinion began.

This one's fifth line was empty.

He noticed that before he noticed anything else, and he noticed it the way a man notices a door left exactly as wide as he needs. A transient of the drive class inside Eden's approach, eleven seconds, at fourteen minutes past three ring time, no transit cleared, the clearance list referenced by number. Nothing about which vessel. Nothing about why a drive would be lit inside an approach where lighting one was forbidden to everything that was cleared and impossible for everything that was not. Nothing about the hour.

The hour was the case. Larsen had worked Eden long enough to know when the dock master's office changed its staff, and a quarter past three was the one time of the ring's night when a tug could leave a berth and come back to it with nobody at the desk to log either, and a tug that did that, and lit its drive inside the approach to do it fast, was a tug carrying something that could not wait for the day shift to ask it a question. That was a Compact matter from end to end — another habitat's registry, an Eden berth, a dock master's rota, a cargo somewhere between — and the only reason he could take a single step of it was that the notice had told him none of it.

If the officer who wrote it had put the hour's meaning on the fifth line, he could not have used the notice. He would have been holding a Ranger's inference about a Compact vessel, from a service that had no authority inside the habitat and knew it, and any advocate the tug's owner could afford would have had the inference struck and the four lines above it struck with it. The notice crossed the wall because it carried nothing the wall was built to stop. It said what the instruments did. It said when. It left him to supply the rest from the side of the wall where the rest lived.

He did not know, and the notice did not say, whether the hour's meaning had been left off the fifth line because the writer had not seen it or because they had. The form did not distinguish. It was not supposed to. He thought, from the exactness of the four lines — the reference number, the class in the table's words — that it was the second, and he put that thought where he put the things he could not use, and wrote it nowhere.

He wrote to the dock master instead, for the berth log of the night, which he was entitled to, and sat for a while with the question of whether to tell Eden's Commissioner first, which in two years he had not made into a rule either way. Then he looked once more at the notice, at the oversight's seal where the watch's names would have stood, and found that he wanted to know who had left the line empty, and could not, and that this was correct.
