The berth log came back on the eighth day, complete, which Larsen had expected, and with the night rota attached, which he had not asked for.

Eden's dock master kept a good log. Every berth on the ring had a clamp, every clamp had a state, and every state was written down at the change of the ring's quarter-hour whether or not anything had changed, so that the log for a quiet night ran to the same length as the log for a busy one and could be read for its silences. Larsen read it for its silences. The two tugs — the other habitat's, on long lease at the paired berths at 22-Low, the only drives on the ring that night that could have been lit inside an approach — had been clamped at both berths from the end of second shift to the start of first, drawing keep-alive current at a draw that did not vary by a digit, and nothing on the ring had opened or closed between two and five ring time. Not a berth. Not a service hatch. The dock master had initialled the hour himself, which he did not usually do, and had added no remark, which he did.

The rota said what Larsen already knew. The night staff signed off at three. The day staff signed on at half past. Nobody had written anything in the half-hour between, because there had been nobody there to write it, and the rota had looked like that for longer than the dock master had been the dock master, and the dock master had attached it anyway, without comment, because a man who sends you a log for a night when nothing moved is a man who would like you to see that he knows what you were going to look for.

So the Compact's record of a quarter past three was complete. It said: everything clamped, everything drawing, nothing moving, nobody at the desk. It said the last part by omission, which is the only way a rota can say anything.

And the Corps' record of a quarter past three said that something artificial, in the drive band, had crossed the approach for a measured number of seconds, and said it six times over, on six principles, with a reference number.

Larsen put the two side by side and sat with the space between them, which was his trade, and found it was not the space he had been built for. The seams Five-O worked ran between habitats: two ledgers, each perfect, and a fact that lived in neither. This seam ran between the Compact and the Corps. On the one side a dock ring, which was the dock master's; on the other an approach, which was the Passage oversight's and had been since before the Compact had a Governor; and between them a hull plate, a hatch the residents walked past without seeing, and no office at all. He could write to the dock master, and had. He could not write to the oversight, which had already passed what it would ever pass and did not take questions from a Governor's task force about what its instruments had agreed on, and would have been right not to.

What he could do was go and look at the thing the paper described. He had been going to do that anyway — the way he did most things on Eden, he noticed, which was by turning up.

The other question, the one he had not made into a rule in two years, the log had answered for him. There was no case. There was a dock ring on which nothing had moved, and a man who wanted to stand on it at a quarter past three to see what nothing looked like, and a man who did that without saying so first was a man investigating something and declining to say what. He had resigned a commission over walls that let cases die quietly. He had not resigned it to be quiet on somebody else's deck.

He wrote to Eden's Space Commissioner. The letter was short, because there was nothing in it he could not support: that the task force had received, by way of the Chief Commissioner's office, a transient notice from the Passage oversight, reference attached; that he had obtained from the dock master the berth log of the night, reference attached, and that it showed nothing moving; that he would walk the dock ring between half past two and four ring time on the following night, at the paired berths at 22-Low and the dock master's office, in uniform; that he would open no berth, enter no office, ask no question of any member of the dock's staff, and open no case; and that the Commissioner would receive a second letter when he had finished, saying what he had done.

He read it once for anything it asserted about Eden, and found that it asserted nothing, and sent it.

:::pov zara-wayland

The letter reached Wayland before the thing it described, which in two years of the task force had not happened once.

It was not that Five-O came unannounced. Calloway's office kept a liaison line for exactly that, and the line worked: a visit reached her as a courtesy from the Chief Commissioner's office, usually on the day, occasionally the day after, in the flat register that office used for anything it would prefer not to be asked about. She had never minded. A Governor's task force with authority across five habitats was going to arrive on her dock from time to time, and the dual line she held had been designed by a Governor who understood that a Commissioner told afterwards was still a Commissioner told.

This was a letter from the Commander himself, a day ahead, and it was unlike anything the task force had sent her because it had nothing in it.

She read it twice, the second time for the sentence she could object to. A transient notice from the oversight, by reference. A berth log, by reference. A night, two berths, an office, a span of ninety minutes, and a list of things the Commander would not do on her dock, which was longer than the list of things he would. No case opened. No finding stated. No request. Nothing about the dock master's rota, which she had seen because the bureau's note on the oversight's notice had gone up through her office on its way to the Chief Commissioner's, with Albercombe's *not Eden's* on it, and which she had thought about for longer than the two words deserved. Nothing about the two tugs beyond their berth. The letter said what a man would do and when, and left the dock exactly as it had found it.

She could not object to it because there was nothing in it to object to, and she noted that this was not an accident of the drafting. A letter that asserted something about Eden was a letter she would have had to answer, upward and sideways, in two versions that said the same thing. This one she could send on unchanged.

She sent it to the Mayor's office, identically, with the time. She sent it to Oyelaran for information, since the bureau had already had the notice and would prefer to have the rest. She wrote *noted* on her own copy and the hour she had read it, and did not write that it was the first time, because the file was not the place for that and she had no other place.

:::pov larsen

At a quarter past three the dock ring was exactly as the log said, and Larsen stood on it in uniform and was, for about the length of a breath, the most interesting thing there.

A loader crew came off a hull at the far end of the rank and looked at him, the way a crew looks at a man standing still on a deck where nobody stands still. One of them read the jacket — the Governor's task force, the one with the name — and filed it, and the crew went back to lifting things, because a man standing still is not an event and the ring had a shift to finish. He had said in writing that he would be here, and the saying had reached the night staff the way written things reach a night staff: a man will be standing at 22-Low, leave him be. They left him be.

The paired berths at 22-Low held two tugs, clamped, each drawing keep-alive current at a single steady indicator on the clamp housing. He stood close enough to see that the indicators did not change and far enough that he was not inspecting anything, which was the line the letter had drawn. The hulls were cold, and dust lay on both in the thin, even way a dock lays it on anything that holds still. The log had said so.

The dock master's office was lit and empty. The desk had a cup on it. The door was open, since a dock master's office is not a thing anyone locks, and the rota on the wall said what it had said in his hand, and nobody came to the desk between three and half past, and he watched nobody come.

Beyond the ring, through the hull, six instruments were agreeing with each other every ninety seconds about an approach he could not see and had no standing to ask about. Nothing crossed it while he stood there. He had not expected anything to; a thing that happens once at a quarter past three does not owe you a second showing because you have come to the right hour in the right jacket. He stood until four, because he had said in writing that he would, and because standing there was the whole of what he was entitled to.

What he thought about, mostly, was the fifth line.

He had read it empty and seen a door left exactly as wide as he needed, and had been pleased with himself for seeing it. The hour was the case. He had thought that in so many words on the fourth day and supplied the rest from where the rest lived: the rota, the tugs, a cargo that could not wait for the day shift to ask it a question. He had written none of it down, and had been proud of that too.

The berth log had struck it anyway. Not the four lines, which stood under the oversight's seal on a record he would never read; those were observations and nobody had laid a finger on them. What the log had struck was his. The tugs had not moved. The hour had meant what it always meant, which was that nobody was at the desk, and that was a fact about a rota and not about a vessel, and he had made a vessel out of it on the strength of knowing the dock. He had kept the Rangers' side of the fifth line for the whole of a career and crossed it in the first minute of the first notice that reached him with the line blank — not on paper, which was what he had been taught to watch, but in the part of him that knew the hour.

Whoever had left the line empty had known the hour too. He was fairly sure of that, from the exactness of the four lines, and he was now fairly sure of something else: they had not left it empty for him. They had left it empty because they could not support it. That the emptiness had crossed the wall and been useful on the far side was true, and was not the reason, and he had mistaken the use for the reason because the use was the part that had reached him.

Four ring time. He left the way he had come, past the loader crew, who did not look up.

He wrote the second letter before the day staff signed on, so that it would reach the Commissioner's desk ahead of anyone who might mention that he had been there. He had walked the ring at the hour and the berths stated; he had found the dock as the berth log described it; he had opened nothing, asked nobody and entered no office; there was no case, and the transient stood on the Corps' record as what six instruments had recorded and on the Compact's as a night on which nothing moved, and no office on either side was entitled to call it anything else.

Then he added the sentence he had not made into a rule in two years, and made it one. *The task force will write to the Commissioner before it comes onto Eden's deck on its own account, each time, stating what it will do; the Commissioner may hold it to that.*

He read the letter once for anything in it he thought, and found only things he had done, and sent it.

:::pov zara-wayland

The second letter was on her desk before the first shift's filings, as the first had promised, and it said what he had done and nothing he had found, because he had found what the log said he would.

She read it once as Calloway would, for what it disposed of, and once as the Mayor would, for what it left on the dock, and the two readings were the same reading. A man had stood at two berths and an empty desk for ninety minutes and gone home. The tugs were where they had been. The dock master's cup, she supposed, was where it had been. There was a last sentence, and it was the only sentence in either letter that promised anything, and it promised it to her.

She sent it to the Mayor's office, identically, under the first. She did not send it to Oyelaran, because there was nothing in it for a bureau, and because the bureau had Albercombe, who would have read the last sentence and said, at volume, that it was not evidence of anything, and been right.

What stayed on the Corps' side of the ring stayed there. A measured number of seconds, a class in a table's words, a reference number, under a seal that carried no names: a thing six instruments had agreed about on a night when, by every record Eden held, nothing had moved. It would stay on a record she would never read, and nobody on her side of the hull was entitled to call it anything, and the one man who might have been had just written to tell her, in two letters, that he was not.

She put the two letters together in the file and wrote the hour on the second one. The Commander, she thought, had not sent her nothing. He had sent her the shape of it, with the middle left exactly where it was.
