::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa had slept on it, which was the only test the survey had ever trusted, and in the morning it looked the same, which is how you know.

"Today," said Nakagawa, at the station, before anyone had eaten. "The handheld goes. Here is how."

Ferrant had no objections, and Egede had several, and Nakagawa let Egede have them all in order, because a rule kept without the objections to it teaches nobody anything.

A knock is one person and a stone. That did not change. Aravena went, alone, with the stone to the front, as a hundred times. The handheld went on the chest strap, under the stone, pointed where the light pointed, which was low, at the silt and the feet, and not up, because the party had been looked at from the edge of its own light for eleven minutes once and Nakagawa was not going to do that to anyone from a hand's distance. It kept what the eye saw. It did not add anything to the knock. If the one on the line could tell a handheld from a light, then the one on the line would know a thing about the party that the party had not meant to say, and that was a cost, and Nakagawa had weighed it against seven weeks of calling a thing a fault and had decided the cost was smaller.

"Sound channel off," said Nakagawa.

"Why?" said Egede.

"Because there is a thing said at the line every shift that is not in the log, and it is not going to get into the log by accident because we strapped a recorder to the person who says it."

Aravena, who had gone a colour, did not say anything, and Ferrant looked at Nakagawa with an expression Nakagawa took a moment to place and then placed. It was the look of someone revising a grade upward.

"To the line," said Nakagawa. "Not past it. Set it down, step back, wait the interval, come back. Whatever is there."

"And if something is there," said Egede, who had said it before, a year ago, and had been answered then too.

"Then something is there," said Nakagawa, "and this time we will have kept it."

::::: scene 2

::: pov aravena

The squeeze was worse with the handheld. Everything is worse with one more thing. Aravena went in sideways with the stone turned lengthwise and the handheld pressing a hard square into the breastbone, and came out onto the silt with the light held low and the breath not back yet, and there was the one on the line, in the open, still, grey, large, as every shift.

And beside it, someone else.

An arm's length to the side. On the line. Facing the squeeze, the way the one on the line faced it. Upright, about the same size, still in the same way. Aravena's light was on the silt, low, finding the first one's feet the way it always found them, and Aravena's hand, without being told, took the light to the side.

It went round.

Aravena had looked at the imager's frame fifty times and had argued with Egede about the edge and had said *the light is going round it* and had not, until now, known what that meant. It meant this. The light lay on the silt and reached the place and did not go onto the shape the way it went onto the first one's grey fur and found the texture of it. It went round. A thin bright line, all the way round, and inside the line, nothing the light could land on. And on the silt behind, where the first one's shadow lay long and black across the ground in Aravena's light, there was no shadow at all. The silt was lit as if the place were empty.

The one on the line did not turn toward it. Aravena saw that. The one on the line looked at Aravena, the way it always did, and did not look to the side, and did nothing, with the whole body, the way it always did nothing, except that this time Aravena thought the nothing was being done on purpose.

Every part of Aravena that was not the eyes wanted to go back through the crack. Aravena knew this feeling. Aravena had had it once before, on this silt, a year ago, and had not gone back then, and had been answered.

So Aravena did the thing they had come to do.

Knelt. Set the stone on the line, on the silt, a hand from the first one's feet. Not from the other's. Aravena did not decide that; it was decided before Aravena was on the ground. Stood. Stepped back, one step, toward the crack. Said the thing, facing the one on the line, going up at the end.

Waited.

The one on the line bent, and put a hand to a rock at the edge of the silt, and moved it. The ground said so, through the silt and Aravena's boots, the way it had a hundred times.

The other one did nothing at all.

::::: scene 3

::: pov odile-ferrant

Ferrant had the handheld's feed on the junction screen, because it was her instrument and she had asked for it, and she watched the whole of it standing at the edge of the light with her hands empty, which was how she had decided to watch it.

Two shapes on the line. One with the texture of fur in the light and a thermal return she would have called an adult of the population on the file without being asked. One with an optical return, a rim where the lamp bent round it, and on the thermal channel, where the shape stood, the temperature of the silt.

She had read that page for a living for nine years and she had never once seen it stand next to something warm. The documented cases stood in corridors, on observation decks, at three in the morning in front of a watch officer with nothing to compare them to. This one stood on a line of silt beside a person, in a lamp held by another person, and the lamp went onto the one and round the other, and the difference was not an instrument reading. It was a thing a child could have seen.

Aravena knelt. Ferrant watched the stone go down a hand from the warm one's feet, and watched the frame hold, and watched the warm one bend, and felt the answer come up through the floor of the junction into her boots, the way she had felt it once before and would not, she thought, ever stop being surprised by.

The other one did not move. Not a frame.

Then Aravena turned, and the handheld turned with Aravena, and the last frames before the lamp went into the crack showed both of them still on the line, the warm one standing in its place and the other an arm's length beside it, in a light the handheld could just work by, that had no source and threw no shadow.

Ferrant did not say anything. Nakagawa did not say anything. Egede, for once, did not either.

What Ferrant was thinking, and would write in her own file and nowhere else, was this. Her grade did not change. A direct observation is better than an inference and the page she read for a living said exactly what the handheld had seen, and the grade she had filed already allowed for that, because she had filed it honestly. What had changed was not the grade. It was that she now knew, from a lamp, the thing the gravimeter and the imager and the page had all told her and none of them had made her feel: that it stood *beside*. Not in the people's place, not in the party's. Beside both, on the one line both had agreed to keep, asked by neither, answering neither.

She had a word for what it was consistent with. She did not have a word for that.

::::: scene 4

::: pov aravena

Aravena came out of the third passage with the light held low and the handheld pressing on the breastbone and a face that, Nakagawa said later, was the same face as a year ago with something added to it, and stopped, and said, in a voice that had not come back yet:

"Two."

"Yes," said Nakagawa. "We saw. Tell me. In order."

Aravena told it in order. The squeeze. The one on the line, as always. The other, beside, on the line, facing the crack. The light going round it, and no shadow. The one on the line not turning toward it. The kneeling, the stone, a hand from the first one's feet. The step back. The thing said, which Aravena did not repeat and nobody asked for. The rock moved. The other one not moving. The going.

"A hand from the first one's feet," said Ferrant. "You chose."

"I didn't choose. I knelt and it was already decided. You put a stone down for the one who answers."

"How did you know which one that was?"

Aravena thought about it, because it was a real question and Ferrant was the kind of person who only asked those.

"The light went onto one of them," said Aravena, "and round the other. You can't put a stone down for a thing the light goes round. There's nothing there to put it down for."

Ferrant said nothing for a moment, and then said, "That is going in my file, in your words, if you'll let me."

"It's not a reading."

"No. It's better than one." And then, to Nakagawa: "The grade doesn't move. I want that said now, at the junction, before anyone has slept on anything. Direct observation, reading unchanged. I'll write it tonight. The one thing I learned on that screen I can't write anywhere, and I'm telling you so that it's said once: it stood beside. I have a page for what it is. I don't have one for that."

"Neither do we," said Nakagawa.

"No," said Ferrant. "I'd noticed."

Aravena knelt to the unit, because there was a shift to log and that was still the job, and said, "Log. Shift one-four-two. Upper junction. Party of three and one. Knock answered," and stopped, because that was not the whole of it, and the unit hummed, low, once, to say it had heard, and waited, the way it always waited, for the rest.

::::: scene 5

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa wrote the log at the junction, standing up, in the survey lead's own hand, because this one was not Aravena's to write and never had been.

*Party announced itself at the line: one member, one stone of the upper levels, set down on the line and left; handheld carried, pointed low, sound channel off. The one who answers was present on the line, in the open, and answered: a shift of rock at the edge of the silt. A second shape was present on the line, at arm's length of the first, upright; optical return, no thermal return, no shadow thrown; it did not answer, did not move, and was present when the light withdrew. Nothing said is recorded. The line was not crossed by any party.* And under it, in the survey lead's own column, nothing. There was no count for this. It was not a thing you waited by.

Ferrant wrote her line beneath. *Direct observation. Reading unchanged.* Then she went up to the station and wrote the rest in her own file, where it would be read by people who knew what the grade meant, with Aravena's sentence in it in Aravena's words, and the gravimeter's excursion at the same minute, and the page's name, and *consistent with* in front of it, which is not the same word as *is* and never will be.

"What now?" said Egede, who had asked that a year ago too.

"Now nothing changes," said Nakagawa. "We knock. We work. We don't go down. The handheld goes with the stone from now on, pointed low, sound off, and the log says what it sees. Same as always, with one more thing kept."

"And that?"

"That is on the line," said Nakagawa, "beside the one who answers, and it is not ours and it is not theirs, and the only person on this planet who has a page for it has told us the page does not say what it is, and we are going to believe her, because she is the one who is trusted to be right about it."

Later, in the wind, Nakagawa sat with it, and did not think it had been slow.

Two years to learn to knock. A third to learn that the line the party had drawn with its own feet, and the people below had kept from their side, and both had answered across for a year, could have a third thing standing on it that neither side had asked and neither could ask, and that both would go on knocking anyway, because the knock was for the one who answered and had never been for anything else. What the people below had made of it, Nakagawa did not know and the log said so. They had not been told. They had not told. The stone was where Aravena had set it, a hand from the feet of the one who answers, and it would be there in the morning, and so would the one who answers, and the party would knock.

That was the whole of what the survey knew, and it was a great deal, and Nakagawa filed it and put the light out and slept, which is the only test there is.
