::::: scene 1

:::pov iklix-varn

The first thing the watch teaches a Deputy is that it does not watch anything.

Varn had expected instruments, and there were instruments — six of them, cross-correlated, filing their agreement with each other every ninety seconds into a log nobody on the watch read, because the instruments read it themselves. What the watch did was stand a shift in a compartment on the fold-approach side of Eden's ring, behind a hatch the habitat's residents walked past without seeing, and be the nearest qualified officer to the event if the event came. It had not come in Field Officer Smith's tenure. She had said so on Varn's first day, flatly, as a fact about the posting and not as a comfort, and Varn had understood that it was neither. It was the job.

On the ninth day the job was a form.

Structural work on Eden that touches the approach monitoring — the mounts, the conduits, anything the six instruments hang from — is certified twice. The habitat certifies it, because the habitat is a Compact member and the ring is its own. The watch countersigns, because the fold route is not the Compact's and the instruments belong to a record older than the Compact. Two signatures, one job, and the Corps' signature is the second because the Corps has no standing to be first. Smith explained this the way she explained everything, with exactly what could be checked and nothing that could not, and then she said the contractor's log had not come up with the certification copy, and that the countersign needed both, and that Varn would go and get it.

Varn went. A Deputy does not ask why a log is missing. A Deputy notices that the officer did not say *lost*.

The Certification Office was on the Compact side of the ring, which meant the tab at Varn's collar was a courtesy there and not a rank. The clerk was courteous back. The work in question was a run of new members under the dock ring — the understructure the approach mounts bolt through — cut by a Federation contractor crew, four Chthonari, and certified sound on the habitat's own monitoring three weeks ago. The certification copy said so in the ordinary way: *structural, within tolerance; approach monitoring, reading normal; no items.* The clerk found the contractor's log in a second file, apologised for it, and printed it.

It was longer than the certification. Varn read it standing up, which is how a Deputy reads anything they have not been told to sit down with, and found that the two documents agreed about everything the certification had asked. The log had one more line. It was in the crew's own hand, dated the last day of the work, and it said: *Approach mount three, understructure: rings under load. Noted. Not a certification item.* Somebody in the office had initialled beside it and written *n/a*.

Varn did not know what *rings under load* meant to a person who read a structure through their feet. Varn did know what *not a certification item* meant, because it was the sentence the whole ring was built out of: a thing that was true and belonged to nobody. The clerk had not hidden it. The clerk had filed it, which is different, and worse, and not the clerk's fault.

Varn thanked the clerk, took both copies, and walked back around the ring to the side where the tab meant something, carrying a line that did not.

:::pov zoe-smith

Smith read the log the way she read an approach trace: for what it matched, and for what it did not.

It matched the certification in every particular the certification named. That was the first entry in her own log, and she made it before anything else, because a Deputy who has just found something needs to watch the officer write down first that most of it is nothing. Then she wrote the line that did not match, in the crew's words and not hers, and beside it the office's *n/a*, and beside that the only thing the watch could truthfully add: *not within the watch's competence to assess; not within the certification's remit to record; observed by the contractor and struck by the habitat.*

Varn was standing where Deputies stand, which is one step further from the desk than they need to be. She did not say to sit. She asked what *rings under load* meant, and Varn said *I don't know*, and she said that was the correct answer and the only one she would have accepted, and watched the Deputy not know what to do with being right by having nothing.

There were three things she could do with the line, and she declined two of them.

She could countersign. The certification was complete on its own terms, the monitoring read normal, and the watch signs to say the record is in order, not to say the ring is. The signature would be honest. It would also make the line disappear under it, and a thing that disappears under an honest signature is the one kind the Corps has never learned to find again.

She could refer. A referral from the watch went to the Starwarden, and from the Starwarden, if it went anywhere, to a habitat that had already written *n/a* and would write it again with more people in the room. The evidence supported none of it: a monitoring record that had read normal for as long as she had stood here, and one contractor's note that the habitat had considered and set aside. She had seen officers refer on less, and seen what it cost the next officer who needed to be believed.

So she did the third thing, which was the watch's actual job and the reason the posting existed. She logged it, flat, timestamped, in a form that could be checked: *Countersign withheld pending clarification of contractor's note. Not a finding. No further action by this watch.* Then she sent the Deputy back to ask the Certification Office, on the Corps' letterhead and politely, what *n/a* had meant — not whether the note was true, which was not the watch's question, but who had decided it was not a question.

Varn asked, from the hatch, whether the crew could be asked directly.

"Not by us," Smith said. "They are the Compact's contractors on the Compact's ring. We are a courtesy there." She turned back to the trace. "Find out whose question it is. It is somebody's. It always is."
