:::pov iklix-varn

On the thirtieth day the ring was a habitat again.

It had been one all along, Varn supposed, and the Corps' engineers under the dock ring and the raven over it and the two shifts of the inspection had been the exception, the way the event would be, if it came. They had gone when the inspection named was done. The Compact side had its ring back and its certification corrected and its remedial work scheduled, and the office two hundred metres round the curve had filed something and closed it, and the hatch sat where it sat, between a door with a name on it and a door with a plant outside it, and the two residents who went past it as Varn came along at the hour did not look at it, which was the posting's whole design, and which Varn now found, without having decided to find it, a comfort.

Thirty days. Varn had not been counting. The log had, in a dozen hands going back years and one Academy hand going back a month, every entry saying what had happened: the instruments, the hatch, the officers, and on one day a story with a duration, and on nine of them a line that belonged to nobody, carried up the ring and set on a desk and left there until the record said what the structure carried. Varn had read back through it standing, that morning, before the shift, for no reason the Academy would have recognised, and had found that the month had a shape, and that the shape was not the shape of nothing.

Varn used the handle, which was right.

Smith looked up and said, "Varn," as a fact, and there was a form on the desk that had not been there the day before, printed, because a form on a reader is a form until the reader fails. Varn did not read it upside down. A Deputy does not read what is on an officer's desk, and besides, Varn knew what it was. The Corps asked for things at the end of a month. The Academy had said so, in the voice the Academy used for things that would happen to you and that you could not affect.

Varn went to the desk and stood.

The six lights were the colour they were. The rack hummed at the pitch it hummed at. Somewhere past the hatch the ring turned, which nobody in the compartment could feel and everybody knew. Varn stood at the desk the way Varn had stood at it for thirty days, and waited for the shift to begin, and it was a while before Varn noticed that Smith had not turned back to the form.

She was looking at the floor. Not at Varn. At the place on the floor where Varn's boots were.

Varn looked down too, and saw nothing, because there was nothing to see: a floor, lit evenly from nowhere in particular, and a pair of boots for grating standing on it. Then Varn looked at the desk, and at the distance between the desk and the boots, and understood that it was the wrong distance, and had been for about a minute, and that Varn had not put it there.

One step. Not the honest step, the one a Deputy stands from a desk because a Deputy does not know where they stand. Nearer. Where a person stands when they have come to a desk to do a job at it.

Varn had not decided that. Varn would have remembered deciding it. Varn had come through the hatch after a month of a log and a line and a countersign and the Corps leaving the ring, and had walked to the desk, and had stopped where Varn stopped, and it had been here.

The Academy teaches a Deputy to correct a posture when it is noticed. Varn did not move. Moving would have been a report, and not a true one.

:::pov zoe-smith

The form was the Corps' and the Corps wanted it by the end of the month, and she had been going to write it at the end of the shift, the way she wrote everything, because an assessment records and a shift is a thing you record after it has been stood.

Then her Deputy came through the hatch and stood at the desk, and she looked up to say the name, and said it, and looked down, because something was different and she was an officer who noticed the small things before she could have said what they were.

The step.

Thirty days. Fourteen Deputies before this one, and every one of them had stood one step too far from the desk for the first weeks, and she had stopped telling them to come closer years ago, because the step was not a mistake. It was a report: an honest account of where a Deputy thought they stood. The ones who moved it on their own were the ones she remembered, and none of them had moved it soon, and none of them had moved it in front of her. They had come in one morning standing somewhere else, and she had noted it, and said nothing, and written it in an assessment the Corps read and the Deputy never did.

Varn was standing somewhere else.

Not forward. She had seen the over-correction in Deputies too, the one who understood a step had said something and wanted it to say the opposite, and that was a different report and not a true one. This was not that. This was a person who had walked to a desk to do a job at it and stopped where a person stops, and had not, as far as she could tell from the face, known until a minute ago that the place had moved.

She had never said it. To any of them. It was not an entry; it was the kind of small thing the assessments were built out of, and the whole merit of an assessment was that it recorded what the Deputy did and what was observed and never once told the Deputy what it meant, because the Deputy would be told what it meant by the next thirty years and did not need it from her.

She said it.

"You moved the step."

Varn looked at the floor, and then at her, and said, carefully, in the voice that said *nothing happened* on the first day and had learned since to say what could be supported: "I did not decide to."

"No," she said. "They never do. The ones who decide to are standing somewhere they have been told to stand. You are standing where you are." She picked the form up, because her hands wanted something, and put it down again. "I have had fourteen of you. Three moved that step on their own. None of them in front of me. I have never said this to one of you before, and I am saying it now because the Corps has asked me what you did this month, and I am going to write that on the thirtieth day you stood at the desk, and I would rather you heard it from me than read it in a file in ten years and wondered why nobody mentioned it."

Varn said nothing, which was correct, and did not move, which was also correct, and she turned back to the form and wrote, in the hand the boards trusted because it never predicted anything, what the Deputy had done.

What the Deputy had done was a month. Stood a watch that did not watch anything. Struck three words with one line so they could still be read. Carried a line up the ring that belonged to nobody and set it on this desk and let it sit there for seventeen days without once asking her to make it belong to somebody. Written a story into the log with a duration. Asked a resident his name. Stood, on the thirtieth day, where a person stands.

She did not write what it meant. The Corps had never once asked her that, and she had never once told it, and the boards trusted her for exactly that reason.

::::: scene 2

:::pov iklix-varn

The shift was the shift. The lights held. At some point in the middle of it, when the form had gone into the tray and the compartment had gone back to being a compartment, Varn asked the question that had been held since the seventh day, because the answer was standing in front of Varn and the Academy had been wrong about that being a reason to wait.

"What is it for?"

Smith did not ask *what is what for*. She waited, which was still worse.

"The story. The girl at the well. You said the officer who had you first told it to you, and that you have told it to all of us, and that you have never told a Deputy what it is for." Varn kept the voice level. "I have had it for three weeks. I have stood a watch with it. I have carried a log up the ring with it. I would like to know what it is for."

Smith put the pen down, which she did when a shift was over, and it was not over.

"I do not know," she said.

Varn waited for the rest of the sentence. There was a rest of it, and it was not what the Academy would have had.

"I was never told. She told it to me at a desk like this one, at the end of a shift like this one, on a station that no longer stands, and did not say why, and I did not ask, and then she was dead and there was nobody to ask. I thought for a year it was about the well. I thought for another year it was about the thing that came. I have told it fourteen times since, and every time I have told it I have been waiting for one of you to tell *me*. That is the honest answer. It is handed across this desk as a question. It was never an answer. I have never said that to a Deputy, because I thought it would spoil it, and I am saying it to you because you asked in the right order and I am tired of being the only one who knows I do not know."

Varn stood where Varn was standing, which was nearer than the first day, and held that, and did not put it down.

"The first day," Smith said. "I told you the watch does not watch anything. It stands where it is, and it is the nearest qualified officer to the event if the event comes. I have said that to every one of you, as a fact about the posting. It is a fact about the posting. It is also advice. I have never said that part either." She picked the pen up. "You worked it out in a corridor on the eighth day. I watched you do it through a hatch on the latch. I am only telling you now so that it has been said."

At the end of the shift she turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it."

Varn wrote the instruments. The hatch, opened once and closed once. The officers, named. No event. Then the pen stopped over the page for the length of one agreement between the instruments, and Varn thought about a form in a tray and a step on a floor and a question with no answer handed across a desk, and did not write any of it, because none of it was the watch's, and somebody who was not here could check every word of what was.

Smith read it as though it had been longer, and initialled it, and said nothing at all, and this time Varn understood that the nothing was not declining. It was the rank.

At the hour Varn went out through the hatch, and the corridor was doing what the corridor did, which was not look, except for one person, who was crouched at a plant with nothing in his hands.

"My mother wants to know," Noah said, standing up, too fast, "whether the hatch is staying. Now the Corps have gone. She saw them go, the engineers, from the Compact side. She said she didn't know the Corps had been on the ring until they left, and then she asked me whether that meant you were leaving too, and I said I'd ask." He stopped. "So I'm asking."

Varn stood where Varn was, which was one step further from him than there was any reason to stand, and left it there, because that one was a different step and had been held on purpose since the eighth day, and said what could be supported.

"The hatch is not the Corps that came," Varn said. "It is the one that stays."

Noah thought about that, and nodded, and went in at the door with the plant outside it, and Varn walked on along the ring, and it curved gently away in both directions, and nobody looked at the hatch, and that was what happened.
