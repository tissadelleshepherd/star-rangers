The tab came off with a thumbnail, the way it was built to.

Six bars, pale grey-green, the Survey track's colour, the colour her collar had carried since the rotation began, read by nobody at Halyx who had not already read the order it came with. She had it in her palm before the transport was on approach, because the order had a second page and she had read the second page on the first night out and every night since, and it said the same thing each time. *Section Lead, general operations, Threshold Station, effective on taking up the post.* The post was hers when she reported. She was not on duty until then, and the collar was bare, and that was the whole of the interval the Corps gave a person between one rank and the next: the length of a dock.

She seated the new one. Seven bars, slate blue, the colour she had worn on this collar two years ago with two bars on it, and had worn with three at Eden before the Survey had taken her sideways. The magnet took and the tab sat proud of the cloth, visibly removable, as the charter required, so that nobody could mistake it for something sewn.

*Seven,* Tobble said, inward, before she had asked. *Counted twice. Still seven.*

She had not asked. She had not needed to; counting a thing twice was his, and he did it whether or not anybody wanted the number. She was not on duty until she had reported, and she had not reported, and so she let him.

*The colour,* she said.

*Slate. It was slate before Halyx. The record shows a return to a previous value.* Then nothing, which was his way of saying a matter was closed, and she left it closed.

The transport put her off at an inboard dock and the air told her where she was before the signage did. Trace ozone, a fraction above station standard, the thing the crews called Threshold Stink and visitors called nothing because it had gone by the second breath. Two years, and the air had not changed. She stood a moment with her kit at her feet and let it go by, and then she picked up the kit and went to find the Chief Ranger's office, which she had never once had cause to enter when she was a cadet on this station, and which was now the first door the order required of her.

Her file had come ahead of her. She knew what was in it, because she had written most of it, and all of it was shorter than the rotation it covered, because the rotation had held a great deal she had not been entitled to file and she had not filed it. Somewhere on the Halyx record a telemetry log stayed unmodified under her name. Somewhere further off, a council that spoke in chemistry and percussion had thanked her for a method that cost nothing to receive. Neither was coming to Threshold. The Survey track kept its own files, and she had rotated off it in the length of a dock.

The certifications stayed. A track was assigned and a certification was held, and the Survey's examinations she had sat were hers whatever colour the collar said. What she gave up was not the standing. It was the queue, and the ear's work, and the right to be asked about a pulse under her own name by an office that would never ask her again.

She knocked, and was told to come in, and went in.

:::pov arashobal

The officer's file was shorter than her rotation. Arashobal had read it twice, once for what it held and once for the length, and the length was the more informative reading.

Most files from a Survey rotation were longer than the rotation. A human on a liaison posting at the edge of someone else's jurisdiction filed what happened and then filed what it had meant, and the second half was where the drift lived: a reading becoming a finding between one page and the next, a resemblance becoming a connection, a council's thanks becoming an endorsement. This one had filed what happened. A reclassification, and the chamber that ratified it. A method, documented, four paragraphs, requested by a reef-city and sent. An assessment that said *the officer has no finding on authorship and enters none*, which was a sentence Arashobal's own people would have written and almost no human did. Then two lines in an Administrator-Voice's office, and the transport. Where the file could have said what the officer had thought the pulse was, it said nothing, and the nothing was not an omission. It had edges. A Krenyi could read the edges of a thing left out as plainly as the thing, and what these edges said was that the officer had held an opinion for one winter and one spring and had declined, every time she was asked, to let it onto a record.

Arashobal would have let the Sentinel go; the Survey kept it, and she held the division correct. What she kept was the shape of the officer who had not let it go and had not claimed it either, because that shape was going to run a section of hers by the end of the afternoon.

The human came in, and stood, and did not fill the room with herself. Seven bars on the collar, new enough that the cloth under the magnet still lay flat. The kit bag was set down against the wall and not in the way. She did not look about the office. She looked at Arashobal, and then at the desk, where the section's roster lay, and then at Arashobal again, and waited to be told what she had come for, which she already knew, and which Arashobal told her anyway, at the speed a human two years off this station and one hour off a transport could take it.

"The section is the department's watch on the outer docks," Arashobal said. "Dock Seven and the docks inboard of it, as far in as the department's watch runs. The watches on it are yours to set. The Rangers on the watches are yours to answer for. The transit windows through the boundary are stated from your desk, and the pilot department flies what you state." She let that stand long enough to be read. "The readings are the boundary desk's, the Survey's own. The window is yours. You will be asked for it by people who would rather have it from the Survey, and the Survey will not give it, because stating a window is not a reading and they do not make claims that are not readings. Neither will you. That is the material part."

The officer said, "Yes, Chief."

"The section had a Lead until the spring. It has had its Principals since. They have kept it; they have not stated a window, because a Principal does not. There is a window due in the summer traffic plan and the plan was written before anyone asked the readings." Arashobal did not add what she thought of the plan. It was not hers to state, and a Chief who stated a Section Lead's window for her had made the window the Chief's. "You will read the readings before you state it."

"All of them, Chief."

"All of them." That was not drift. It was the opposite of drift: a stated term and the conduct that would match it, offered before anyone had asked for the match. Arashobal registered it and filed it under nothing, because the Krenyi did not keep a human's good habit until the habit had shown its use, and the habit had the week to show it in.

The human asked one question. She asked it in the form that could be answered in one sentence, which was the form the file had taught Arashobal to expect from her. "What does the section answer for that its Principals do not."

"Who is on the watch," Arashobal said. "A Principal answers for a watch. You answer for who you put on it, and for what it costs them, and for noticing that before they report it, because they will report it late."

The officer did not say *yes, Chief* to that. She nodded, once, in the manner of a person entering a line she had already been carrying, and Arashobal, who could not un-notice anything, noticed that the nod was the first thing in the interview that had not been offered to her. It had been taken.

She gave her the roster. The human read it standing, all of it, which took some minutes, while Arashobal went on with the department's own work and did not watch her read it. There was no need to. A body that was its own instrument did not have to look at a thing to know it was still.

The officer thanked her and took up her kit and went. The door closed. Arashobal, who had known the outer docks were thin since the hour she had first stood on them, and had nothing to file about it, and would have nothing to file about it tomorrow, filed the section as handed over and returned to the summer traffic plan, which was going to be rewritten, and which she would not be the one to say so.

The general operations log came up to the Chief's desk at the turn of the station's day, every section's, with the initials on it.

Dock Seven's carried two. The cadet's, under each line, as a cadet's did. And under the time line, a second: the new Section Lead's, at an hour the log recorded and no roster had set, which meant she had gone down to the dock on her first evening on the station with no watch to take and no reason on any sheet.

Arashobal read the page for what had been struck, and nothing had. She read it for what had been added, and nothing had. The time line carried the station's time, from the left-hand instrument, marked, as a cadet on that dock was told to mark it on the first watch now. The second initial sat under it and claimed nothing further.

She had known, from the file, that this officer did not write the sentence after the sentence. She had not known that the officer would go and stand where the sentence was and not write it there either. The Quiet-Built kept no fact whose use was not yet shown, and this one had shown its use in the first evening, so she kept it.

:::pov tissadelle

Dock Seven kept two times, and the watch log had one line for it, and the line had one reading on it.

She had come down at no hour a cadet could learn, because that was how the Principal had come down when the watch was hers, and because a Section Lead who had been given the watches to set and the people on them to answer for was owed one look at the dock before she set anything. Not present, and entirely responsible. She had not understood the manner when she was on the receiving end of it, and she had copied it anyway, as one copies a thing one has been read by, and she found, with the log open on the sill between her and the cadet, that she understood it now, in the way a person understands a word by having to say it.

The cadet had stood up. She told the cadet to sit, and the cadet sat.

The two chronometers were where they had been. One above the pressure seal and one above the inner hatch, the station's and the other one, each of them certain. She did not look at them yet. She read the log, standing, back from tonight's line, page by page, at the speed she read anything she was going to initial, and the time line carried one reading, every watch, marked *station time*, left-hand instrument, in a cadet's hand that was not hers and had never written two.

"The log is kept in station time, Section Lead," the cadet said. Not asked; offered, the way a thing is offered that was learned on the first night and has not once been needed since. "The left-hand instrument."

"Yes," Shepherd said.

Somebody had told this one. On the first watch, by the sound of it, which was forty watches sooner than anyone had told her, and the telling had been correct, and the gap she had stood in for forty nights writing two times with a stroke between them had been closed by somebody who had read her log and understood what the form had failed to say. The practice had not outlived her. The form had been explained instead, which was the right fix and the smaller one, and the smaller one was the kind that held.

Then she looked at the right-hand instrument.

Forty seconds. She had counted it on forty-one nights and it had not moved, and it had not moved since, through a survey that had read two centuries of a lighthouse keeper's corrections and come back with the same number and no cause. Thirteen years the station had carried it. It was outside the log by standing practice. It was certain, and it was on the wall, and it was not written anywhere a Section Lead initialled, and nobody on this dock was going to write it unless she said so.

She had a sentence. She noticed that the way she had noticed a sentence at Halyx with her hand already drafting it, and it was the same sentence in a different uniform: that there was a second reading in this room, and that nothing on the sheet in front of her showed anyone had been asked. It was true. It was also not the log's. The log recorded the watch, and the watch was the station's, and the station had decided thirteen years ago which instrument was the station's and had never been wrong about which one it had decided. A Section Lead who wrote the right-hand reading into the time line under her own initial had not corrected anything. She had made a claim about what the dock was for, on a form that asked for the time, under the initial of a cadet who had done exactly as told.

What was hers was the window. Not the clock. The window was stated from her desk, and the readings that put its edges there were the Survey's, and the Survey had thirteen years of them, and she would read every one before she stated anything, and when she stated it she would state the width the readings supported and not the width the traffic plan had been written around before anyone asked. That was the sentence she was entitled to. It was due in the summer. It was not due tonight.

Tonight she was entitled to an initial.

She wrote it under the cadet's, on the time line, with nothing struck and nothing added, and closed the log, and told the cadet the watch was in good order, which it was. The right-hand instrument read forty seconds later than the log as she went up, as it had on her first night, and it was outside the log by standing practice, and the practice was hers now to let stand.
