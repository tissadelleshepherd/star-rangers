:::pov achebe

The case was eleven days old, weighed about four kilograms, and was hers.

That last part mattered more than she was going to say aloud in a bureau where Detective Inspector Albercombe's compliance queue was famous two decks in every direction. The referral had come down through the Senior Sergeant with the annotation *suitable for development*, which is how a bureau says *boring* to somebody it is being kind to, and it read exactly as boring as anything that has ever fallen off the bottom of that queue: a dry-stores discrepancy in the Deck 7 galley. Flour. Cooking oil. Root vegetables. Once, inexplicably, a tin of a spice nobody on the deck could pronounce, which the steward's report had valued at almost nothing and Achebe had privately valued higher, because a thief who takes the worthless thing is a thief whose shopping list you should be reading more carefully.

The difficulty was not the evidence. The difficulty was the reporting. The steward's first loss report said four kilograms a week. His second said two, with a note that the first had probably overcounted. His third proposed, in the defeated tone of a man asking you to agree with him, that the galley's scale might want recalibrating, and would the bureau prefer to close the matter pending that. Every statement he gave was a smaller statement than the last. Witnesses usually grow their stories. Hers was shrinking, and she did not yet have a word for what that meant.

Her interview technique was the one she had learned at home, from the best interviewer she knew: sit still, say nothing, and let the other party talk themselves into a decision. It worked on everyone in the galley except the steward, who could not be waited into saying more because more was the direction he was walking away from. So she canvassed the regulars instead, morning by morning, and on the fourth morning she reached the table nearest the warm-air vent, where the author sat — the one whose books about [Smart Pets](/star-rangers/glossary/smart-pet/) got read to half the children on the habitat, including, at one remove, to a rabbit who had opinions about none of it.

:::pov kate-ukrainka

The detective was young, sat down without her slate, and did not open with a question, and Kate liked all three of those things more than she intended to be useful about.

"I should save you the trouble," Kate said. "I've been asked. I'm no good. I don't notice events — I notice people. If somebody had carried a sack of flour past this table I'd have missed the sack and remembered that they walked like their shift had been doubled. You want a witness to things, and I am a witness to the other part."

The detective said that was all right, and then said nothing further, in a way Kate recognised the shape of at once, because she had written it. Sat still. Said nothing. Let the other party talk themselves into a decision. It was in her second book; a rabbit does it to a customs officer. She considered saying so, and instead — because silence that patient does eventually get what it is patient for — heard herself begin to talk.

"Your steward," she said. "I've watched him give you three statements. I couldn't hear a word of them and I don't need to. Each one asked you for less than the one before. Less flour, less concern, less of your time — he's revising himself downward, and people don't do that because they're lying to you. They do it because the true answer costs somebody, and they mind about the somebody, and the only thing they can think to make smaller instead is the question." She stopped there, one sentence past where she should have, which was one sentence further than she had managed in years of being careful. "None of that is evidence. I'd rather it wasn't written down."

The detective had not written it down. Kate noted — professionally, against her will — that this was how you could tell it would be kept.

She watched the young woman thank her and go, unhurried, in the direction of the dry stores rather than the steward, which meant she had understood the actual content of what Kate had said: don't press the man, find the somebody. A decent character, Kate thought. The rabbit she'd heard about was a better one. She resolved to put neither of them in a book, in the particular way she had resolved such things before, with the next book already quietly disagreeing with her.

:::pov knopka

The clinic visit was over. The clinic was two doors past the big food room, and the carrier stopped in the big food room, because the tall one who brings carrots always has one more thing to do.

There was a warm.

The warm was low down, near the floor, at the bottom of a wall, behind the shelf that smelled of flour. Warm is for sitting against. Knopka sat against it.

Then there was lunch.

Lunch was not supposed to be now. Now was after-clinic, which is not a food time; Knopka's whole day is arranged around which times are food times, and this was the first thing she had ever known before anyone else in the room. Lunch was also not supposed to be *there*. Lunch was behind the wall. Bread, warm; something with onions in it, warm.

Knopka looked at the wall.

The tall one looked at Knopka. Knopka looked at the wall. That is the whole of what happened, and it was enough.

:::pov achebe

The bureau's experimental record on her rabbit stood at: noticed lunch, every time; noticed nothing else, ever. Achebe had defended that record to three colleagues as a perfect detection rate on a narrow instrument, and she stood in dry stores watching the instrument point at a blank wall, and did the arithmetic. If Knopka said there was food behind that wall, then there was food behind that wall. The warm patch she was sitting against settled the second question, which was whether it was anybody's leftovers or an appliance somebody was running.

The panel behind the flour shelving came away by hand — maintained hinges, renewed seals, the housekeeping of a person who intended no harm and expected no company. Behind it, a proofing pantry from the galley's last refit but one, absent from the current register the way old rooms on Eden sometimes are; and in the pantry, a warming cabinet, clean as a clinic, holding two covered meals.

She did not call it in. She put the panel back, sat down on a sack of the flour that kept not being stolen, and practised her interview technique on the room until the junior cook came at end of shift to collect the meals and found a detective and a rabbit waiting, one of them asleep.

The story came out the way true stories do, all at once and in the wrong order. A grandmother, moved aboard two years ago when her old habitat closed its berths. A ration category misfiled somewhere in transit — a code for *visitor, temporary* where a code for *resident, dependent* should have been, feeding her at half-entitlement ever since. A correction form that could not be filed without triggering the one thing the family feared, a residency review for an old woman with nowhere to be reviewed back to. And a steward who had worked out most of it months ago and had been quietly revising his own reports downward ever since, trying to shrink the question until it fit inside nothing.

Theft requires someone to have taken something the record says existed. The record was the crime scene, and the record was wrong. Achebe spent a day and a half walking the correction through the ration registry herself — the category code, the berth-transfer paperwork, a residency confirmation that confirmed instead of reviewed — and on the way she checked, because Eden teaches you to check, whether any of it touched the famous manifest business the task force had ridden out of the habitat on. It did not. It was four kilograms of flour a week, going where rations go: to a resident. Her closure report was one page long and recommended no charges, on the grounds that there was no longer anything on the station's books for a charge to be about.

It would not make her name. By every metric the bureau kept, it was still not a case worth remembering. She remembered it. She remembered, too, passing the evidence shelf on her way to file it and finding the station cat sitting on the cabinet top with the settled air of an auditor closing a satisfactory year — he looked at the report, and at the rabbit in the carrier, and filed whatever he filed wherever he files it, and volunteered, as ever, nothing.

Knopka got the better carrot. Fair is fair; of the two of them, only one had found anything by trying.
