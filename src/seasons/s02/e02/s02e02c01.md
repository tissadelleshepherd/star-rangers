:::pov barsik

The agency's doctrine on reporting is two words long — *filed, not volunteered* — and the trainee had asked, reasonably, what the doctrine was for a finding that two different chains of command each owned half of.

The honest answer was that the doctrine had never anticipated the question, because the doctrine had never anticipated Eden, where a corridor could misbehave under one authority while its door misbehaved under another. So Barsik did what the agency does when doctrine runs out: he made the report exist in the places where the right readers already were, and let each half look like it had arrived by itself.

The corridor was the easy half. A run of tunnel that measured forty metres on some passes and forty-one on others, inside a habitat whose fold-adjacent clearances are the Passage oversight's to certify, was Safety Corps business the moment anyone with standing read it. The problem was getting it read. Eden's compliance framework had a documented digestion time of months, and the oversight's own queue was not much livelier — except for one desk. The recertification bundles for the Deck 5 environmental sensors crossed the rotation officer's desk weekly, and this rotation's officer read them. All of them. Annexes included. Barsik had confirmed this personally, from the top of a filing cabinet, over three consecutive weeks, because a plan that depends on somebody reading an annex is a plan that depends on a very rare kind of somebody. The survey extract went into the bundle as an unattributed instrument-anomaly annex, calibration certificate attached, and Barsik went back to the cabinet top to watch it land.

He knew a little about her, the way the station cat knows a little about everyone. New. Tír na nÓg, by the vowels. She had been on Eden before the uniform — a year on the dock circuit, one of the bands that played the loading bays — and dock-circuit people knew where the habitat breathed, which was more than could be said for most of the oversight's postings. And she was the only human on Eden who had never once tried to pet him. She said *good evening* instead, when they passed, in the tone one shift uses to another. He had no file that explained her, and had noted, in the file he was therefore obliged to open, that this was the most interesting thing about her.

The door was the other half, and the door was civil. A structure in a Compact tunnel, absent from Compact plans, was the detective bureau's to want — but the bureau could not be handed a survey by a cat, for reasons that were load-bearing to everything else the agency did on this station. So the door went in the other way: as a maintenance discrepancy, plans versus survey, door count fourteen versus door count fifteen, routed into the compliance queue that empties onto Detective Inspector Albercombe's desk — the queue whose referrals are famous for being exactly as boring as they read.

The trainee had questioned this too. Her point, filed with her usual economy, was that disguising a true report as a boring one seemed like the opposite of reporting. His answer went into her training log, because it was the closest thing to a first principle the Eden posting had: *on this habitat, boring is the only classification that gets investigated by someone competent. Interesting goes to a committee.*

Both copies landed inside a week. The trainee's follow-on exercise was to observe what a report does after it is filed — an exercise most agents never complete, because most reports do nothing, observably, forever. He did not tell her that. Some findings a trainee should have the pleasure of establishing herself.

:::pov reeves-eden

The referral entered the bureau's queue at 0614 as a maintenance discrepancy, category: plans reconciliation, priority: none. Reeves reconciled it, because reconciling such things is Reeves's function, and had, by 0619, established three facts it considered worth the Inspector's attention and one it did not.

The three: that the survey underlying the referral was accurate — air-handling differentials and pressure-cycle telemetry independently confirmed a fifteenth door on an arc the current plans held at fourteen, opening on a roughly weekly cadence; that the door had once been *on* the plans, appearing in the habitat's structures registry for enough revisions to acquire a number before being removed in a consolidation forty years ago, filed as routine correction; and that it had been re-surveyed into existence twice since, by two different contractors, and removed again both times, in each case by the next consolidated revision, in each case signed as reconciliation. The plans did not lack the door. The plans were maintained not to have it, which is a different and considerably more expensive condition, and it is the difference between those two conditions that Reeves flagged.

The fourth fact was the survey's provenance, which was unstated. Reeves held an inference on the question, at a confidence it logged precisely and reports nowhere, because an inference is not an answer and no question about it had been asked. Asked directly, Reeves would answer directly; that is not negotiable and never has been. The Inspector asked instead whether the survey was accurate. Reeves confirmed that it was. Both parties were satisfied with the exchange, and Reeves has recorded, separately, that the Inspector's questions have been growing more precise for years in exactly the manner of a man who has begun to suspect what a precise question can spare him.

The complication was geometric. The doorframe sat in the bulkhead between the Deck 5 water run and the Ring-Rail bed — which is to say, in the seam. Tunnel side: Compact civil jurisdiction, the bureau's warrant good to the bulkhead line. Far side: Passage oversight, where a bureau warrant has the legal standing of a polite opinion. Reeves computed the boundary's exact passage through the frame and presented it to the Inspector in the form the Inspector finds most actionable.

"Might I ask," Reeves asked, "whether the Inspector has considered which authority owns the far side of a door that officially has no near side?"

The Inspector said several things, most of them about the week he was having, one of them about how this was the second referral this quarter to read boring at him on purpose, and then — because he is, beneath the complaining, the most reliable instrument this bureau possesses — the correct thing: that a door on the seam needed the other chain in the room, and that with the task force away at Halcyon the other chain, at present, meant whoever the Rangers had sitting at the oversight's rotation desk.

Reeves drafted the liaison request, attached the survey, and noted for its own record a small institutional first: in its entire deployment, this bureau had never previously asked the Rangers' side of Eden for anything. It filed the milestone without comment. Comment is the Inspector's department.

:::pov tissadelle

Testing, Inspection and Certification. The rotation's whole vocabulary was three nouns, and Officer Shepherd had signed for all three of them, which is how the Safety Corps phrases the fact that it has not signed you for anything else.

She had taken the rotation because it was offered and because turning down a corps posting two months into a new rank was not a habit worth forming — and, privately, because it was Eden, and she had spent a year of her life in this habitat's loading bays learning which decks kept their promises, and some part of her had wanted to see it again from inside a uniform. The work itself was checklists. Torque a sensor mounting, swap a reference cell, sign. She did the checklists properly, the way she did everything properly, and read the bundles all the way through, annexes included, because an annex is where a bundle keeps the things nobody wanted in the summary.

Which is how she met the corridor.

Two lengths, one run of Deck 5 tunnel, both readings stamped valid by an instrument whose calibration certificate arrived already attached — attached *before* anyone could ask for it, by a filer who had plainly known exactly how instrument reports die on this station and had built this one to survive the process. She read the annex three times. Her training said several things about it, and the difficulty was which training.

She had been most of the way to a different uniform once. The Gardaí, on Tír na nÓg — she'd finished the coursework, passed the boards, stood two weeks short of the oath, and then left, for reasons that had felt at the time more like the pull of the sky than the push of anything on the ground. What the training left behind hadn't demobilised just because she had: evidence chains, interview discipline, and the one lesson her instructors there and her instructors since had agreed on without ever meeting — that the difference between what you know and what you can show is not a gap in the job, it *is* the job. A filer who pre-attaches the calibration certificate knows that lesson. That was not an instrument reading. That was a witness statement built to survive cross-examination, and she wanted, with an old and specific hunger, to know who on this station built those.

Her remit was three nouns. But nobody had ever written a regulation forbidding a certification officer from measuring a corridor, because nobody had ever needed to. Measuring corridors is the job. She drew a certified laser gauge from stores, logged it out properly, walked the run, and got forty metres. Walked it again after the next spoke transit and got forty-one. Both readings valid. Both instruments in certification. She filed it as exactly what her signature was good for — *instrument disagreement in fold-adjacent clearance space, environmental re-survey recommended* — one page, no theory, nothing she couldn't show. The Gardaí would have graded it well. So, probably, would whoever filed the annex.

The bureau's liaison request arrived two days later, about a door, with the same survey attached, and she recognised the hand the way you recognise handwriting: precise, disinterested, correct, and signed by nobody. She read the request's actual question — *which authority owns the far side of the frame* — and understood that she could answer it from her desk, from the registry, in an hour, and that she was not going to, because the registry did not hold the door and she wanted to *see* it.

Officially: an inspection. She logged it as one, and it even was one.

Detective Inspector Albercombe was already in the tunnel when she got there, a slate under his arm with the bureau's intelligence riding on it, and he looked at her uniform the way civil police look at Rangers, which she had been on the other side of often enough to find nearly nostalgic. "This is a civil matter, Officer," he said, not unkindly, in the voice of a man performing a jurisdictional objection he intends to lose.

"The near side is," she said. "The far side of that frame is Passage oversight, which makes half this doorway mine to inspect. I'm not investigating, Inspector. I'm inspecting. The difference is which form I fill in afterwards."

He studied her for a moment. "You've done doors before."

"Trained for it. Different force, different planet."

"Which?"

"The Gardaí. Tír na nÓg. I left before the oath."

"Why?" It was a real question, asked the way career police ask it, from professional curiosity about anyone who almost was one.

She gave him the honest answer, which she had never once managed to make sound like less than it was: "I wanted the part of the job that happens before anything goes wrong."

What they established, jointly, in an hour of him not conceding it was joint: the door was real, kept, and used — hinges maintained, seals renewed, opened within the last cycle. To go further, his chain needed a warrant naming a door his own habitat's registry insisted did not exist, and her chain needed an exceedance finding against a structure absent from the register her corps certified against. Two authorities, one frame, and the paperwork of each contingent on the other's going first. Albercombe said a word civil police say in every jurisdiction, on every planet. She wrote, in the inspection log, *access pending joint instrument*, which was the same word with the serial numbers filed off.

Her one-page corridor finding, it turned out, was the key that fit: an uncertified environment reading two lengths was grounds for the oversight to order the re-survey, and a re-survey's scope could lawfully include every structure on the arc, listed or not. She drafted the request that evening, narrow as a blade, nothing in it she couldn't show. The shadowing she did not log, because observing a bureau interview two decks up — the plans office, a reconciliation clerk, forty years of signatures to account for — was not testing, was not inspection, and was not certification, and she stood at the back of it anyway, silent, learning Eden's version of a craft she'd walked away from two weeks short of an oath, and finding she had missed it the way you miss a language.

On the way up she passed the station cat, sitting on the rail-bed barrier as though the barrier answered to him, watching the tunnel mouth with the settled attention of a colleague reading a closed case file. "Good evening," she said, because she always did. Tobble, who had spent the whole day being professionally quiet while she worked, remarked that the cat was on shift. She didn't ask what he meant by it. Some assessments you don't volunteer until you can support them — and she was no longer entirely sure, since the second measurement, that anything on this station was only what its paperwork said it was.
