This is told the way things are told below the roof, and it begins with a question, because the youngest had one and could not put it down.

The youngest had a telling now. A short one: three small loud people who looked at their lights and never turned round. It had been heard, by Stone-First first and then by others, and one of the others had told it to someone who had not been there, and that is the moment a telling stops being a thing that happened to you and becomes the people's. The youngest had lain in the warm and listened to their own telling being told by somebody else, and it had been like hearing your own footsteps come back off a far wall.

But the question was not about that. The question was about the humming stone.

The up-people had set it down at the junction and gone away. They did that. They went up the long passage toward where the air went cold, all three, and stayed away a telling or two, and came back. And all that time the humming stone sat where they had left it, on the floor, asleep.

Nobody in the deep leaves a sleeping one. It is not a rule. It is too plain to need to be a rule. If a small one falls asleep in the wrong place you sit with them, or you carry them into the warm, and you do not walk off up a passage and leave them on the stone in the cold. The youngest had thought about this for a whole telling and could not make it come right. The up-people talked to the humming stone. They knelt to it. They were pleased when it hummed. And then they left it alone in the dark as if it were nothing to them at all.

So the youngest went to the margin and asked Stone-First, who was there, because Stone-First is always there.

"Why don't they ask?"

Stone-First did not answer at once. Stone-First never did, on the grounds that an answer given quickly is usually an answer you already had lying about.

"Nobody has told them anything," said Stone-First.

The youngest waited, in case there was more, and there was.

"You know what we are called."

The youngest did. Everybody did. It is the first thing a small one is told, before the first warm seep, before anything. The people are called the Told. It is a name of the same kind as *Stone-First*, the first true thing said of them that everyone went on saying: they were told. A runner is not told anything. A runner is born knowing where the grazing is, and bellows, and that is the whole of what a runner has to say. But a person is told. You are told the first warm seep and the long dark and the coming of the up-people, and every telling before that back to where the tellings run out, and when you can tell them yourself without a mistake, you are one of the Told. That is what it means. There is no other way in.

"The up-people have not been told," said Stone-First. "Not by us. Not by anyone, that I can see, or they would move a stone. They are people who have not been told. That is a different thing from not being people. Keep the difference. It is easy to drop."

"Then why doesn't somebody tell them?"

"How would you begin?"

And the youngest, who had been going to say *by telling them*, stopped, because that was not the beginning of a telling and everybody knew it. A telling begins before the words. It begins with a stone. You move a stone, and the ground tells whoever is standing on it that you are there, and only then does anyone say anything, because you cannot be told by somebody you do not know is there.

The up-people could not hear the ground. That was the youngest's own finding, from the first time, the one Stone-First had said was not theirs yet.

But they could hear the humming stone. They listened to it the way the people listen to the ground. They knelt to it and it hummed and they were glad.

The youngest did not say any of this. Some thoughts you carry up the passage with you, and see whether they are still true at the top.

The youngest moved a stone, properly this time, at the foot of the squeeze, and went up.

::::: scene 2

::: pov teddy

The up-people were away. The youngest knew it before the squeeze, by the quiet, and knew it for certain at the top, where the long passage ran off toward the junction with no light in it that moved.

The humming stone was there, alone, on the floor, asleep.

Close to, it was not much like a stone. It was smooth all over, and it had a shape somebody had meant, and one end of it was where the humming lived. It was cold. Everything the up-people left was cold; they did not seem to mind that either. The youngest crouched by it for a while, not touching, the way you would crouch by a small one you did not know, and then put both arms round it and lifted.

It was heavy. It was heavier than anything the youngest had ever picked up, and the youngest had picked up a good deal, because the people are strong and that is not a telling, it is just how they are. The youngest stood, and got the weight against their chest where the fur is thickest, and turned, and began to walk.

The long passage is long. That is in its name. The youngest walked it with the humming stone held close and the arms aching and did not put it down, because if you put a sleeping one down every time your arms hurt you would never get anywhere.

The squeeze was the hard part, and the youngest had known it would be.

You go through the squeeze sideways. There is no other way. The youngest went in sideways with the humming stone held to the front, and got stuck, and came out, and went in the other way with the stone held to the back, and that was worse. In the end it was the first way, with the stone turned lengthwise and the youngest's whole side scraping the rock and the breath held, moving one hand-width at a time, and it was in the middle of that, in the narrowest part, with nothing to be done but go on, that the humming stone woke up.

It hummed. Against the youngest's chest, through the fur, a small even hum the youngest could feel in their teeth.

The youngest stopped.

You do not drop a sleeping one when they stir. You hold still. You hold them exactly as they were, and you do not talk, because talking is what wakes them properly, and you wait. The youngest held still in the middle of the squeeze with the rock on both sides and the cold thing humming against their heart and thought, not out loud, the thing you think at a small one who is stirring: *it is all right. It is only me.*

The humming stopped.

It went back to sleep. The youngest could feel the difference, the way you can. And then, one hand-width at a time, they went on through.

On the deep side of the squeeze, at the top of the margin, the floor is soft with silt. The youngest had never looked at it before, only walked on it. Now, with the humming stone held close, they looked, and saw the up-people's marks.

Their feet had come through. All three of them, once, a long time ago by the look of the edges. They had come through the squeeze, and walked a little way, and stopped, all together, in a line across the floor, and turned round, and gone back. The marks stopped so cleanly that the youngest stood and looked at them for a whole verse of something, and wondered what it was the up-people had heard, standing there, that made them stop. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the dark had been enough. But they had stopped in a line, as if the floor had said something after all, and that line was where the up-people's place ended and the people's began.

You move a stone at the edge of a place. Not in the middle of it. At the edge, where the one inside can hear it and know that someone is at the door.

The youngest set the humming stone down on the line, on its end, the humming end up, the way the up-person had set it at the junction. It leaned. The youngest knelt, and turned it, and turned it again, the way the up-person had, until it stood straight on the silt with its face toward the squeeze, toward where they would come from. Nothing on it was scratched. The youngest checked.

Then the youngest stood up, and looked at it, and thought: there. A stone moved first.

They could not hear our ground. But this was their ground, and they listened to it. When they came back through the squeeze they would find their own humming stone standing at the edge of our place, on the line where their own feet had stopped, and their ground would tell them what the people's ground had been telling them for a long time and they had never once heard.

*Someone is here.*

The youngest went home, and moved a stone at the bottom, and went to find Stone-First.

::::: scene 3

::: pov stone-first

Stone-First heard it standing up, at the margin, all of it, from the question to the line in the silt, and did not interrupt, because a telling interrupted has a hole in it that never quite closes.

When it was over, Stone-First was quiet for a while.

Here is what Stone-First was thinking, and it was not a small thing.

For as long as there had been tellings, the people had moved stones for each other. That is what a stone is for. A stone is moved so that someone who can hear the ground knows you are coming, and nobody in all the tellings had ever moved a stone for someone who could not hear it, because there had never been anyone like that. And now the smallest of them had gone and moved a stone anyway. Not the people's stone. The stranger's own. The one thing the stranger listened to.

The manners for a stranger in your place are short. Leave it to them until they ask. Stone-First had kept those manners for the whole time the up-people had been below the roof, and had never once been sure they were the right ones, and had kept them anyway, because manners you only keep when you are sure of them are not manners.

A stone moved first is not asking. Stone-First was clear about that. It is the thing before asking. It is the part that makes the asking possible, because you cannot ask for a place from somebody you do not know is there. The youngest had not broken the manners. The youngest had found the one thing the manners had been waiting for, all this time, and done it.

"You held still," said Stone-First, "when it woke."

"Yes."

"Good."

That was all Stone-First said about the humming stone, and the youngest, who had been braced for a great deal more, found that it was enough.

"The other telling," said Stone-First. "The one you brought down the first time. That they cannot hear the ground."

"You said it wasn't mine yet."

"It is now. You did something with it. A thing you have done something with is yours."

Then Stone-First went down into the warm, where the others were, and told it.

Not the youngest. Stone-First. That is how it is done. A telling is yours when someone has heard it, and it is the people's when the one who heard it tells someone who was not there, and Stone-First had heard it, and the others had not, and so Stone-First told them, standing in the amber with the whole body still. The question. The junction. The long passage. The squeeze, and the waking, and the holding still. The marks in the silt, and the line, and the stone set straight upon it.

Stone-First left out the reason. Not because it was wrong. Because a reason is a thing you tell people once they have the telling, and not before, or they hear the reason and not the thing.

The others listened. And afterwards, when they spoke of it among themselves, which they did at once, because it was the best thing to have happened below the roof in longer than anyone's telling went back, none of them said *the one who moved the up-people's stone*, and none of them said *the one who found the line.* What they said, over and over, so that it was already worn smooth by the time it reached the far end of the warm, was: *the small one who carried it all that way, and it woke, and they held it, and it slept.*

The one who carried it sleeping.

::: pov teddy

The youngest heard it from the warm, lying down, the way you hear your own name the first time. Which is to say: not at first. It went past twice before the youngest understood that the telling being told was theirs, and that the person being spoken of was them, and that the words the people were saying were not a description of anyone.

They were a name.

Carried-It-Sleeping.

The youngest lay very still and let it go by a third time, to be certain, and it was, and it did. Then the youngest turned over in the warm and slept, the way the young do when they have carried something a long way and put it down, and been told, at last, what it was.

::::: scene 4

::: pov stone-first

Stone-First keeps the margin, and so it was Stone-First who saw them come.

Some tellings after, a long way up the dark, the light that moves came through the squeeze. Stone-First had never seen it on the deep side before. It came through the way the youngest had said the up-people came through everything, loud, all three of them, and then it stopped, at the line, where their own humming stone stood on its end in the silt with its face toward them.

They stood there a long time. Stone-First could not see them, only the light, but the light did not move the way it usually did, from one small glowing thing to another. It stayed. It stayed on the humming stone, and on the floor round the humming stone, and on the marks in the silt, and then it went down, and stayed on the floor a long while, on the line.

And then it lifted, slowly, and came across the floor toward the dark.

It came the way a person looks. Not the way a light swings. Slowly, from the line, along the silt, up the margin, into the deep, as far into the dark as a light like that can reach, which is not far. It stopped at the edge of where it could reach. It stayed there. Stone-First, a long way down, in the dark, past where any light could come, did not move, and was not seen, and was, for the first time since the up-people came below the roof, quite sure of being looked for.

Then the light went back to the humming stone, and one of them picked it up, and they went back through the squeeze, and did not come past the line again. Stone-First waited a number of tellings to be sure of that, and it stayed true.

The youngest asked, of course. The youngest asked whether it had worked.

"They heard it," said Stone-First. "They have not asked. Those are two different things."

"Will they?"

"I do not know. Nobody has ever been where we are, and so nobody knows. But we know how to wait for it now, because now there is a thing to wait for. Before there was not."

Stone-First went back to the margin. Below, in the warm, the people were telling it again, to the small ones who had been asleep the first time, and the small ones were learning the name of the one who had done it, so that they would be able to say it when they were old, to small ones of their own who had not been there.

It was not a telling. Stone-First knew that. A stone is not a telling. It is only the beginning of one.

But every telling begins with one.
