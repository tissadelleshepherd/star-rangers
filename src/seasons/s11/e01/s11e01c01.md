::::: scene 1

::: pov teddy

This is told the way things are told below the roof: by someone who was there, to someone who was not, until the one who was not can tell it too.

The light in the deep does not change. It is amber on one wall and blue-green on the other, and it has been like that since before anybody's telling begins. So nobody in the deep says *morning*. They say *before the telling* and *after it*, and everyone knows which telling is meant, because there is only ever one going on.

The youngest of the people was awake before the telling, which is the wrong time to be awake, and was going up.

Going up is allowed. Going up past the margin is allowed too, where the warm stone stops smelling of the people and starts smelling of the runners, so long as you move a stone first. That is manners. You shift a stone, and the stone tells the ground, and the ground tells whoever is standing on it, and then nobody is surprised by anybody. The youngest knew the manners. The youngest had been told them many times, by Stone-First, who was called that for a reason.

The youngest did not move a stone.

Here is why, and it is not a good reason, but it is a true one. Everything the youngest knew had been told to them. The first warm seep, the long dark before the fungus came, the coming of the up-people: all of it was somebody else's, told down and down until it reached the smallest ears in the deep. The youngest could tell every one of those stories without a mistake. Not one of them had happened to the youngest. And a young one who has never been anywhere first has nothing that is theirs to tell, which is a small thing to be short of, and the youngest was short of it all the same.

So the youngest went up quietly, to be somewhere first.

The squeeze is the way. It is a crack in the rock at the top of the margin, and the big ones stopped fitting through it before the youngest was born, which is why nobody had been past it in a long time. The youngest fitted. It scraped, and the youngest let it scrape, and came out the other side into the upper levels, where the runners graze and the light is thinner and the air has the roof in it, cold and sharp and far away.

And there, at the end of a long passage, was the light that moves.

::::: scene 2

::: pov teddy

The up-people had three lights, and the lights went where the up-people looked, so the youngest could see at once what the up-people looked at, which was their lights.

There were three of them. They were small, and wrapped in something that was not fur, and they were loud. Not loud like the runners, who bellow once and are done. Loud like a stone that never finishes falling. They talked all the time, all three, to each other and to the humming stone they had set at the junction and, as far as the youngest could tell, to nothing at all. They held little things that glowed, and they looked at the little things, and they looked at each other, and they never once looked at the dark, which was where everything else was.

The youngest stood at the edge of their light. Not hidden. There was nothing to hide behind, and the youngest did not try. Just there, on the open floor, in plain sight of anyone who turned.

Nobody turned.

The youngest stood there for as long as it takes to tell the story of the first warm seep, which is a long story, and counted it through twice to be sure. The up-people worked. One of them laughed. One of them knelt by the humming stone and said things to it, and it hummed back, and the up-person seemed pleased. The light that moves moved, and swung, and once came so close across the floor that it touched the youngest's feet and went on past, and the up-person holding it did not follow it with their eyes.

Then the youngest went back the way they had come, before any head could turn, because a telling has to have an end and this was a good one.

The youngest thought about it all the way to the squeeze. The up-people cannot hear the ground. That was the finding. The youngest had moved no stone, had stood in the open at the edge of their light for two whole tellings, and nothing on any of them had turned. Nobody in the deep had ever told that. Nobody had known it to tell.

It was new. It was the youngest's.

And then, at the bottom of the squeeze, with the warm of the people coming up to meet them, the youngest remembered the other half of the rule, the half that comes after the manners: a telling is not yours until somebody has heard it.

The youngest moved a stone, late, and went to find Stone-First.

::::: scene 3

::: pov stone-first

Stone-First keeps the margin.

Keeping the margin means being at the place where the deep stops, on all the times when nothing happens, so that you are there on the time when something does. It is not a hard thing to do and it is not an easy thing to keep doing, and Stone-First had been doing it for longer than the youngest's whole telling.

Stone-First had known the youngest was going up. Not by a stone; the youngest had moved none, which Stone-First noticed and put aside to speak about later. By the way the warm changes when a small one leaves it. A warm place has a shape, and a person leaving takes a piece of the shape with them, and anyone who has kept one place long enough feels the piece go.

Stone-First let them go. That was a choice, and Stone-First was aware of making it.

The up-people had been in the upper levels for a long time now. Long enough that the telling of their coming was already told by people who had not been there, which is how you know a thing has stopped being news and become the people's. When they came, the people did the correct thing for a stranger in your place, which is to leave it to them until they ask. That is the whole of the manners for strangers. It is short because it has never needed to be long. Nobody had ever come before.

The trouble was the asking.

The up-people had never asked. Not for the upper levels, which the people had left to them. Not for the runners' ground, which was the runners'. Not for anything. Perhaps they did not know how. Perhaps where they came from, nobody asks; you arrive, and it is the arriving that does the asking, and everyone understands. Stone-First did not know. Stone-First had thought about it on the times when nothing happened, which was most of them, and had not got to the end of the thought, and did not expect to. Some thoughts are for keeping, not for finishing.

What Stone-First knew was this. A people who leave a place to a stranger until the stranger asks, and a stranger who never asks, can go on that way for a very long time. And every day of it the people are being polite to someone who does not know they are there.

Below, a stone shifted. Small, and late, and at the bottom of the squeeze.

Stone-First did not move, and waited to be told.

::::: scene 4

::: pov teddy

The youngest told it standing up, in the amber, the whole thing in order: the squeeze, the runners' air, the light that moves, the three, the loud, the humming stone, the light across the feet, and nobody looked up.

Stone-First listened the way the old ones listen, which is with the whole body still, so that nothing you do while you are listening becomes part of the telling by mistake.

When it was done, Stone-First said, "You did not move a stone."

"No."

"So they did not know you were there."

"They would not have known anyway," said the youngest, because this was the finding, and the finding was the point. "They cannot hear the ground."

"That is a thing you know now," said Stone-First. "It is not a thing they know. Do you see the difference?"

The youngest did not, yet, and said so, because saying so is also manners.

Stone-First did not explain it. Stone-First hardly ever explained a thing, on the grounds that a thing explained is a thing you have been told, and a thing you have been told is not yet yours. Instead Stone-First said, "Tell it again. From the squeeze. Leave out what they cannot do. Tell only what they did."

So the youngest told it again, from the squeeze, and this time there was no finding in it, only three small loud people who looked at their lights and knelt by a humming stone and laughed once and never turned round, for as long as the first warm seep takes to tell, twice. It was shorter the second time. Stone-First nodded at that, the way the old ones nod when something is getting true.

"That one is yours," said Stone-First. "The other one is not yet. You will know when it is."

:::

::: pov stone-first

The youngest went to sleep after that, in the warm, quickly, the way the young do when they have carried something a long way and put it down.

Stone-First went back to the margin.

Nothing had happened, in the sense that mattered. Nobody had been hurt. Nothing had been taken. A small one had stood in a stranger's light and come home with a story, and the story was true, and it was short, and it was theirs. Stone-First had heard worse tellings from people three times the youngest's size.

But Stone-First kept the margin, and keeping the margin means noticing what has changed at it, and something had. For the whole time the up-people had been below the roof, the people had known where the up-people were and the up-people had not known where the people were, and that had been the shape of things, and it had held. Now a small one had been close enough to be touched by their light. Now a small one knew a thing about them that they did not know about themselves.

That was still the same shape. It was just closer.

Above, a long way up, past the squeeze and the runners' ground, the humming stone hummed at the junction where the up-people had left it, asleep, the way they leave things. The youngest had not touched it.

Not yet.
