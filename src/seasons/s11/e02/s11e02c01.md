::::: scene 1

::: pov aravena

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there, into a unit that was there too, and read afterwards by someone who was not.

Aravena carried the unit. That was the job, or the part of the job that was Aravena's and nobody else's, and Aravena did not mind it. The unit was heavy and smooth and about the length of an arm, and it rode on your back down the long passage and sat on the floor at the junction and did what it was for, which was to listen, and to remember, and to talk to the relay when the relay was overhead. When the relay was not overhead it slept. You could tell. It went quiet in a particular way.

The junction was the party's furthest working point. Three passages met there, and the fungus on the walls was thick enough to work by if you had to, amber on one side and blue-green on the other, though nobody had to, because the party brought its own light. Egede was at the far wall with the imager, saying things. Egede always said things. The survey lead was reading the ground, which was what Nakagawa did, and which looked like standing still. Aravena set the unit down on its base at the middle of the junction, turned it until it was oriented the way the party oriented them, and knelt to it.

"Log," said Aravena. "Shift forty-one. Upper junction. Party of three. Nothing so far."

The unit hummed, once, low, the way it did to say it had heard. Aravena liked that. It was not a person and Aravena knew it was not a person, and it was still nice to be answered.

Behind them, down the third passage, was the dark. Not fungus-dark. The real kind, where the upper levels stopped and the ground went down into the deep networks, which the survey did not go into, because that was the rule. The party kept its back to it. That was not the same as forgetting it was there, though from the outside it looked the same.

The Anstolik had been through; you could smell them, warm and musky under the mineral cold. Egede logged the tracks. Nakagawa logged the ground. Aravena logged the temperature, twice, because the first reading looked wrong and was not. Then the working time was over, the way it always was, before anyone had finished, and they gathered the light and the imager and the handsets, and left the unit where it sat, asleep, because it was heavy and it was fine and that was what you did.

They went up the long passage toward the cold, all three, talking. Nobody looked back. There was nothing to look back at.

::::: scene 2

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa read the unit's channels every evening at the station, after the others had eaten, because a thing read the same day is a thing you can still do something about.

The station sat on the roof of the world, on the equatorial plain, in a wind that smelled of ice and stone. Nothing about it would stay if the party left: that was the rule for this planet, and Nakagawa had written half of it. The unit's log came up the relay in the afternoon and waited. Sound channel, nothing. Imager, Anstolik, three, passing. Thermal channel.

Nakagawa read the thermal channel twice, and then sat for a while, and then went and got the other two.

"Look at that," said Nakagawa, and pointed.

A profile. Warm, upright, at the edge of the working light, on the open floor, where the third passage came in. Not moving. It had arrived between one sweep of the channel and the next, without any of the things that usually arrived first — no boulder shifted, no sound, nothing — and it had stood there, in plain sight of anyone who turned, for eleven minutes. Then it was not there.

"Eleven minutes," said Egede.

"Nobody looked up," said Nakagawa. "I didn't. Did either of you?"

They had not. Aravena went a colour.

"It's the small one," Egede said. "It's Teddy. Look at the width of it. That's not an adult."

"That is the fourth individual we have learned to tell apart," said Nakagawa, "and the log will say so." Nakagawa had the entry up. Aravena had written it, that afternoon, before anyone had read the channel — the temperature twice, the tracks, the time — and had added, at the end, from the profile's roundness and the way it held its warmth, the word *young*.

Nakagawa struck it out.

"We have no way to age a Pandoid," Nakagawa said. "We have never had one. *Smaller* is what we measured. *Young* is a guess wearing the measurement's clothes, and if the guess is wrong, every reading we make afterwards leans on it. Smaller."

"He is smaller," said Egede. "Fine."

Nakagawa let *he* go by. It was the party's word and it did no harm in the party's mouth, and there were only so many things a survey lead could strike out in one evening before people stopped hearing it.

What Nakagawa thought, and did not say, was simpler than any of that. Somebody had stood behind us for eleven minutes, close enough to touch the light, and we did not know. Nobody was hurt. Nothing was taken. And we were watched from nearer than we had ever been watched, by someone who knew exactly how near that was.

"Nothing changes," said Nakagawa. "We go on working. And we write down exactly what happened, and not one thing more."

Eleven minutes is a long time to be looked at. Nakagawa filed the entry, and put the light out, and did not sleep for about that long.

::::: scene 3

::: pov aravena

Some shifts later the unit was not at the junction.

Aravena saw the empty floor from the bottom of the long passage, before the others, because Aravena was the one who looked for it. The Anstolik do not pick things up. Aravena's stomach went the way a stomach goes when a thing you were responsible for is gone.

Nakagawa said nothing, went to the third passage, stood, and then walked down it slowly with the light held low, and the others followed.

The party had been down there once. Aravena remembered it. The third passage narrowed to a crack you went through sideways, scraping — Egede called it the squeeze and had rated it, in the log, impassable for anything of an adult Pandoid's mass — and on the far side the floor was soft with pale silt, and Nakagawa had walked a little way onto the silt and stopped, and said *far enough*, and they had all stopped, together, in a line, and turned round, and gone back. The survey did not go down where the deep began. The line was where the rule had stopped them. Their tracks were still there, in the silt, with the edges gone soft.

The unit was standing on the line.

Upright. On its base. Oriented the way the party oriented them, with its face toward the squeeze, toward the way they had come. Exactly on the line where their own feet had stopped, as if somebody had measured it. Nothing on it was scratched.

Aravena knelt to it, the way Aravena knelt to it every shift, and put the handset to it, and read its log. It had slept at the junction. Then, some time after, in transit — *in transit*, said the log, the unit's own flat word for being moved — it had woken. Thermal trigger at zero range. It had classified the trigger at the highest confidence it had: an upright bipedal profile. And then it had gone back to sleep.

At zero range. Held against something warm, all the way here, and it had woken up and said what it was looking at, and then it had gone back to sleep in the arms of the thing it had just named.

"It came back," said Egede, behind Aravena. "He brought it back. He carried it the whole way, through *that*, and put it down where we'd find it. That's a courtesy. A dropped thing returned."

"That's a line," said Nakagawa. "Drawn with our own kit, at our own furthest mark, by somebody who watched where we stopped and thought about it."

Aravena did not say anything. Aravena was looking at the unit, straight on the silt with its face toward the party, and thinking that it had not been dropped, and it was not a line, and that neither of them was wrong, and that there was nothing to file, because what Aravena thought was not a reading. It was a feeling, and the survey does not file those. *It was put where we would find it. By someone who held it while it woke.*

Nakagawa took the light.

Nakagawa swung it the way a person looks: slowly, from the unit, along the silt, over the old tracks and the one new round print beside them, up the slope of the margin and into the dark, as far into the dark as a light like that can reach, which is not far. It stopped where it could not go further. It stayed there a while.

Nothing.

"Lift it," said Nakagawa. "We are not placing anything past this line, and nobody is crossing it. Log that."

::::: scene 4

::: pov nakagawa

Both readings went in the file, that evening, side by side, with neither one chosen.

Egede argued for choosing. Egede always did, and was usually worth listening to, and was not wrong: two readings of one event, filed as equals, is a file that says the survey does not know what happened. Nakagawa said that this was exactly what the survey should say, because it was exactly what was true. A dropped thing returned. A line drawn. Both of them were readings. The unit standing on the silt was the fact, and the fact was filed first, above both, in the plainest words Nakagawa could find.

Then the standing order, and this was the one Egede really did not like.

"From now on," said Nakagawa, "the log says, every shift, that nothing happened."

"That isn't a log entry. That's a —"

"It is a count. Somebody has moved our instrument to the edge of their place and set it facing us. We do not know what it meant. When you do not know what a thing meant, you do not do the next thing. You write down that you do not know, and you wait, and every day you wait you write that down too. So that on the day something does happen, the file will say how long it had been true that nothing did."

Egede thought about that, and then said, "*Unasked*," to nobody in particular, the way Egede said a word when it was going to end up in a document, and let it go.

Nakagawa filed the order. Then Nakagawa sat with the two readings a while longer, in the wind, and thought that the trouble with a party of three is that there are only three readings to be had, and one of the three had not been given, and that the one who had not given it was the one who had knelt.

::: pov aravena

Aravena was last up the passage, that shift, with the unit on their back where it had ridden every shift since the first, and heavier than Aravena remembered, though it was the same unit.

At the bottom of the long passage, where the third passage came in, Aravena stopped.

The others were ahead, going up, their light going up with them and the talking getting fainter the way it did. Aravena stood with the light held low and turned round, and faced the third passage, and the dark at the end of it, the real dark, down where the squeeze was and the silt and the line and whatever had carried the unit all that way and held it while it woke.

Nobody had told Aravena to do this. Nobody would have said not to. Neither of those is a reason, and Aravena knew it, and did it anyway.

"Was that all right?" said Aravena, to the dark, not loudly. The question went up at the end, the way a question does. Aravena meant it for the one who had carried it. For Four.

And then waited. Stood there, with the light low and the others gone on, and waited the way you wait for an answer, for as long as it is polite to, and then a little longer.

Nothing answered. On Aravena's back the unit hummed once, the way it did when it woke and found nothing, and stopped.

Aravena went up.

The log that shift said that nothing had happened, in Aravena's own voice, because that was the order, and it was true, and the log did not have a place in it for the other thing. Some things you can only say to the dark. That does not make them not said.
