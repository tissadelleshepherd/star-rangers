The order came up the line with the morning's others and Wender read it standing, as she read anything she might be asked to agree to.

*Promotion, High Captain. Wender, K., Star Captain. Theatre assigned, effective on assumption. Seated on the Council of High Captains from the date of this order. Command ship: Fel Trohen, at yard, crewed. Staff: see schedule. Chief of staff: at the High Captain's nomination.*

A theatre, a seat, a hull, a list. The Corps did not explain its promotions any more than it explained its pairings, and the theatre it named was the one she had been expecting for a season, since a Star Captain who held the lines at the edge of a theatre was generally the officer who got the theatre when it came open. She had the four rings at her collar most of a year. Asteria had left the service at that rank, at about a hundred, and Wender had held it since without once thinking of herself as level with anyone; the rank was a post, and the post was lines, and she had run them. Now the rank was a theatre, and the one thing the order said about the theatre was that it was hers.

She read it again, which she did now, since the last one.

*Fel Trohen.* She had read that first. Before the rank, before the seat, before the word *theatre*, her eye had gone to the name of the hull, because that was where a pilot's eye went on any page, and she noticed that it had and set the noticing down for later. The ship was at a yard whose name she knew and whose berths she had flown out of, crewed already, the schedule said, for the work. A High Captain's ship was a headquarters that moved. She had known that the way she knew most of the ladder above her, as a sentence on a page she had never had to stand in.

The schedule ran to three pages. Records. Weighing. Relay. A watch across the whole theatre's traffic. Every post on it filled from the lists, by the Corps, in the ordinary way, except one, and the one was the staff's own head, and the order left that to her by name. It was the only part of the whole document a High Captain got to decide for herself on her first day, and she did not need the day.

Two things would travel with her that were not on the schedule. The first was the pairing. *Pairing is on the record and does not lapse with posting*, the order of 2829 had said, and it had not said whose posting; her mentee was an Archwarden now, second on a vessel that could get somewhere, two rungs below her this morning and three by the evening, and the duty went where the mentor went, which was onto a hull built not to go anywhere quickly. The second was the name she was about to write, and she wrote it, on the nomination line, and then did not send it.

A nomination from a High Captain to a Chief was an order. It said so nowhere; it did not need to. The officer she had written was Krenyi, and a Krenyi did what a legible request warranted because it was worth doing, and nothing on any page could obtain from her what she had not extended. Wender had served beside her for years and had never once had to be told that. She folded the nomination into the sleeve of the order, unsent, and asked the flagship for Threshold, which was most of two days, and which the flagship could do, this once more, at a flagship's speed.

::: pov arashobal

The flagship was on the dock schedule four days before it was on the dock, and Arashobal had the list made the day the schedule changed.

She had not been told what the list was for. A Star Captain's flagship did not come to Threshold for the general operations department, and the dock schedule said only that it was coming, and from which region, and the region was Wender's. So the Chief Ranger of Threshold had sat down one evening with the department as it stood and written out what a Chief handing it over would need to leave behind, in the order a successor would need it: the sections and their Leads, the outer docks, which were a Lead's now and had been thin for three years and were still thin, the traffic plan, the one instrument on Dock Seven that read forty seconds on and was outside the log by standing practice, and who knew it. Four pages. Nobody had asked for them. She had been anxious, she supposed, in the way her people were, which showed on a staff as a list made earlier than anyone needed it, and she filed the list and went on with the department.

Wender came up from the dock in a field shell with four rings on the collar and stood in the doorway of the office across the deck from the one she had held, and did not fill the room with herself. She had never filled a room. Arashobal had known her for years as a Chief Pilot who said one sentence in a briefing and counted the rooms afterwards, and since then by relay as something that went up the ladder a rung at a time and sent, at each rung, nothing a Krenyi would have needed to keep.

"Chief," Wender said.

"Star Captain."

"High Captain by the evening. The order is in my sleeve and I have not assumed it, which is a thing a High Captain can do once." She came in and sat, which humans did when the agreeing was done, and Arashobal registered that the agreeing, on Wender's side, was done. "The theatre is mine from assumption. The ship is the *Fel Trohen*, at yard, crewed. Every post on her is filled from the lists but one. The chief of staff is the High Captain's to name, and I have named you, and I have not sent it."

That was the material part, and Wender had put it in the order a Krenyi would have put it in: the fact, the post, the thing not done. Arashobal did not answer at once. She read the human for drift, as she read everything, and found none; a stated term and the conduct that would match it, offered before anyone had asked for the match. The posting could have come up the line with a seal on it. A Chief who received such a posting did what it said, because that was what the ladder was for, and Wender had read the ladder, and had flown most of two days to arrive with the paper unsent.

"What a chief of staff answers for," Arashobal said. It was not a question. It was the sentence she had said to a Section Lead once in this office, turned round.

"A department is one part of a command. The staff answers for how every part of a theatre's command fits together: that an order I give states what it is for and what it must not cost, and reaches the rank certified to carry it through a Line Captain or a Starwarden with its detail still where it belongs. The office has no authority. An order from the staff is mine or it is nothing. And when the theatre has started to run on what I prefer rather than on what the orders say, the staff is the office that notices first, and says so, and returns the order to its terms." Wender stopped. "You would notice first. You cannot not."

"No."

"The rank stays Chief. The post is larger than the department and the mark on the collar does not say so. The Corps has one ring for that and it is not on the ladder." A pause the length of a corridor clearing. "The department passes to its senior Lead pending a Chief. I would rather you said no here than yes on the ship."

Arashobal considered the two answers, since both were true. Threshold's department was hers and in good order and she would have kept it; general operations was the usual road from Chief to a first command, and it had been open to her for some years, and she had not taken it, for reasons that were material and that she had never been asked for. A staff office on a command ship was the one place in the Corps where a distant desk wrote orders for people it would not see, and the Corps' own instinct distrusted the shape of it, and the distrust was correct. An office that could not un-notice an overly detailed order was not an office that would write one. She had known that about herself for longer than Wender had known it about her, and had never had a desk it was any use at.

"Yes," she said.

Wender took the paper out of her sleeve and put it on the desk between them, and Arashobal initialled it, and that was the whole of the move, since nothing more was required and nothing more would have added anything. Then she gave Wender the four pages, which Wender had not asked for and read standing, all of them, while Arashobal went on with the department's work and did not watch her read.

"You made this before the dock schedule had the ship on the dock," Wender said, at the end.

"The day it had the ship on the schedule."

Wender did not say anything to that. She folded the handover with the nomination and went down to the dock, and Arashobal returned to the department, which was a Section Lead's from the turn of the day, and whose outer docks were thin, and would be thin for whoever came, and were on the list.

::: pov wender

The tab came off with a thumbnail in the flagship's lock, because there was no ceremony in the order and she had read the order.

Four rings, plain-finished, no insert, most of a year old. She had worn one ring, and two, and three, none of them for long, which was what a ladder looked like from a rung reached late and climbed on a schedule nobody had shown her. The new tab was the cluster. She seated it, and the magnet took, and the tab sat proud of the cloth as every rank she had ever worn had, visibly removable. The seal was separate, in its own sleeve, and she looked at it and did not seat it. A seal was a seated body, not a rank. It went on when the Council sat, and the Council sat aboard a High Captain's ship, and she had not yet stood on hers.

The yard had the *Fel Trohen* at a berth she had flown a department out of once, in a different decade. She went across by the yard's own tender, which a High Captain did not, as a rule, and which she did because she wanted to see the hull from outside before she was inside it answering for it, and the hull was long and deep and had the lines of a thing built to carry weight and keep it dry. Not a ship. A building with a drive in it.

Inside it was a headquarters. She had known the sentence and now she stood in it. A records deck, two decks of it, with the theatre's files already coming aboard in sealed crates from the region's lines, and the people who weighed them already at the desks, since the schedule had said *crewed* and the schedule was accurate. A relay watch across the whole theatre's traffic. A room with a long table and a seal on the wall where the Council would sit when it sat here. Her own office, which was the size of a Line Captain's cabin and had been the previous High Captain's, and still looked it. A chief of staff's office beside it, empty.

Last, because it was where her feet went, the flight deck.

It was small. A Principal held it, a pilot on the navigation track with a certified boundary plate beside the rank bars, who stood when the High Captain came in and did not fill the deck with herself, and Wender told her to sit, and the Principal sat. The instruments were the ones Wender had spent her whole service at, in the places she would have put them. She did not sit at them. She asked for the hull's sheet instead, and the Principal gave it to her, and she read it standing, as she read anything she was going to be held to.

The figure was where a figure like it always was, in the transit column. She read it twice. A Line Captain's ship made the far side of a theatre like this one in a day; a Star Captain's flagship, which she had just stepped off, in two. The *Fel Trohen* made it in three, laden, and would always be laden, because what she carried was the theatre, and a theatre did not get lighter.

"That is the laden figure, High Captain," the Principal said. "The yard has a note on it. We can shave it a little with the records deck sealed and the relay watch standing down for the transit, if a crisis asked. I can have the alternatives drafted by the turn of the day."

"No," said Wender. "That is the correct figure. Leave it."

The Principal looked at her, and then at the sheet, and did not ask, which Wender noted and did not write down; an assessment records, and this was not an assessment.

She had been, for a whole career, the officer who arrived. A Chief Pilot's entire competence was the difference between a hull that was there and a hull that only looked, on a map, as if it could be. She had made Threshold's corridors in less than the window said for years and had never once mythologised it, since the work was the work. What the order had given her, and what she had read first on the page without reading it, was a hull built not to get anywhere first. Every ship she now commanded would reach the thing before she did. By the time the *Fel Trohen* was there, the thing would be hours old, third-hand, and already decided by somebody two rungs down who had been correct given what was currently known, or had not been; and the whole of what a High Captain was for, she saw now, standing on a flight deck she would not sit at, was what she did with the order when it reached her stale. Which was the one thing she had told Shepherd in a previous Starwarden's office, in more words than she liked. She had thought she was teaching it.

She gave the Principal back the sheet.

"Three days," she said. "Plan on it."

Then she went down to the records deck, where the theatre was coming aboard in crates, and put the chief of staff's four pages on the first desk that had nobody at it yet, and sat down.
