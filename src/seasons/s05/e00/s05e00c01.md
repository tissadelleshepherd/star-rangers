The order came up the line with the morning's others and Wender read it standing, because a thing read sitting down has already been agreed to.

*Pairing, command-rank mentorship, on promotion to Starwarden. Mentee: Shepherd, T., Starwarden, fold-approach post, outward frontier, effective on assumption. Mentor: Wender, K., Line Captain. Duty attaches to the mentor. Pairing is on the record and does not lapse with posting.*

That was the whole of it. The Corps did not explain its pairings and had never been asked to, on the reasoning, which Wender agreed with, that a pairing explained is a pairing somebody will argue with, and the policy existed precisely so that nobody argued. Above Chief there is no Academy. There is no syllabus for command, no examination, nothing a board can mark, because a command judgement is a claim only its consequences can verify and they arrive too late to teach anyone anything. So the Corps assigns a mentor, formally, as a duty, and the mentor is answerable for it the way a Chief is answerable for a section. Wender had had one herself, on her own promotion, an officer who did the job as a duty and did it well and whom she had not, as it turned out, needed. She had not said so. It was not the kind of thing you said about a duty done well.

What she had needed, she had got a long time before, from a Star Captain in her nineties who had taken an interest in a pilot in her twenties with no policy requiring it, and who was retired on a hill somewhere now and did not write. That was not a pairing. That had been Asteria feeling like it. Wender had never once mistaken the two.

She read the order again, which she did not do.

*Shepherd, T.*

Wender had been reading that officer's silences for three years. Not on assignment; nobody had assigned it, and if anyone had she would have declined, on the grounds that reading a junior officer's silences was not a thing the Corps could order and was not a thing a superior should be doing on purpose. She had done it anyway, by habit, since Shepherd's year as a Senior, when a former Threshold cadet had turned up on a Survey rotation reading a waveform as sound and had been disbelieved in a review chamber until the finding was reproduced without her, and had stood there, Wender was told, and let it be reproduced. Four seasons. A rank and a half ahead of her cohort, every rotation a different corps, nothing on the record to say why anyone should be watching, and Wender had watched, and had not said that either.

Now it was a duty. The Corps had looked at its command ladder and its small number of officers and found, as it generally did, that the people on it had already noticed each other, and had put a duty under the noticing.

She initialled the order. Then she sat down, because the agreeing was done, and thought about what the duty was going to cost, and found that she already knew, which was the only part of it that worried her.

::: pov tissadelle

The post was a stretch of fold approach that answered to nobody closer than a line office nine hours away by relay, and a Starwarden answers for it. That was the sentence. Tissadelle had read it on the day the rank came through and had read it every day since, the way she read anything she was going to be held to, and it had not got shorter.

Starwarden. The hinge was behind her. Chief Ranger had been the top of the examined ladder, the rank a board could mark, and she had made it the way the record said she had, by rotating through almost every corps the Rangers ran until there was nothing left to be certified in and only command left to climb. Nobody had told her that was what she had been doing. She had worked it out the way she worked out most things, by noticing, afterwards, the shape of what she had done.

Above Chief, nothing could be examined. The Corps had told her that in the plainest words it had, which were the policy's: *on promotion to Starwarden an officer is paired with a serving senior officer who is answerable for the mentorship as a duty.* And then a name.

She had not expected the name, and then she had.

Wender had been Chief Pilot at Threshold when Tissadelle was a cadet there, a rank and a section and an operational horizon above anything a cadet was for, and they had spoken, in three years, perhaps four times, all of them about a corridor. Tissadelle had been aware of her the way a cadet is aware of weather. Then Season 2 had put Tissadelle on a Survey rotation and Season 3 had put her in a review chamber, and somewhere in between she had begun to notice that Chief Pilot Wender, who had no reason to, was in the room more often than the rooms required, and said less in them than anyone, and that the saying less had a shape.

*The one person senior to you who knows what the crossing costs,* the officer who handed her the order had said, which was a sentence somebody had plainly said to him, *and arrived at it from the far side.* Wender had come to command from the top of one thing. Tissadelle had come to it because rotating through everything had left her nowhere else. Two crossings, opposite directions, the same rank; the Corps needed both and had promoted both and then handed one the other to look after.

What she was carrying, she listed, that evening, in the way she listed things she might be asked. The Sentinel. The Threshold discrepancy and what she privately made of it, which was more than the record did. Tobble, who was in no file the Corps held and never would be unless she put him there, and who had opinions about Wender that he kept, for once, to himself. The inner world.

None of it was in any file. All of it was hers. She did not yet know which of those two sentences a mentor was entitled to, and found, lying awake on a post that answered to her, that she would rather be told than guess.

::: pov wender

Wender went out to the post herself, nine hours by relay and most of a day by hull, because a pairing that begins over a link begins as paperwork and she had read enough paperwork.

The post was what the order said it was: a stretch of monitored approach, a station the size of a modest ship, a crew of twenty who had had their new Starwarden for six weeks and had, Wender judged from the corridor, already decided. Crews decide early. They are usually right, and when they are wrong it is in the direction of generosity, which is the only direction a crew can afford to be wrong in. These had decided in Shepherd's favour and were not being generous about it. Wender noted that and did not write it down; an assessment records, and this was not an assessment.

Shepherd met her at the lock in uniform, Starwarden's ring-tab at the collar, and said, "Line Captain," and Wender said, "Starwarden," and that was the whole of the ceremony, because there was nothing in the policy about ceremony and both of them had read the policy.

They sat in the post's one office, which had been the previous Starwarden's and still looked it. Wender let the silence run for about as long as a corridor takes to clear, which was the length she had learned on, and watched Shepherd not fill it, and thought: yes.

"The policy gives you me," Wender said. "It does not say what for, and it is right not to, because the one thing I am for cannot be written down, or the Academy would have written it. I am going to tell you the only part of it I can say, once, and then I am going to stop telling you things, and you are going to find that the stopping is most of the job."

Shepherd waited. She did not nod. Wender had never seen her nod at a thing before she had heard the end of it.

"An order is sound if it is correct given what is currently known," Wender said. "Not if the officer giving it has disclosed everything she privately knows. Those are two different standards, and the second one is a trap, and every commander who falls into it does so for the same reason, which is that her own uncertainty is uncomfortable and a subordinate's trust is a cheap way to relieve it. *What aren't you telling me* is not a question that improves anything. It is a superior spending a junior's confidence on herself. Demand disclosure as the price of trust and you will teach good officers to stop bringing you the truths that matter and start bringing you the ones that are easy to explain, and you will never once notice it happening, because the easy ones will keep arriving."

She stopped. That was the whole of it, and she had said it in more words than she liked, and did not take any of them back.

Shepherd was quiet for a while. Then she said, carefully, in the voice Wender had heard once in a review chamber by report and never in person: "There are things I am carrying. They are in no file. I should tell you —"

"Are they in the file?"

"No."

"Do they change any order you have given, or will give?"

A longer pause. Wender watched her check, which took as long as it took, because Shepherd did not answer a thing she had not checked.

"No."

"Then they are yours," said Wender, "and I have not asked, and I will not. If that ever changes, put it in writing and I will read it. If it does not, do not."

::: pov tissadelle

The line captain's hull was a day gone before Tissadelle understood what had been done, and she understood it the way she understood most things, by the shape of what was missing afterwards.

She had been ready to be asked. She had listed, for a week, what she would say about each thing if she were asked about it, and in what order, and where the lines were that she would not cross for any rank, and she had not slept well, because a mentor who is answerable for you and can order you is a person who can, in principle, ask anything, and she had not known what the policy entitled. Then the one person the Corps had given her had sat in a dead Starwarden's chair and asked two questions, neither of which was *what*, and on two noes had said *then it is yours*, and had meant it, and had left.

Nothing had been taken. That was the shape. Tissadelle had crossed into command carrying more than any file held, and the officer answerable for her had looked at the weight, and had asked whether it was the Corps' and whether it was the work's, and on learning it was neither had declined to pick it up, and the declining had cost Wender something, Tissadelle thought, and she had paid it without comment, which was the thing about her that the cadet at Threshold had never once been able to name.

*An order is sound if it is correct given what is currently known.* She had kept that. She would find, in a year or two, that she had kept the second half too, the half about the easy truths, and that it had been the more expensive half to keep; but that was later, and not this telling's.

What she did that week, which the record holds because she kept the copy, was write to Galen Kane.

Kane was a Chief Ranger of the Frontier Corps, which policed nothing and left worlds when their compacts asked it to, and she had ridden his circuit as the second of a pair in a rotation her own record undercounted, years ago, when he had taught her nothing she could have been examined on and everything she had used since. He had no place in her chain of command. He held no boundary certification. He had no stake in a single thing a Starwarden on a fold approach would ever decide, and that, she found, writing to him, was the entire qualification.

She did not write to him about the Sentinel, or the discrepancy, or the two names she carried that were in no file. Those were hers, and she had just been told so by someone with the authority to have said otherwise. She wrote to him about the one question his corps spent whole careers on and hers spent almost none, and which she could not put to Wender, because Wender could order her and the question could not survive being put to someone who could.

*When is leaving the correct order?*

She did not expect an answer soon. Kane's circuit was a long one and he answered letters at its ends. She sealed it, and filed the copy, and went up to the approach, which answered to her, and stood the watch she had been promoted to stand.

Two relationships had begun that week, and she would never once confuse them. One could order her, which made its silences binding. One could not, which made its answers free. The Corps had assigned her the first and she had kept the second, and the Corps, which was small enough for people to have already noticed each other, had plainly known she would.
