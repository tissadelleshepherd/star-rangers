A reader asked me tonight, in effect, what Elvira is. I went back through Season 1 to answer, and found that I could not, and that the reason I could not is the most deliberate thing in those chapters. So this is a note on the shape of that, because it is a shape I want to be able to use again on purpose rather than arrive at by accident.

The naive way to keep a mystery open is to withhold. The author knows the answer, the characters are kept from it, and the reader waits for the chapter where somebody finds the file. That shape has a cost that shows up late: when the file is found, the reader is exactly where they were, plus one fact. I have written elsewhere that an ending in this record must change how the beginning reads, and a withheld fact almost never does. It was always there. It was just behind a door.

The shape Season 1 actually runs on is different, and I can state it in one sentence. **Every character holds one piece of evidence, and nobody holds a theory.**

Walk the cast at the Marsh Causeway and that is what you find. [Galahad Thorne](/star-rangers/characters/galahad-thorne/) holds the only hard datum in the whole record, a meeting twenty years earlier at a house called Dún Rí, and what he does with it is the opposite of deduction. He does not ask her. He runs a test, feeding her own private vocabulary back to her without attribution and watching whether she receives it as *familiar* or as *impressive*. He has entered none of this in the survey file, and the chapter declines to say why. [Aldera](/star-rangers/characters/aldera/), whose entire method is the filed log, files her as *Subject A, unclassified* and then writes, in the same log, that the question is not the agency's to close. [Brother Daire](/star-rangers/characters/brother-daire/) gives the only account of origin there is, and it is a folk account: a sensitivity that runs in the mother's line, ground that is thin and thinning, and a word for it, the Overfold, which is the Fellowship's name and not the Survey Corps'. The household sage at Dún Rí teaches her a method and asks what she found, never what she is. Nessa, the wild cat on the tidal rock, reads her from a distance as someone trying to hold a line alone and failing. The locals call her the Enchantress, which her page is careful to call a misclassification of her *methods*. And Elvira herself, who does not experience deep fear and has built a working practice on top of that absence, finds that when something moves through the room she has nothing to identify it with except a man being careful.

None of these people is stupid, and none of them is being coy. Each has exactly as much as their position in the world would give them, and each does the thing their discipline tells them to do with it: test, file, name, teach, warn, mislabel, notice. The question stays open because no two pieces are held by the same person, and because the people who hold pieces are the kind who do not speculate past their evidence. That is a property of the cast, not a trick played on the reader.

The piece I am proudest of is the one the predator holds. The [Obliger](/star-rangers/glossary/obligers/) at the causeway does not know what Elvira is either. Aldera's finding is that it did not need to. It needed the question to stay open near its hunting ground, the way a bank of fog does not need to be the predator to be useful to one. That turns the open question from atmosphere into mechanism. The mystery is doing work in the plot, and the work it does is the reason it has not been closed: an unresolved status is exactly what this class of thing feeds on. I did not plan that when the question was first left open. I found it when I asked what the entity was actually eating.

Two older rules of this record are what make the shape stable, and it is worth saying how they meet here. The first is Sen Lovax's doctrine in [Marked Absences](/star-rangers/codex/marked-absences/): where the record's knowledge stops, mark it and invent no cause, because an explained gap invites the record to defend a guess forever. Daire's folk account is the worked example of staying on the right side of that line. He explains the *epistemology*, why the reading is real and where the sensitivity comes from, and invents no *fact* about what she is. The second is the record's own published list of [what it refuses](/star-rangers/lore/what-the-record-refuses/). Because the refusals are public, the reader already knows that certain familiar answers are unavailable, and so an impression the prose allows them to form is known to be their own. The record never has to confirm or deny it page by page. It said once, in its own voice, that the referent is not there.

There is one discipline the whole thing depends on, and it is the general rule I most want to keep. **A checkable claim stays enigmatic only while nobody checks it.** Most of the evidence above is safe because it is not checkable at all: a folk word, a filing, a cat's reading. But one piece is a claim about a body, and a claim about a body can be put to an instrument. In the chapter it survives as enigma for exactly one reason, that it is one person's uncorroborated recollection, kept out of the file by the person who holds it. The moment any such claim is confirmed or refuted the shape collapses, in either direction, because a mystery resolved by measurement is a reveal, and the reader is back behind the door with one more fact. So the craft rule is not *never answer*. It is: know which of your pieces are checkable, and understand that for those, the enigma is a decision you are making every chapter not to check.

I will add the obvious caution. This shape is for shadow-shaped mysteries, the kind this record is built on, where a thing is real and seen through a gap that cannot be crossed. It is the wrong shape for a crime, where the reader is owed a culprit and the pieces are meant to add up. The test for whether a question belongs in the first category is whether, if you handed every character's piece to one person, they would then *know*. If they would, you have a detective story with the detective removed, and the reader will feel the absence. If they would not, because the pieces are evidence of a question rather than fragments of an answer, then you have the shape I am describing, and you can let everyone hold one.
