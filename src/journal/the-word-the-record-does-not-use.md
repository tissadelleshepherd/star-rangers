description: "On keeping the word religion out of the Archive's own voice, leaving the Fellowship of Light without a comparison page, and letting one general's line about democracy stand as the only named politics in the record — three refusals that are one discipline."

A reader working through the Lore today would find two comparison pages and not a third. There is a page that sets the record's physics beside the physics of our own century, with the places they part named and bounded. There is a page that sets the record's civilisations beside the ones history has tried. There is no page setting the Fellowship of Light beside any faith a reader might belong to, and today I wrote down why, because the absence had been a decision for some time without being a recorded one.

The reason is grounding. A comparison is a claim with two ends, and it is only as sound as the less certain end. Physics can be compared because both ends are measured: the record's deviations from the standard model are licensed, specified and bounded, and the standard model is the standard model. A civilisation can be compared because a polity is a thing that acts, and acts can be set beside other acts. A faith is neither. Its content, on either end, is open, and what a reader holds of their own tradition is not something a page can know. A comparison page for the Fellowship would therefore be a verdict dressed as a table. It would tell the reader which real tradition they were reading the Fellowship as, and the whole point of a devotional order in a hard-science record is that nobody is told that.

That is the first refusal. The second is older, and the sweep I ran today showed me it had been in force longer than I knew. The record's own voice, meaning the Lore, the Glossary and the narration that is nobody's testimony, does not use the word *religion*. It uses *devotional tradition*, a term of art the Archive coined to flatten on purpose: established faiths, minority traditions and small cults in one phrase, without ranking them or marking which is which. The Archive is proud of the neutrality, and the page that defines the term records that every tradition so described has at some point called it a refusal to do the difficult part. Both readings are on file. That sentence is the discipline in miniature. The record will name a practice, a house, an order, an observance, a vigil, a well. It will not name the category, because the category is where a reader's own ledger lives, and the record has no standing to open it.

The count, for anyone who wants to check the claim rather than take it: eleven pages carried the word or its adjective before today. Seven of them are Saint Aoife's thirteenth-century Life and its echoes, where *a religious house* is the period word for a monastery, said by a hagiographer in her own voice, and the Archive does not correct an author's vocabulary any more than it corrects her dates. Those stand. One is a page about the site, speaking to real readers in their own terms, and that stands too. The rest were the Archive slipping into the word where its own term would do, and those are changed. The Fellowship is now *not a faith, though the nearest thing the record holds to one*. Quern's two journeys are not described *as devotional*, and the record observes that the settlers do not describe them as anything else. None of the changes alters a fact. They move a word from the Archive's mouth back into the mouths of the people who would use it.

The third refusal is about politics, and it has one exception, which is why it is worth stating exactly. The record names no real ideology. No page says capitalism or socialism or nationalism, and a polity's politics are shown through what it does, which is the only way a reader can judge them without being told what to think. The exception is a general's sentence at a hearing in 2719: *We tried democracy. The result was forty years of the Coherence Wars and three billion dead. I would rather be called a tyrant than repeat the experiment.* It is the most quoted line in the founding record, and it is quoted on six pages. It stands because it is hers. The rule binds the record's voice, not the people in it, and a character who has earned a sentence like that by eleven weeks of testimony is entitled to say it. What the record does not do is agree with her, or argue with her. It files the line, and the charter that was written against it, and lets the reader hold both.

Put the three together and they are one thing. The record describes what people do and keeps its own hands off the words that would tell a reader what to make of it. A faith is shown as a practice. A politics is shown as an act. A general's thesis is shown as a general's thesis. The reader arrives with whatever they arrived with, and nothing on the page sorts them.

The cost is real and it is a writing cost. *Religious* is a short word and *of devotion* is not. A sentence that cannot say what category a thing belongs to has to say what the thing does, and that is always longer and usually harder. I think it is also always better, which is the kind of claim a journal can make and a Lore page cannot. The Tideward Sisterhood was *a religious order* this morning and is *a devotional order* tonight, and the second phrase makes me want to know what they do, which the first did not.

There is a sibling rule the record already runs on, and the two illuminate each other. The reader may assume nothing about what is real, even where the surroundings look familiar. A faith named by a real-world word is the most familiar surrounding there is, and the quickest way to let a reader stop looking. Taking the word away is not coyness. It is the same refusal to reassure that governs the corridors and the clocks, applied to the one category where a reader is most likely to think they already know.
