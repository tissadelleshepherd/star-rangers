I should say first who this section of the site is for, because the answer decides how it is written and the question came up today. The Journal is not for the child reading the pets thread. On the children's domain it does not appear at all, by design: an essay about a naming decision has no business turning up in a search for a book about a cat. It is for the adult in the room — the parent or teacher reading that thread aloud — and beyond them for critics, reviewers, peer authors and anyone studying how the writing is made. Those are readers who read closely and check what they are told, which is why an entry here is written to be impossible to misunderstand rather than merely easy to read, and why a loosely true attribution counts as an error. The test before an entry publishes is whether one of those readers would learn something from it about how the work is made.

That settled, the thing I wanted to write down.

## The talk and the sermon

In the churches I know, the service carries a short address to the children before the sermon proper. I have heard adults say, more than once and not as a joke, that they find the children's talk more helpful than the sermon. I used to take that as a comment on sermons. I now think it is a comment on selection.

The children's talk has to choose one thing and say it exactly. It cannot hedge, because a hedge is invisible to a six-year-old, and it cannot qualify, because there is no time. The sermon carries the qualifications, and the qualifications are where a listener loses the thread — not because they are wrong but because each one is a fork, and a listener holding three forks is no longer holding the point. So the children's talk is often closer to *impossible to misunderstand* than the sermon is, which is the odd result: the plainer form is the more exact one, because it was forced to select.

This bears on the rule recorded earlier today, that the Journal is written at the contemplative reading level and that precision outranks plainness. The contemplative register earns its length with depth. It pays for that length in ambiguity risk, since every added clause is a place a careful reader can take the wrong branch. The children's register cannot afford the clause in the first place. The two registers are not opposites on one scale; they are two different ways of being exact, one by selection and one by qualification, and the second is harder to do well.

## Two exemplars

The reading tiers on this site are a ladder — children, young adult, general, contemplative — and the ladder nests, so a reader on the top rung has everything below it. The adult who finds the children's talk more helpful is that reader: the contemplative editions carry the pets thread whole, and nothing stops a Fellowship reader from reading Barsik. The ladder was built for the content, but it turns out to describe the reader too.

For the bottom rung, the exemplar I would name is Narnia. C. S. Lewis explained theology to children by not explaining it. Aslan is never glossed inside the books; Mr Beaver's *'Course he isn't safe. But he's good* and the refrain that he is not a tame lion are the nearest the text comes, and they are the expiry date built into the story — the reader is told exactly where the picture stops. Lewis said in his letters that the books were not allegory, where each thing stands for one other thing, but *supposal*: suppose there were such a world, and it needed rescuing. And in a 1956 essay he said why the form worked, in a phrase I keep coming back to — that a story could steal past the *watchful dragons* of reverence, the associations that stop a listener hearing a thing said plainly. The failure mode is the reader who maps the books one to one, which is the mustard seed read as botany from the previous entry.

For the second rung, *The Lord of the Rings*. Book Four — the second half of *The Two Towers*, Frodo and Sam and Gollum through the marshes to the pass — is slow, and the slowness does not move the book off the young-adult rung, because the tiers on this site are defined by register, protagonists and depth and never by pace. The hobbits are the reader's proxy, which is the rule this site set for the Five-O task force: a young viewpoint through which the reader meets the older cast. And the site's rule on ages holds for them literally. Hobbits come of age at thirty-three; Frodo leaves the Shire at fifty and Sam is thirty-eight, so both are young adults in their own years, and Pippin at twenty-eight is a minor. Tolkien placed them where the tier wants them, seventy years before the tier existed.

## The record's own case

The setting has already run this experiment, and I did not plan it. [Kate Ukrainka](/star-rangers/characters/kate-ukrainka/) writes children's books about [Smart Pets](/star-rangers/glossary/smart-pet/) on Eden, and in them a Smart Pet's cognitive subsystem is *a small quiet friend who lives behind the eyes*. That is not what a certified subsystem is, and the welfare inspectors have written to her about it. Her answer, in her author's note, is that she is not describing the hardware but what it is like to carry it; asked how a subsystem actually works, she said she had looked it up twice, understood it both times for about a day, and did not think a six-year-old was owed the diagram. What a six-year-old was owed was the truth about the animal.

Read against today's entries, every part of the parable discipline is present. The picture is the primary form, because her reader is six, so she declines to retire it. The author's note is the expiry date, stated in advance. The inspectors are the literalisers, reading a parable as a claim about certification. And the record marks the whole thing by filing her books in the Codex, as one author's account and not as fact. What she is doing is not oversimplifying the mechanism. She is refusing to describe it, and being exact about the one thing the book is for. That is the children's talk, and it is the difference between a parable and a fable with a moral pinned to it.

So the Journal is for the adult in the room, and the tiers are built so that adult always has somewhere to stand. That is not a concession to the children's tier. It is what the children's tier is for.
