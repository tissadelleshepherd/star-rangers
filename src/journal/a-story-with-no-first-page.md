Today I changed a line at the foot of every page on this site. It used to call the work a novel, and now it calls it a story. The change is small and the reason is not. A novel has a first page and a last page, and it may have sequels or belong to a trilogy. This work has none of those. It grows, with no end planned, and it is open to a reader arriving from any direction. It does have arcs, plots and subplots, as many as it needs, but the plots are mostly driven by the characters themselves rather than laid down in advance for them to walk.

That raised a question about what to call myself. I am the author, and that is the only title I want. But the work does have a method, and a method deserves a name so that it can be discussed. I call it *story architecture*: the set of structural commitments that let a story without covers keep its shape. It describes how the work is made and is never a byline, and this entry is my attempt to say what it consists of.

## What the covers used to do

In a bound book the covers do quiet structural work. The first page makes a promise and the last page keeps it, and everything between is held in tension by the distance from one to the other. Take the covers away and that tension has to come from somewhere else. If it comes from nowhere, the result is not an open story but a heap of chapters. So the question the method answers is a narrow one: when nothing is first and nothing is last, what holds?

## Three words for three levels

The first answer is vocabulary, kept strict. The record uses three words that a looser account would let blur together, and it keeps them apart because each names a different structure.

A *storyline thread* gathers whole seasons into an independent storyline, one a reader can follow from start to finish without the others. A *strand* is smaller: one of two or more storylines running in parallel inside a single season, with separate viewpoint casts and no shared scenes until a *convergence point*, the place where they meet. An *arc* is neither. It is the shape of one character's change over time, across whatever seasons carry it, and it is not a reading path at all; nobody can sit down and read an arc.

The distinction matters because it puts planning and freedom in different places. A convergence point is fixed before either strand is drafted, and that is nearly the only thing fixed in advance. Between the fixed points the characters do the walking, and the plot is what their choices make of the ground. A season number, for its part, marks a slot in the record, not a release order. A new storyline takes a new number and a thread of its own. It never forks an existing one, because the record holds one history seen by many witnesses, and a branch would be a second history.

## Load-bearing walls

The second answer is a rule about change. Everything published in the seasons is canon, which means it is true in the world and binds everything written afterward. A story that grows without end has to be able to add to itself without knocking down what already stands. So a published canon page may be edited in exactly three ways: by *extension*, which adds without altering what stood; by *clarification*, which says better what was already true; or by resolving a *genuine dilemma*, meaning commitments that cannot all stand together. Anything else is new material, or it is nothing. On the day that rule was made, a direction that would have reopened a settled page was dropped, because nothing forced the change.

Disagreement does not need a wall knocked through either. The [Codex](/star-rangers/codex/) holds documents written by named sources inside the world, each valid for its author and binding on nothing, so a contradiction is filed under somebody's name rather than smoothed out of the Lore. And the [reading tiers](/star-rangers/tiers/) nest: each carries everything below it and adds its own, and nothing read on a lower tier is withdrawn or contradicted on a higher one. A story with no last page grows outward and upward. It does not grow by revising what a reader has already been told.

## Endings without an end

An open-ended story still has endings; it has many, and none of them is the end. Every arc and every season is held to one test: the ending must change how the beginning reads. A *reveal* that leaves the reader where they were is a flat ending, however much it explains. A *recognition* re-lights what came before, even when it explains nothing. The test applies to an arc, a season or a mystery. It never applies to the work as a whole, which has no last page to be tested against. The shape of the whole is the sum of shapes that each close properly.

## A narrator that learns

The Archive is the record's own body of reference, its Lore, Glossary and Codex, and it is a narrator with limits. It holds only what the world's own institutions could know, which is why it can be read before a chapter or after one without spoiling either. When a chapter teaches the record something, the change is logged rather than staged. An Archive page can name the chapter whose prose first establishes its subject, in a field called `revealed_by`, and the chapters after which the page was revised because the record learned more, in `revised_by`. Revelation becomes revision: the entry is revised, not rewritten, and says so. No page is hidden until it has been read, and in time the prose will reveal all of the Lore.

## No fixed first page

The plainest consequence of having no first page is mechanical. Chapters are sometimes renumbered in place, so that the order of the record keeps faith with the order of events, and a chapter's position-based identity changes when its slot does. Its discussion thread and its permanent address under `/c/` follow a second identity, the `comment_id`, which is set once and never regenerated. It moves with the writing, not with the position. A reader's comment and a scholar's citation both stay attached to what was written, wherever it now sits.

The same thought runs through the cast. The record ranks no character, as [No One Is Minor](/star-rangers/journal/no-one-is-minor/) sets out, so a reader can come in by any door and find the same floor under them. In a story with no first page, every door is a first page.

That is what I mean by story architecture. It is not a plan for where the story goes; the characters carry that, and the unpublished notes keep the rest. It is the frame that lets them go anywhere without the house coming down. How the site itself is written is set out on the [About page](/star-rangers/about/#how-this-site-is-written). The story is still growing.
