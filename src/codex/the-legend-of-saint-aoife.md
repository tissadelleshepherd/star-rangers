**FOR THE READER-SCHOOLS**
**Told again, as it was told to me**

---

You will hear this story told badly, and you should know how it goes when it is told well, because Aoife herself took trouble over the difference. So here it is, the way the ships told it, before there was an Aethelrock to tell it on.

### The Girl at the Well

She came to the thorn well at Cill Aoife when she was fourteen, in a year with a date — about 1200 AD by the old count, 1400 UCSD by ours — in a townland on an island on Earth, which is the world the ships left. Nobody chose her for it. The well had a name for healing and the sick came to it, and somebody had to sit with them, and the girl who sat with them best was the one who did not fuss.

The older women did not like her much. They said she was too still. They were right that she was still, and wrong about what it meant: she was the kind of still that listens, and a sick person can tell the difference from across a room. She took no husband, though there were offers. She took no dowry, though there was one. She sat with the sick at the well for sixteen years before anything happened to her that anyone would put in a book, and if nothing ever had, she would still have been the best nurse in that townland, and this is the part the bad tellings leave out.

### The Night the Air Folded

On the eve of Saint Muirenn's feast, in her thirtieth year, Aoife kept vigil at the well alone, which was her habit. Muirenn was the well's own saint, carved above the shrine, and on her eve you sat up.

What happened next she never told all of, not even to Brother Daire, who wrote down the little she allowed. This is what she allowed.

The air over the water folded, the way cloth folds. It did not tear where it folded. Something was in the clearing that was too big for the clearing, and the clearing held it anyway. It did not walk toward her. It was already at every distance at once — as near as her hand and as far as the hills, both, and not one after the other. It spoke, and the voice was not in her ears. It named things in her that she had not named herself. Then the folding went backwards, and the clearing was a clearing, and the well ran warm.

It ran warm for three days. It had never run warm before, in all the years she had tended it, and it never ran warm again.

### What She Said, and What She Would Not

Here is where the tellings go wrong, so read slowly.

Aoife said it was Muirenn. Who else comes to a well on her own eve? That is what she said, and the Union has said it after her for fourteen hundred years, and we say it at her feast.

And in the same breath she said that the shape looked nothing like Muirenn. Nothing like the carving, nothing like any picture of the good Sister anyone in that townland had ever made. She said both. She did not pick one. Brother Daire wrote down both, because she told him to, and because he was honest too.

The sick she treated with the warm water that week got better — more of them, and faster, than anyone could remember. People wanted to say she had been healed by what she saw, or that she had been given healing to hand out. She would not have it. She said she had been *shown* something. She said she had not been *healed*. Those are two different things, and she would not let anyone push them together, however much easier it would have made her to explain. Brother Daire admitted, in his own book, that he did not understand the difference at the time.

You may not either. Keep it anyway. Nearly everyone who tells this story wants to tidy it, and the whole point of Aoife is that she did not.

### The Rest of Her Life

Three times she was asked to enter a religious house, where a woman who had seen what she had seen could be kept and honoured and explained. Three times she said no, and went back to the well and the sick. She died in the winter of 1241 AD — 1441 UCSD — at the well, working. Nobody in authority ever made her a saint. The townland just went on calling her one until the word stuck, and it kept her feast on the day of the vision and not the day of her death, and nobody wrote down why, because nobody needed to.

### How She Came to Us

When the Currach Fleet left Earth, it carried Aoife the way you carry your grandmother's story: not in a box, but in the telling. Her feast was kept on the ships. Water was blessed in her name by people who had never seen a well. Children who would never see Ireland were told about the thorn well and the night the air folded, and told it right, with both halves. That is why she is not a saint out of a book to us. She is somebody's grandmother's saint, and the grandmother was on the ship.

### What the Grown-Ups Argue About

Since the Union found the Solar System again, there are scholars who say what Aoife met was not Muirenn at all but one of the beings the Rangers call Levrils, and that the folding and the every-distance-at-once and the warm well are what those meetings look like when someone has no word for them. The Rangers keep her account in their own archive, beside things they have measured a very long way from Ireland. There are chaplains at the Ridgemoot who say the scholars and the saint were never answering the same question, and that both can stand.

I am not going to settle it for you. Aoife would not have. She told what fitted and what did not, in the same breath, and let it stand. If you are ever shown something you cannot name, that is the whole of what she has to teach you: say what you saw, say what did not fit, and do not let anyone — not even yourself — make it tidier than it was.
