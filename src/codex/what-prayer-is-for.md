**SURVEY ARCHIVE — FIRST-CONTACT TRANSLATION SERIES**
**Document:** Instruction to novices of the Tideward Sisterhood, set down at the Abbess's direction
**Occasion:** A delegation linguist, working through the offices of the day, asked what they were *for*. The Abbess answered that the question deserved a better answer than an afternoon's conversation, and had this prepared.
**Standing:** Conveyed in translation, reviewed against the source by the Abbess's own correctors. Filed as received.

---

You have asked what prayer is for, and I will tell you first what it is not for, because the error is older than the Abbey and you will meet it dressed as piety.

Prayer is not asking that works. If you have come to the terraces believing that the right words, said rightly, at the right hour, will bring what you ask, then you have not described prayer; you have described bargaining, and you have imagined a counterparty. The Sisterhood keeps no such doctrine. Hear it plainly, because I will not soften it for you and you should distrust any house that does: **no sister of this order has ever been taught that prayer is answered.** The Long Accounting keeps a column for answered prayers. It is ruled, and it is empty, and it has been kept ruled and kept empty through every hand that has held the book. Novices sometimes take the empty column for a failure of the record. It is the opposite. It is the record succeeding, where a less honest house would have filled it.

Now I will tell you what prayer is for, and I will use the only teacher this order has ever had.

**Go and stand tideward.** The tide does not come because you called it. It has never once come because you called it, and it has never once failed to come. Nothing you do hastens it by a finger's width; everything the Abbeylands are — the terraces, the render, the hours of the offices, the shape of every working day you will live here — is set by it. It does not answer. It does not fail. Attend to that pairing until it stops seeming like a contradiction, because the whole of the practice is inside it.

Prayer is how we stand toward what is vast, regular, and does not reply. It has four works, and none of them is asking.

**The first work is attention.** The offices return at fixed hours the way the tide returns, and for the same reason: so that attending stops being a mood and becomes a discipline. A sister who keeps the hours is not reminding anything of her existence. She is practicing the kind of seeing that does not grasp — looking at what is so, at the hour it is so, without requiring it to be otherwise before she will look. You will find this is the rarest skill on the islands, and that the court, the harbour, and your own heart all lack it.

**The second work is speaking true.** In the offices we say things aloud that we would otherwise arrange more comfortably. Understand the direction of this. We do not speak to inform — a novice who thinks the sea is short of news has not stood near it. We speak because the speaker was hiding, and stops. What is said tideward must be said whole, in the words that are so rather than the words that flatter, and the discipline of saying it whole is the entire fruit. Prayer does not tell anything a secret. It ends one.

**The third work is letting grief go out.** What is mourned on the terraces is mourned aloud, at the hour, in company, and then the hour ends. This is not coldness; it is the tide-table applied to sorrow. Grief that is never spoken circles the house forever, and I have watched it thin a strong sister like a long illness. Grief that is spoken whole, at its hour, goes out with the water. It returns — grief keeps its own tides — and it goes out again, and each time it goes out a little further. The sisters of this house grow old lighter than the harbour does. I make no claim about why. I keep the observation in the Accounting, where observations belong.

**The fourth work is being set.** You do not move the tide; the tide moves you — your hours, your work, your bread. To pray is to consent to be the thing arranged rather than the arranger, one hour in each day, so that the consent is in you somewhere when the day comes that you need it. Sisters die well in this house. The Crown has remarked on it. That is not an answer to prayer. That is what a life shaped by its hours looks like when it ends, and if you cannot yet tell the difference between those two sentences, keep the offices until you can.

You will ask — the honest novices always ask — *does it work?* Attend to your question. The tide comes. Nothing you do brings it. Everything you are here will be set by it. Now choose what you meant by *work*, and when you have chosen, you will find the question has been answered for years.

Asking is not forbidden here. A sister may ask for anything, aloud, at any office — the way she may weep, which is to say: not because it avails, but because it is true, and we do not teach anyone to lie tideward. But she asks knowing the column is empty, and that the emptiness is kept honestly, and that nothing about the practice was ever waiting on it.

The sea has the rest of your instruction. Go and stand where you can hear it.
