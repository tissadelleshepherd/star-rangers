Nobody on Deck 4 could tell you when the stair had started coming up short. That was the first thing Detective Oyinlola wrote down, and she underlined it, because in her experience the thing nobody could date was the thing that had been going on longest.

Fourteen steps. Everyone agreed on fourteen. The maintenance drawing said fourteen and the drawing was right; she had counted them herself, going down, with her hand on the rail, and got fourteen. Going up she got thirteen. She did it four times. Thirteen, and her foot arriving on the landing a step before her body expected to, the small jolt of a stair that is not there, except that it was there, she had just walked up it, and the landing had come anyway.

"Everyone does that," said the man who ran the laundry at the top. "You get used to it."

"Used to what?"

He thought about it. "Being early," he said.

---
---

She did what a detective does with a thing like that, which is to find out who benefits, and nobody did. Nothing had been taken. Nobody had fallen. The laundry's takings were ordinary. The stair had no insurance and the deck had no enemies. She wrote up what she had, which was a stair that everyone on the deck had quietly agreed to be slightly early on, and sent it to her Inspector with a note that said *not a case*, and the Inspector sent it up with a note that said *not ours*, and the note came back down three weeks later with a fourth hand on it, and the fourth hand said *invitation issued*.

The Commander arrived on a Thirdday with a case the size of a lunchbox and no visible interest in anyone's opinion. She was shorter than Oyinlola had expected and older, and she said *good morning* to the laundry man as if he were the one who had sent for her, which, Oyinlola realised later, was how she said good morning to everyone.

She did not go up the stair. She stood at the bottom and looked at it for a long time, and then she opened the lunchbox and took out something that looked like a spirit level with opinions, and laid it on the third step, and the fifth, and the ninth, and the landing, and wrote a number down each time in a notebook with a pencil, and the numbers did not seem to surprise her.

"Well?" said Oyinlola.

"Fourteen steps," said the Commander.

"I know it's fourteen steps."

"Yes." The Commander closed the notebook. "Going down."

---
---

What she did next took most of the afternoon and did not look like anything. She sat on the bottom step and asked the laundry man how long he had run the laundry, and his mother before him, and whether the stair had always been the stair or whether there had been a lift once, there had, and when it had come out, he did not know, before his mother. She asked Oyinlola how many people used the stair in a day, and Oyinlola did not know, and the Commander said that was all right, nobody ever did. She asked three people coming down whether they counted, and they all said no, and she asked one woman coming up whether she counted, and the woman said *thirteen*, without stopping, the way you would say your own name.

Then she wrote for a while. Oyinlola, who had spent nine years being told things by people who did not want to tell her, found that she could not read the Commander's face at all, and that it was not because the Commander was hiding anything. It was because there was nothing yet to hide. The woman was simply not finished.

"What is it?" Oyinlola said at last. "The stair."

"I don't know."

"You've been here six hours."

"Yes." The Commander looked up. "It's a stair with fourteen steps that people climb in thirteen. That part I can measure, and I have, and the measurement will go to people who have a hundred stairs like it and may, in time, have something to say. I can tell you what it is not. It isn't a fault in the stair. It isn't anyone doing anything. It isn't dangerous, so far as a measurement of six hours can say, and I'll say so on the page, with the six hours beside it." She tapped the notebook. "What it *is*, I haven't established, and I'm not going to guess, because a guess written down here is a thing this deck will have to live with long after I've gone, and you've all been living with the stair quite well."

"That's it?"

"That's what I'm for." She stood up. "You were right to send it up, by the way. Most people don't."

---
---

Oyinlola walked her to the dock. At the ramp the Commander stopped, and turned, and looked back along the concourse towards Deck 4 as if she could see the stair from there, and perhaps, Oyinlola thought, she was simply remembering where it was.

"Do you count?" Oyinlola said. "When you go up a stair. Now."

The Commander considered it. "I used to count going up," she said. "Now I count going down." And she went up the ramp, and Oyinlola, watching her go, found that she had counted the steps on it, and that there were nine, and that she had got nine, and that she did not know why that should feel like a thing she had been given.

She went back to Deck 4 and climbed the stair. Thirteen. She stood on the landing a step early, in the laundry's warm doorway, and the man looked up and said, "Well?"

"She doesn't know," said Oyinlola.

He nodded, as if that were the answer he had been expecting, and perhaps the one he had wanted, and went back to the folding. And Oyinlola, who had come to the deck three weeks ago to find out who benefited, stood in the doorway and did not go in, and did not go down, and understood, in the way you understand a word you have been using wrongly for years, that everyone on the deck had been climbing that stair for longer than anyone could say, and arriving early, and getting used to it, and that she had never once asked what it was they were early *for*.

*Cultural note, Survey Archive general collection annotation: this copy of* The Short Stair *came to the Archive from the shared shelf of the Dock Seven monitoring station, which is where the* Called In *stories are mostly found: passed hand to hand on survey postings, in kiosk printings that go out of print and come back. It carries pencil corrections in three hands, all of them to the procedure and none to the stair. The Archive records them here because they are the Corps' own reading of the book, and because they are right. No Ranger holds the rank of Commander, or any naval rank; a Ranger enters a self-governing habitat by invitation and the invitation in this story comes down the right chain by accident rather than design, the fourth hand on the note being, in the Compact's actual arrangements, [Orbital Five-O](/star-rangers/lore/orbital-habitats-compact/)'s and not an Inspector's superior's; and no Ranger carries a sidearm. Pinya's Rangers always do and have never, in twenty-six stories, drawn one. One of the three pencil hands has written beside the lunchbox:* she wouldn't have the gun. She'd have a second instrument.

*What the corrections leave alone is the whole of the story's method, and the Archive notes the omission as a judgement. The Ranger measures what can be measured, states what the thing is not, writes down what has not been established as not established with the hours beside it, and declines to guess on the grounds that a guess written down outlives the one who wrote it. That is [a doctrine this Archive holds](/star-rangers/codex/marked-absences/) and she has never, on the evidence of her author's notes, read it; the second pencil hand has written* she got there anyway *against the Commander's last speech on the stair. The stair itself is Pinya's and is not on any record the Archive holds. Readers on Threshold have assumed with some confidence that it is a stair they know. The Archive has not asked, and files the assumption as an assumption.*
