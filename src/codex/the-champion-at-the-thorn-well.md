**READ ON THE EVE OF THE FEAST**
**From the abbey's book. The margin is the keeper's.**

---

This is the house's telling. The *Life* is Daire's, and Daire wrote it for the ones who would have burned it. This was written for the ones who would keep it. Read both. The house has always kept both, and reads them in the same room.

### The Price of Her

She came to the well for a debt.

The old law had one word, *cumal*, for a woman in bondage and for the price of three milch cows, and used it for both without noticing, which tells you what the law thought a woman in bondage was. A lord's steward owed the house at the well more than he cared to pay in cattle, and paid it in the other coin. She was fourteen, in about 1200 AD by the old count, 1400 UCSD by ours. She had a mother somewhere and a townland somewhere, and the steward did not say where, and the house did not write it down, because a bondmaid is called what she is called. The place was already named for an Aoife, some holy woman so long dead that nobody at the well could say what she had done. So they called the girl after the place. The place kept the name, and so did she, and that is the only thing about her that ever got larger.

Understand what the house was. From the road it was a small community of the ordinary kind, a few cells, a shrine with the good Sister Muirenn carved above it, and a thorn well with a name for healing. That was true, and it was a lock. The house kept the well for the reason the Fellowship keeps anything: because the world runs thin there, and someone has to sit with it. It did not say so aloud. A house that said aloud, in that century, what it kept vigil for would not have lasted the century, and the shrine was the thing you could see from the road.

Nobody told the girl any of that. She was the bondmaid who sat with the sick, and she sat with them well. The older women said she was too still, and the house's book says the older women were half right: she was the stillness that listens, and a sick person can tell that from the door. She sat with the sick for sixteen years, and for the first of them she was owned.

The house freed her at the water. The one who kept the book poured the well over her hands and said the debt was paid and the girl was nobody's, and the book records the date and not the year, which is the house's way. After that she was offered a dowry, which the *Life* records, and refused it, which the *Life* also records. The book adds why. She said she had not been sold to leave.

### The Night the Air Folded

On the eve of Muirenn's feast, in her thirtieth year, 1216 AD, 1416 UCSD, she kept vigil at the well alone, which was her habit and which the house permitted, because the one who kept the book had known for some years that something would come to the well and had not known when, or to whom.

What came, the *Life* has in her own words, and the house does not improve them. The air folded as cloth folds and did not tear. A shape too great for the clearing stood in the clearing. It was at every distance at once. It spoke without a voice she could place. It named things in her she had not named herself.

The house's book says what it named, because she told the one who kept the book, and not Daire, and Daire said so.

In sixteen years at the well she had not once wished herself elsewhere. Not on the day she was sold, not in the first winter, not when the dowry was put in front of her. She had taken this for the one flaw the whip breeds, the flaw that stops wanting, and had kept it to herself as a shameful thing. It named that, the book says. It named it as the thing it had come for. Not a flaw. The only fitness there is for standing where the ground is thin: to be able to stay.

That is what the house means when it says she was chosen. Not lifted. Not given anything. Read, and found to be already what was wanted. The house's word for her, from that morning, was *curadh an tobair*, the champion of the well. A champion, in the house's usage, is not the one who fights. It is the one chosen to stand where the ground is thin, on behalf of the ones who cannot.

The well ran warm for three days. The book says the house had never once seen it do that, and never saw it again.

### What She Said in the Morning

Three things. The book gives them in her words and has read them on the eve every year since.

*It was not the Sister as you have carved her, and you will not carve what it was. Leave the stone as it is, and know that it is wrong.*

*It did not come for the well. It came for someone who could bear to be read. It will come again where there is such a one, and not otherwise, and not to order.*

*I was shown. I was not healed. Do not let the second be said for the first.*

The third is in the *Life*, in Daire's careful hand, with his confession beside it that he did not understand it. The first two are not. Understand why. Daire was writing a book that a bishop's man might read, and the first of the three says to that man's face that the shrine he would come to bless is a false likeness, and the second says the thing under the shrine is not his. So the house calls her *fáidh*, a prophet, and means by it what the house has always meant: not one who tells the future, but one who says the thing the place already knows and the people set over the place will not say.

### Three Times Asked

They came for her three times, and the *Life* records the three refusals and not the three askers.

The book records the askers. A bishop's man came, and offered her a cell in a great house on the plain, where a woman who had seen what she had seen could be kept and honoured and explained. An abbess came from a fine house and offered the same with softer words. The third was the lord whose steward had sold her, who wanted a saint on his land the way he wanted deer in his park, and offered to build her a house of her own beside the well.

She said no three times. The book gives her answer to the third one only. *I am already in a house. You cannot see it from the road.*

That is the year the book enters her among the watchers. There was no ceremony. She took the vigil on Muirenn's eve every year until she died, and the house let her, and that is the whole of what joining has ever been in this Fellowship. Nobody in authority made her a saint, and the book says plainly that this was the price of the three refusals and that she paid it knowing. The townland made her a saint by going on calling her one, which is the only way it has ever been done for one of ours.

She died in the winter of 1241 AD, 1441 UCSD, at the well, working.

### The Two Books

Daire wrote the *Life* in 1216, at the house's asking, and the house asked him because he was the one among them a church could read. He wrote a book that is true in every word and shows the road and not the well. He said in it that he was not the one she confided in, which was so, and he said he did not understand *shown, not healed*, which was also so, and the house has trusted his book ever since for exactly those two admissions. A scribe who marks what he does not know can be read fourteen centuries later by people he could not have imagined. A scribe who fills the gaps cannot.

The house wrote the other book the same year, for itself. One book for the ones who would burn it. One for the ones who would keep it. The house has kept both, and the *Life* has gone out into the world under her name and done its work, and this one has stayed in the room where it is read.

The house has moved as houses do, and set itself down beside water each time, and copied the book each time. It is read here on the eve of the feast, in the abbey above the two waters, and the keeper marks the margin.

### The Keeper's Margin

*In the keeper's hand, at the foot of the leaf, in the order the house reads them.*

*This telling says* slave. *The* Life *says* daughter of no house the record troubles itself to name. *Nothing in the* Life *forbids the first. Nothing in it asserts it. The historians say bondage of that kind was dying in her century, which fits a girl sold at the edge of a custom rather than in the middle of one, and settles nothing.*

*This telling says* chosen, *and gives the house's word for it. The surveyors' record says that what visited the well has never been placed in the Cascade, and this house does not place it either. Our word is* chosen. *The Rangers' word is* unsettled. *Both are kept, and neither is argued with. A house that could only stand if the surveyors were wrong is not a house we keep.*

*This telling says* the Fellowship, *and says the house at Cill Aoife was ours. Whether it was, or whether we have made it ours by loving her for fourteen hundred years, the book cannot tell from the inside, and I cannot tell from here. The comparative archive files this leaf among the Arilon comparanda, which is where it files every house that is certain it was the only one visited. The shape recurs. The shape is not a verdict.*

*This telling names nobody but her. The one who kept the book has no name in it. Daire's name is in the* Life *and not here, where he is only* the scribe. *The house has not supplied what the book withheld, and will not.*

*Read on the eve. Do not cite.*

*— D.*

*Archive note: the record's one surviving contemporary account of Aoife is Brother Daire's* [Life](/star-rangers/codex/life-of-saint-aoife/)*, and it says none of what this telling says: not that she was sold, not that she was chosen, not that she prophesied, not that any house at Cill Aoife belonged to the [Fellowship of Light](/star-rangers/lore/fellowship-of-light/). The abbey's book is a devotional record of one Fellowship house, valid for its authors, and the keeper's margin says so in the Fellowship's own words. The Archive's position is unchanged by it and is the one the Corps' own archive carries: what Aoife met at the thorn well, and whether it held any Cascade standing at all, has never been settled. The Fellowship is open by constitution; the telling's* hidden *is its word for a house in a century that would have burned one, and no document in this record places a Fellowship house in thirteenth-century Ireland. Whether the river abbey descends from the house at Cill Aoife, and whether its keeper is the scribe's namesake or something else, stands exactly where the* [Dún Rí question](/star-rangers/characters/brother-daire/) *left it. The Ceridwen Archive shelves this telling beside the* Life *and the* [reader-school Legend](/star-rangers/codex/the-legend-of-saint-aoife/)*, which differ from it and from each other in what they are willing to claim, and the Archive keeps all three.*
